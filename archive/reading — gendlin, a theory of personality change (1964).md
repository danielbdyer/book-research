# reading — gendlin, a theory of personality change (1964)

**What was read.** Eugene T. Gendlin, "A Theory of Personality Change," chapter four in Worchel & Byrne (eds.), *Personality Change*, New York: John Wiley & Sons, 1964. Read complete — thirty-eight pages, about 22,900 words — from the PDF the Focusing Institute publishes in its Gendlin Online Library (focusing.org/sites/default/files/upload/2023-08/personality_change.pdf, retrieved 2026-08-05). This is the paper the Institute's own library index describes as containing the original observation and definition of focusing.

**How it is cited.** By the paper's own numbered definitions and footnotes, which are stable across editions: "Gendlin 1964, def. 11" for the eleventh numbered definition (referent movement), "Gendlin 1964, fn. 9" for the ninth footnote. Quotations were taken verbatim from the PDF's extracted text; the extraction breaks words with stray spaces ("refe rent," "para digm"), and those spacing artifacts are repaired in quotation. Nothing else is altered.

**Why this paper first.** The reading queue's first item is an adversary for the verification standard, and the chair at [[eugene gendlin]] queued three questions that had to be answered from the standard's own author before any adversary could be read against him: whether Gendlin himself makes the verification claim the book makes on his behalf, what he says about checking, and where the philosophical weight sits.

## What the reading settled

1. **The verification question — settled, and the answer is narrower than the book's use.** The paper defines the correctness of a symbolization as its experienced effect on the process, and explicitly denies that correctness means the symbols match the felt referent: "'correctness' does not imply that a given set of symbols means what the felt referent alone means. Rather, 'correctness' refers to the experienced effect which certain symbols produce" (fn. 9). The claim is [[the felt shift certifies carrying forward rather than correspondence]].

2. **The checking question — settled, in a footnote the vault had no record of.** Footnote 13 is a taxonomy of four kinds of inward attention that are not focusing: sheer emotions, the circumstantial orbit, the explanatory orbit, and self-engineering. This is the standard's own author distinguishing the real process from its counterfeits, fourteen years before *Focusing*. The claim is [[gendlin names four kinds of inward attention that are not focusing]].

3. **The friction property — confirmed at a primary text.** "He may find that his previous formulation which felt correct must be replaced by another which now feels more correct," and "Not even the individual himself judges it but, we might say somewhat poetically, his direct referent does the judging" (def. 8 and fn. 11 discussion). The vault's note at [[the felt sense rejects formulations and that friction is what separates it from fluency]] had this property only at second hand from a 2004 paper; the 1964 text states it directly.

4. **The solitary question — settled against the solitary reader, in one sentence.** "It is often possible, though always unsteady and difficult, for the individual to focus by himself" (fn. 13, on sheer emotions). The surrounding section says the difference is dramatic: alone, a person circles for hours "feeling the same series of emotions and lacking any referent movement"; with an understanding listener, even saying a little produces movement.

5. **The vocabulary — dated.** The 1964 term for the felt shift is "referent movement" (def. 11); "felt sense" appears here as "direct referent" and "felt meaning." The familiar vocabulary is later. A citation of the felt shift to this paper should say so.

## What the reading did not settle

- **The empirical history.** This is a theory paper. Its own research citation is Gendlin and Berlin 1961, autonomic correlates of inward focusing, and the paper calls that work "tentative" in its own words (fn. 10). The outcome research the corpus's story rests on — what predicted change in the recorded therapy studies — is in Gendlin, Beebe, Cassens, Klein & Oberlander 1968 and the later reviews, none read here. The 1969 paper in *Psychotherapy: Theory, Research and Practice* is not freely available and was not read.
- **The life-sentence.** "What is split off, not felt, remains the same" is quoted by the corpus from *Focusing* (1978), which was not read. The 1964 paper carries the claim's machinery in other words — see the bullet added to [[what is split off not felt remains the same]] — but the sentence itself remains unverified against its book.
- ***A Process Model.*** The chair names it as where the philosophical weight sits. Unread.

## The one disagreement the reading opened

The corpus seats McLaren beside Gendlin as the alphabet's affective wing, her teaching being that every emotion is a messenger carrying a question. Gendlin's footnote 13 says emotions are "sheer," internally all one quality, and that they "often keep him from sensing that in himself which is the complex ground of the emotion" — his instruction is to move past the emotion to the felt sense beneath it. One co-patron says open the emotion's mail; the other says the emotion is not the mail. This is recorded on the chair and not resolved.
