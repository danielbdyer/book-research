# reading — laozi, daodejing 11 and 28 (legge trans.)

**What was read.** The *Dao De Jing* (*Tao Te Ching*), chapters 11 and 28, attributed to Laozi, in James Legge's public-domain English translation (1891) as published at the Chinese Text Project (ctext.org/dao-de-jing), retrieved 2026-08-10. This addresses reading-queue tier 1b, entry 33.

**How it is cited.** By chapter number, which holds across editions: `Daodejing ch. 11`, `Daodejing ch. 28`. Quotations are Legge's and are marked below; read in translation, not the classical Chinese, and the key terms — the "empty space" of ch. 11 and *pu* 樸 (the uncarved or unwrought block) of ch. 28 — should be confirmed against a modern scholarly translation before manuscript use, since Legge's Victorian phrasing ("profitable adaptation," "unwrought material") is dated.

**Why this reading.** [[the daodejing reaches the open center through the emptiness a structure holds]] claimed the Daodejing is the cleanest independent arrival at the book's open center and gives the privative grammar its affirmative face, but stood privated on the loci alone. The failable question: does ch. 11 actually locate usefulness in the emptiness a structure encloses, and does ch. 28 carry the refusal of systematization the note attributes to the uncarved block?

## What the reading settled

1. **The open center through emptiness — confirmed, and exact.** Ch. 11: "The thirty spokes unite in the one nave; but it is on the empty space (for the axle), that the use of the wheel depends. Clay is fashioned into vessels; but it is on their empty hollowness, that their use depends." The chapter closes: "what has a (positive) existence serves for profitable adaptation, and what has not that for (actual) usefulness." Usefulness lives in the emptiness a structure encloses, reached *through* the structure (the spokes, the clay walls) — which is the book's open center, made possible by the three structures and identical with none of them, arrived at from a tradition with no contact with *Lichtung* or *prakāśa*. It is also the affirmative face of the privative grammar: absence is given a positive job.

2. **The refusal — the uncarved block — confirmed, with one interpretive step named.** Ch. 28: "The unwrought material, when divided and distributed, forms vessels." Legge's chapter valorizes the return to the uncarved state; the text's own move is to prefer the undivided whole and to note that dividing it produces implements (vessels). The note's gloss — that Laozi would read the book's twelve primitives as the cutting that turns a whole into instruments — is a faithful extension of "when divided… forms vessels," not a phrase the text uses, and is marked as the reader's inference.

**Verdict for the note.** The Daodejing is a genuine and clean independent arrival at the open center, confirmed at ch. 11. The refusal holds at ch. 28 as the valorization of the uncarved whole against its division into implements, with the application to the book's apparatus marked as inference. The note moves from privated to full.

## What the reading did not settle

- **The classical Chinese, and a second translation.** Legge (1891) is dated; the "empty space" of ch. 11 and *pu* of ch. 28 should be checked in a modern scholarly translation (D. C. Lau, or the Mawangdui/Guodian-informed editions) before manuscript use.
- **The dissolved self.** The note's second refusal — that Daoism dissolves the striving self rather than restoring it, an adversary to the book's restored first person — was not read here; it wants the *wu wei* chapters, already a separate queue concern.
- **Whether ch. 11's emptiness is ontological or only pragmatic.** Legge reads it as usefulness; whether the Daoist "empty" is the book's open center as a site of disclosure, or only a functional void, is a comparison a fuller reading would test.
