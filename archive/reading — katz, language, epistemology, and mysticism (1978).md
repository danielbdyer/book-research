# reading — katz, language, epistemology, and mysticism (1978)

**What was read.** Steven T. Katz, "Language, Epistemology, and Mysticism," the opening essay of *Mysticism and Philosophical Analysis*, ed. Steven T. Katz, Oxford University Press, 1978, pages 22 to 74. The essay's epistemological argument was read at its own pages: the opening statement of what the essay does and does not claim (pp. 22–23), the statement of the mediation thesis and the criticism of Stace that follows it (pp. 26–29), the criticism of Zaehner and the list of ten things a Jewish mystic is conditioned to believe (pp. 32–33), the logical argument against comparing experiences that are all called ineffable (pp. 54–55), the reconditioning passage (pp. 56–57), the attack on the notion of a "given" (pp. 58–59), the passage on the knower's activity in mystical experience (p. 60), the turn to the object side (pp. 64–65), and the closing section IV (pp. 65–66). The comparative body — the sustained comparison of Jewish *devekuth*, Buddhist *nirvāṇa*, Christian absorptive and non-absorptive union, and Sufi *fanā'* at roughly pages 34 to 53 — was sampled rather than read continuously, and pages 24–25, 30–31, 62–63 and the endnotes at 66–74 were not reached at all. The reading is therefore complete over the argument and partial over the evidence Katz assembles for it, and every finding below comes from a page named above.

**How it was read, and the limits that puts on the quotations.** The essay was read through a scan of the printed pages published at yumpu.com (document 15134537, retrieved 2026-08-08), which reproduces the Oxford edition with its printed page numbers intact. The scan's character recognition is imperfect and mangles diacritics — *nirvāṇa* prints as "nirviiQa," "Neither" prints as "Nether" — and those errors are silently repaired in the quotations below; nothing else is altered. Two better routes were tried and failed: the copy in the Internet Archive's lending collection does not open its text to search without a borrower's login, and the copy Trinity College once published at `www2.trincoll.edu/~kiener/` has been taken down, its archived snapshot sitting behind a host this session's network policy refuses. The consequence for later work is that any single sentence quoted from this essay in the manuscript should be checked against a paper copy before it prints, and the pages not reached remain a queued remainder rather than a closed question.

**How it is cited.** By the printed page numbers of the 1978 Oxford edition, which are stable across its reprints: "Katz 1978, p. 60." The essay's four sections are numbered I through IV in the text itself, and a citation may name the section where the page alone would be uninformative.

**Why this reading.** The reading queue's first tier carried Katz with a question the vault wrote when it moved him off the convergence and onto the verification standard: his hypothesis is that a tradition's concepts shape the experiences its practitioners have, and the question was whether that hypothesis reaches a *discrimination* — a reader telling change from no-change — as strongly as it reaches a *description*. The stake was stated in both directions. If it reaches the discrimination, the felt shift confirms the book's training rather than anything beyond it, which is the deepest available form of the objection that fluent prose can produce bodily assent. If it does not, the objection lands on the book's prose and not on its instrument.

## What the reading settled

1. **The question's answer is no, and Katz says so himself on his second page.** He states the limit of his own argument before he makes it: "it is not being argued either that mystical experiences do not happen, or that what they claim may not be true, only that there can be no grounds for deciding this question, i.e. of showing that they are true even if they are, in fact, true" (p. 22). What the essay denies is evidential force — "no veridical propositions can be generated on the basis of mystical experience," and mystical experience "is not and logically cannot be the grounds for any final assertions about the nature or truth of any religious or philosophical position" (p. 22). It does not deny that anything occurred, and it offers no argument that a person is unable to tell that something in them moved. The claim carrying this is [[katz's objection reaches what a felt shift proves and not whether anything moved]].

2. **Katz does name discrimination among the mediated activities, in one sentence, and the sentence works against passivity rather than against reliability.** Arguing that the mystic is not a recording device, he writes: "The creative role of the self in his experience is not analogous to the passive role of the tape-recorder or camera. Even in mystical experience, there seems to be epistemological activity of the sort we know as discrimination and integration and, in certain cases at least, of further mental activities such as relating the present experience to past and future experience, as well as traditional theological claims and metaphysics" (p. 60). This is the closest the essay comes to the vault's question, and it comes at it from the far side: Katz's point is that discriminating is something the mystic *does*, which is why the experience cannot be a pure reception. He nowhere argues that the discriminating is mistaken. The same claim carries this finding, because the two halves are one answer.

3. **The costly finding is on the practice pole rather than on the standard, and the vault's question did not ask for it.** Katz holds that contemplative training does not clear the practitioner's frame but replaces it: "Properly understood, yoga, for example, is not an unconditioning or deconditioning of consciousness, but rather it is a reconditioning of consciousness, i.e. a substituting of one form of conditioned and/or contextual consciousness for another, albeit a new, unusual, and perhaps altogether more interesting form of conditioned-contextual consciousness" (p. 57), and "There is no substantive evidence to suggest that there is any pure consciousness per se achieved by these various, common mystical practices, e.g. fasting, yoga, and the like" (p. 57). He generalizes it at p. 59: "the mystic even in his state of reconditioned consciousness is also a shaper of his experience; that he is not a tabula rasa on which the 'ultimate' or the 'given' simply impinges itself." The claim is [[a practice reconditions the frame rather than clearing it]].

4. **The one sentence that reaches an anticipated change, and Katz does not develop it.** Listing what conditions a mystic's experience, he includes the practices themselves: "Related to these expectations are also future directed activities such as meditation, fasting, ritual ablutions, self-mortification, and so on, which create further expectations about what the future and future states of consciousness will be like. There is obviously a self-fulfilling prophetic aspect to this sort of activity" (p. 59). This is the essay's nearest approach to attacking a reader's report that something changed, and it arrives as one clause in service of a different conclusion; Katz draws nothing from it about whether the anticipated state occurs. The claim built on it, with the book's own exposure stated rather than borrowed from Katz, is [[a practice that describes its destination manufactures the expectation it later confirms]].

5. **The essay's own refusal, kept whole.** Katz declines to rank traditions and declines the position he is often read as holding: "we do not hold one mystical tradition to be superior or 'normative' as, for example, did Stace and Zaehner," and "Our sole concern has been to try and see, recognizing the contextuality of our own understanding, what the mystical evidence will allow" (pp. 65–66). His positive proposal is that a plural account "is able to accommodate all the evidence which is accounted for by non-pluralistic accounts without being reductionistic" (p. 66). A book that cites him as the opponent of its convergence owes him this: he is arguing for reading traditions in their own terms, not for the impossibility of inner report.

## What the reading did not settle

- **The comparative evidence.** Pages 34 to 53 carry the case that Jewish, Buddhist, Christian and Sufi mystics have different experiences rather than one experience differently described, and that case was sampled rather than read. Nothing in the findings above turns on it, because the findings are about the scope of Katz's epistemology; a later reading that wants to test the founding isomorph — Heidegger's *aletheia* beside Pratyabhijñā recognition — against Katz's method needs those pages, since that is where the method is actually run.
- **The endnotes.** Pages 66 to 74 hold the essay's citations, including whatever Katz says about the psychological literature. Unread.
- **Katz's later position.** The essay is the first of four volumes he edited on the subject, and he restates the mediation thesis across them as his "working hypothesis." Whether he ever narrows or hardens it is unexamined here, and the tier-six entry that reads Luhrmann after him is unaffected by this reading either way.

## The one thing this reading changes about the company's shape

The vault carried Katz as "the deepest available version of the fluency objection, because it survives plain prose." His own text does not support that ranking. The experimental attack the graph already holds is the sharper instrument at exactly the point the book's standard stands: Nisbett and Wilson exempt reports that a sensation is present now and aim at reports of causes, and Schwitzgebel builds his case on bodily and emotional experience by name and rejects the present-tense exemption. Katz, by contrast, disclaims the accuracy question in his opening pages and never returns to it. The recorded consequence is not that he leaves the company — his reconditioning finding costs the book more than the objection he was recruited for — but that the objection he was recruited to carry is carried better by two other names, and his chair should say so.
