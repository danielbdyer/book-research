# reading — winnicott, the use of an object and relating through identifications (1969)

**What was read.** D. W. Winnicott, "The Use of an Object and Relating through Identifications," read complete — about 5,900 words, including the summary, the five numbered notes, and both appendices. The text used is the one in *The Collected Works of D. W. Winnicott*, volume 8, chapter 28, Oxford University Press 2016, at pages 355 to 364, published as a PDF by the University of Pennsylvania (web.english.upenn.edu/~cavitch/pdf-library/Winnicott_Use_of_an_Object.pdf, retrieved 2026-08-08). The paper first appeared in the *International Journal of Psycho-Analysis* 50 (1969), pages 711 to 716, and was reprinted with minor revisions as chapter 6 of *Playing and Reality* (Tavistock 1971), pages 86 to 94, which is the address the reading queue gave.

**How it is cited.** By the Collected Works page numbers, which the PDF prints in the margin: "Winnicott 1969, p. 360." Where a citation would be more useful to a reader holding *Playing and Reality*, the chapter is chapter 6 of that book and the correspondence is close, because the reprint made only minor revisions. Quotations are verbatim from the extracted text; the extraction drops the "fi" ligature throughout, printing "identi cations" for "identifications" and " nd" for "find", and those are repaired silently in quotation. Nothing else is altered.

**Why this reading, and what it was asked to settle.** The reading queue put Winnicott first among the design-gating readings because two of the vault's proposed treatments for the resistance tension cite his object-usage account as their warrant, and neither cited it from the paper. The question was whether the destruction has to originate with the subject. If it does, a refusal that the book writes for itself fails the warrant, and a surface the reader can write on is the only compliant form. The stake is that the whole treatment set for the resistance tension either survives or collapses to one design.

## What the reading settled

1. **The destruction originates with the subject, and Winnicott makes the point twice in the same paragraph so that it cannot be read the other way.** The transition from relating to usage is "the subject's placing of the object outside the area of the subject's omnipotent control; that is, the subject's perception of the object as an external phenomenon" (p. 358). He then reverses the sentence to forestall exactly the misreading a staged refusal would need: "It is important to note that it is not only that the subject destroys the object because the object is placed outside the area of omnipotent control. It is equally significant to state this the other way round and to say that it is the destruction of the object that places the object outside the area of the subject's omnipotent control" (p. 359). The destruction is what does the work, and it is the subject's.

2. **An object that supplies the attack has protected itself, and Winnicott names the protected object's outcome in a sentence that reads as a description of this book's own failure mode.** "Without the experience of maximum destructiveness (object not protected) the subject never places the analyst outside and therefore can never do more than experience a kind of self-analysis, using the analyst as a projection of a part of the self… The patient may even enjoy the analytic experience but will not fundamentally change" (p. 360). The book's defined reader is someone whose enormous experience did not hold, and the second reader type uses fluent integration vocabulary to avoid feeling anything; a reader who enjoys a well-made book and does not change is that reader exactly.

3. **Answering the attack is the specific failure, and Winnicott's word for it is parrying.** "The analyst feels like interpreting, but this can spoil the process, and for the patient can seem like a kind of self-defence, the analyst parrying the patient's attack. Better to wait till after the phase is over" (p. 360). A staged refusal that the text answers in the next paragraph is the parry, performed by the same hand that wrote the attack.

4. **Survival is defined negatively and the definition is strict.** "It is important that 'survive', in this context, means 'not retaliate'" (p. 360), and the danger is not the object's absence but its change of manner: "even the actual death of the analyst is not as bad as the development in the analyst of a change of attitude towards retaliation" (pp. 360–361). Elsewhere he puts the whole weight of the word on the object's fallibility: "The word 'destruction' is needed, not because of the baby's impulse to destroy, but because of the object's liability not to survive, which also means to suffer change in quality, in attitude" (p. 361).

5. **The object has to be able to receive the communication, and this is where a book's position is genuinely different rather than merely weaker.** Winnicott's sequence is a dialogue: "The subject says to the object: 'I destroyed you', and the object is there to receive the communication. From now on the subject says: 'Hullo object!' 'I destroyed you'. 'I love you'. 'You have value for me because of your survival of my destruction of you'" (p. 359). A codex satisfies the survival condition by construction, because it cannot retaliate and cannot change its attitude, and it fails the receiving condition absolutely. The claim built on this split is [[a book survives destruction by construction and cannot receive the reader's communication]].

6. **Two sentences bear on claims the vault already holds and were not known to come from here.** On who has the answers: "The principle is that it is the patient and only the patient who has the answers. We may or may not enable him or her to encompass what is known or become aware of it with acceptance" (p. 356) — the sovereign-reader maximum, stated by the thinker the resistance tension already cites, from inside a clinical practice. And Winnicott's own reading list for the paper, sent to the New York Psychoanalytic Society, names "The Capacity to be Alone" among the four papers he asks his audience to have read (Appendix I), which puts the paper that anchors the book's reading posture inside the bibliography of the paper that anchors its resistance treatment.

## What the reading did not settle

- **Whether a reader-writable surface actually produces the sequence.** Winnicott's account is of a live analyst who might genuinely fail. Nothing in the paper says what happens when the object cannot fail, and he never considers a book, a text, or any object that is not a person. The compliant design named below is an inference from his conditions rather than a finding of his.
- **The clinical illustrations.** Winnicott prepared two of them for the 1968 meeting, catalogued separately in the Collected Works, and neither was read.
- **His own second thoughts.** "Comments on My Paper 'The Use of an Object'" and "The Use of an Object in the Context of *Moses and Monotheism*" were both written in response to this paper's reception, and neither was read. If the staged-refusal question is ever reopened, those are where he answers his critics.

## What the reading changes in the vault

The resistance tension carried two treatments, and this reading disqualifies both in their author-written form: the Refusal Pass stages a refusal the book writes, and the Doubting Hand is a margin voice the book writes. Earlier the same day this session recorded that the Kashmir Śaiva refusals could supply the Doubting Hand its script, and that pairing inherits the same problem — a refusal derived from the book's own foundation and printed by the book is still the object supplying the attack. The pairing is not withdrawn, because a printed refusal does other work that the vault should not confuse with this one: it models that the book can lose, and it keeps a real opponent inside the text. What it cannot do is the thing the Winnicott warrant was cited for. The claim carrying this is [[a refusal the book writes for itself fails the warrant the design cites]].
