# reading — the discrimination family (ignatius §§313–336, engler 1984, kapleau's three pillars)

**What was read.** Three guards, read at primary on 2026-09-02 as reading A9 of the practice-model program. **Ignatius of Loyola**, *Spiritual Exercises*, the two sets of rules for the discernment of spirits (§§313–336) complete, in Elder Mullan's 1914 translation (CCEL, unnumbered) and Louis Puhl's 1951 translation (numbered), with Annotations §§1–2, 6–10, 14–15, 17, 19–20, 22 — Grade **A**, paragraph numbers verified against Puhl's own markers. **Jack Engler**, "Therapeutic Aims in Psychotherapy and Meditation: Developmental Stages in the Representation of Self," *Journal of Transpersonal Psychology* 16:1 (1984), 25–61, complete at the journal's own archive — Grade **A**, PDF page mapping to journal page verified against the running heads. **Philip Kapleau**, *The Three Pillars of Zen*, Anchor revised edition (1965/1989; Afterword 2000), complete, cross-checked against the Matheson Trust reprint of Yasutani's introductory lectures — Grade **A**; the Anchor text is reflowed, so the makyō lecture is cited by lecture number and title (pp. 42–44 in the 1967 Beacon printing). **Not reached:** Engler's 2003 revision, "Being Somebody and Being Nobody," in Safran, ed., *Psychoanalysis and Buddhism* (Wisdom, 2003), pp. 35–80 (lending-restricted); his 2000 *What Is Enlightenment?* interview, which states the same softening, is Grade **B** via a secondary quoting it with a page locator.

**How it is cited.** `Spiritual Exercises §333 (Puhl)`; `Engler 1984, p. 39`; `Kapleau, Three Pillars, Yasutani's Introductory Lectures, Lecture 3` and `Kapleau, Three Pillars, Part One §III (The Encounters)`.

**Why this reading.** The book "walks readers toward deliberate destabilization with no way to tell dissolution from regression" ([[ken wilber]]), and every guard the practice-model survey found — Wilber's pre/trans, Gendlin's four counterfeits, Ignatius's rules, Zen's *makyō*, the Sufi state/station — was either from memory or constitutively second-person. The question that could fail, already queued as the Engler entry: does any of these supply a criterion a non-clinician could even in principle apply, or are they all second-person by construction? Fails if all are second-person, which converts the safety hole from a reading gap into a design constraint on a book read alone.

## What the reading settled — Ignatius

1. **The rules certify by course and aftermath, and §331 denies that consolation as such certifies anything.** "If a cause precedes, both the good angel and the evil spirit can give consolation to a soul, but for a quite different purpose" (§331). §333 certifies by beginning, middle, and end — "If the beginning and middle and end of the course of thoughts are wholly good and directed to what is entirely right, it is a sign that they are from the good angel"; §334 by retrospective review, "by the trail of evil marking his course"; §336 by distinguishing the consolation from its afterglow — "must cautiously distinguish the actual time of the consolation from the period which follows it… resolutions and plans which are not granted directly by God our Lord… must be carefully examined before they are given full approval." §318 supplies the rule of no change: "In time of desolation we should never make any change, but remain firm and constant in the resolution and decision which guided us the day before the desolation." The vault's queued question — whether the rules certify by course and aftermath rather than by felt quality in the moment — does not fail.

2. **The one felt-quality rule inverts by direction of travel.** §335, the sponge and the stone: "In souls that are progressing to greater perfection, the action of the good angel is delicate, gentle, delightful. It may be compared to a drop of water penetrating a sponge. The action of the evil spirit upon such souls is violent, noisy, and disturbing… In souls that are going from bad to worse, the action of the spirits mentioned above is just the reverse." To read it one must already know whether one is progressing or deteriorating, which is the fact a reader in the book's position lacks. (The brief had named §335 as the trail rule; the trail is §334.)

3. **First-person instruments under second-person gatekeeping, with one rule inside the set that cannot be executed alone.** The rules are written in the exercitant's voice — "We must carefully observe the whole course of our thoughts" (§333) — and handed over by the director, who decides whether and when: §8, "If the one who is giving the Exercises should perceive… that the exercitant has need of them, he should explain to him the rules"; §9 withholds the Second Week set from a beginner because it "will be harmful, since they deal with matter that is too subtle and advanced for him to understand"; §10 releases it when temptation comes "under the appearance of good"; §17 routes the exercitant's movements to the director as input. And §326 makes disclosure the operative move: the enemy "earnestly desires that they be received secretly and kept secret. But if one manifests them to a confessor, or to some other spiritual person who understands his deceits and malicious designs, the evil one is very much vexed." Ignatius nowhere authorises solitary use.

4. **One translation correction.** The vault's Annotation 2 quotation — "it is not much knowledge but the inner feeling and relish of things that fills and satisfies the soul" — is Michael Ivens's rendering, not Puhl's ("it is not much knowledge that fills and satisfies the soul, but the intimate understanding and relish of the truth") or Mullan's ("it is not knowing much, but realising and relishing things interiorly").

## What the reading settled — Engler

1. **The famous line, twice with pages.** "Put very simply, you have to be somebody before you can be nobody" (p. 31); "But you have to be somebody before you can be nobody. The issue in personal development as I have come to understand it is not self or no-self, but self *and* no-self" (p. 51). The developmental claim: "in order to practice an 'uncovering' technique like Vipassana and to achieve the structural changes the practice aims at, it is developmentally necessary to acquire a cohesive and integrated self first" (p. 53); the contraindication, "Insight meditation, i.e., Vipassana, does not address this range of psychopathology, is not designed for it, and is probably contraindicated" (p. 54).

2. **Third-person, explicitly, and the confusion is the one the practitioner makes from inside.** The criterion is a structural assessment of level of personality organization — "healthy, neurotic, borderline or psychotic" (p. 37) — which "does not depend on personality or character type, and still less on symptoms or symptom clusters." "Students may mistake subjective feelings of emptiness for 'śūnyatā' or voidness; and the experience of not feeling inwardly cohesive or integrated for anattā or selflessness" (p. 39). The teacher cannot see it either: the tradition's "ways of thinking about personality in terms of types would not allow teachers or students to recognize it" (p. 38). And self-observation is doubted as the instrument: "It is questionable whether self-observation in this sense is even possible. What is required is confrontation and interpretive exposure of the split-off object relations units as they occur within a transference relationship" (p. 43). One mitigation, his own: the practice's demands may self-select — "a self-selective and self-protective mechanism is probably built in" (p. 42) — which is a property of a retreat practice and not obviously of a book.

3. **The softening withdraws the linear ordering, not the clinical basis.** Grade B, from the 2000 interview: "I wouldn't do that in the same way today because now I think our spiritual life and our psychological life are much more interwoven. I think the statement still has value in the way I originally meant it, but I would take it out of this tight psychological model of human development." Nothing found suggests Engler ever proposed a first-person test.

## What the reading settled — Kapleau and Yasutani

1. **Makyō verbatim and located.** "MAKYO ARE THE phenomena—visions, hallucinations, fantasies, revelations, illusory sensations—which one practicing zazen is apt to experience at a particular stage in his sitting… These phenomena are not inherently bad. They become a serious obstacle to practice only if one is ignorant of their true nature and is ensnared by them"; "Never be tempted into thinking that these phenomena are real or that the visions themselves have any meaning. To have a beautiful vision of a Buddha does not mean that you are any nearer becoming one yourself… But such visions are certainly a sign that you are at a crucial point in your sitting… Whenever makyo appear, simply ignore them and continue sitting wholeheartedly" (Yasutani's Introductory Lectures on Zen Training, Lecture 3, "Illusory Visions and Sensations"). The dokusan replies are from Part One, Section III, "Yasutani-roshi's Private Encounters with Ten Westerners": "These things have no particular significance—they are neither beneficial nor harmful. Don't embrace them, just go on earnestly with your practice"; "Makyos do not occur when you are dawdling, neither do they appear when your practice has ripened." Edition variance: the Anchor text reads "a Buddha," an earlier printing "Buddha," and earlier transcriptions "Bodhisattva"; the vault's quotation elides across a paragraph break.

2. **The instruction is first-person because it is not a discrimination; the certification is not self-authenticating, in Yasutani's own words.** "Whether you have no teacher in America or only a mediocre one, you can continue to discipline yourself in Zen by following what you have learned at this temple… But the truth is, you can never be sure by yourself that what you take to be kensho actually is kensho… STUDENT: But isn't enlightenment self-authenticating? ROSHI: No, it isn't" (Part One §III, Student G), with the diamond analogy — one who has never seen a real diamond "may think when one finds glass or quartz… that one has found a genuine diamond."

3. **Kapleau faced the book's design problem and answered it in print.** From his editor's introduction to the dokusan section: "No one, nothing, can take the place of an enlightened master… Is, then, the practice of Zen without a teacher impossible? By no means… A thorough reading of this section… will enable anyone to commence this journey on the road to enlightenment," compiled for "students in the West and elsewhere who wished to discipline themselves in Zen yet lacked competent teachers." His substitute for the absent teacher is a transcript of the second person's actual replies, and his verb is bounded: the book lets anyone *commence*. Two solo-practice footnotes confirm where the line falls: a self-applicable progression criterion for breath-counting (fn. 40) and "It is not wise to assign yourself a koan. Only a teacher who has given you dokusan… can, with your help, assign you a suitable koan" (fn. 43).

## The comparison

Ranked by how much of each guard survives the solitary condition: **Gendlin** (first-person by construction — "From this lack of referent movement, one knows that nothing has really changed," 1964, def. 11, with his own "always unsteady and difficult," fn. 13); **Zen's makyō rule** (fully applicable alone because it discriminates nothing); **Ignatius §§318, 333, 334, 336** (applicable alone in principle, needing memory over time and honesty about the aftermath; §335 unusable alone); **Engler** (not applicable alone, and the teacher cannot supply it either). What each requires that a text cannot supply: an understanding listener; a teacher who has seen a real diamond; a director who gates and receives, and a confessor for the decisive disclosure; a clinician's structural assessment. Three of the four define the second person by having been there, not by license. They converge on one rule a book could state, each clause carried by at least two guards at primary: continue and do not stop the practice; do not act on it; check it against time; check it against another person. The first three are statable in a book and applicable alone; the fourth is where every guard locates the discrimination proper.

**The verdict.** The question does not fail on its own terms — three of four supply a criterion a solitary reader could apply — and it fails in the way that matters: every criterion that survives the solitary condition works by declining to evaluate, and every act of actual discrimination in all three traditions is second- or third-person without exception. The safety hole converts into a sharper constraint than the one anticipated: a book read alone can state the whole of the restraint and none of the discrimination ([[a solitary reader can run every guard that declines to evaluate and none that discriminates]]).
