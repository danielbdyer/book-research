# reading — weil, gravity and grace on decreation (1947)

**What was read.** Simone Weil's concept of *decreation* (*décréation*), from *Gravity and Grace* (*La Pesanteur et la grâce*, French first edition 1947; the Emma Crawford English translation, 1963), the "Decreation" and "Renunciation" material, cross-read against the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry on Weil for its scholarly framing and its locators into *Waiting for God*. The quotations from the "Decreation" section were confirmed at two independent sources (a full reproduction of the section, and a corroborating search), and the *Gravity and Grace* volume was not read whole in-container. This addresses reading-queue tier 1b, entry 45.

**How it is cited.** `Weil, Gravity and Grace, "Decreation"` and `Weil, Gravity and Grace, "Renunciation"`, by section, in the Crawford translation; the one *Waiting for God* line is cited `Weil, Waiting for God` (SEP gives it as WFG 100). Quotations below are the Crawford translation's and are marked; the French and a scholarly critical edition were not read.

**Why this reading.** [[weil's decreation un-creates the self and reaches the opposite of the restored first person]] holds that Weil's decreation is the un-mined mechanism of her seated opposition to the book's householder commitment — the same make-room-by-self-withdrawal move as *tzimtzum*, *kenōsis*, and the *fanā* pole, reaching the opposite conclusion — and that the withdrawal family therefore splits along the book's householder-versus-renunciation fork. The failable question: does *Gravity and Grace* define decreation as un-creating the self to make room for God, in imitation of God's own self-renunciation, and does it reach the far pole from the book's restored first person?

## What the reading settled

1. **Decreation is un-creating the self, and it is not destruction — confirmed.** Weil's definition: "Decreation: to make something created pass into the uncreated." She marks it off sharply from destruction: "Destruction: to make something created pass into nothingness. A blameworthy substitute for decreation." So decreation is not annihilation of the self into nothing; it is the passage of the created "I" back into the uncreated, the obstacle un-made rather than smashed. This is the precise, quotable concept the seat needed.

2. **The move is imitation of God's own self-renunciation in creation — confirmed.** From "Renunciation": "Imitation of God's renunciation in creation. In a sense God renounces being everything. We should renounce being something. That is our only good." The withdrawal is grounded in a prior divine withdrawal — God renouncing being everything so that a world can be — which is the same theological shape (the maker contracts to make room) that *tzimtzum* names and that the container law borrows structurally. Weil states the make-room-by-withdrawal move at the level of the self imitating that cosmic contraction.

3. **The direction is the opposite of the book's — confirmed, and this is the finding.** For Weil the "I" is the obstacle to be un-created and the room is made *for God*: to be decreated is to "empty ourselves of our false divinity, to deny ourselves, to give up being the center of the world" (WFG 100, per SEP). The book's fullness contracts and then opens back into fullness, the first person restored on the last page ([[the fullness of the first person is the road to selflessness rather than its obstacle]]). So decreation and the book run the same withdrawal in opposite directions, which confirms the fork the whole withdrawal family divides on and keeps Weil an adversary rather than company.

4. **The earlier caution is lifted.** The formulation flagged in the note as unconfirmable — "We participate in the creation of the world by decreating ourselves" — was confirmed at the text at two independent sources, appearing in the same "Decreation" material and followed by "Thus we are co-creators." It may now be used as a quotation, with its translation provenance (Crawford, 1963) stated. It sharpens the finding rather than softening it: the decreated self becomes a co-creator with God, not a restored householder in a room of ordinary life.

**Verdict for the note.** Decreation is confirmed as the sharpest statement of the far side of the withdrawal fork: the self un-created permanently, the room made for God, in imitation of God's self-renunciation. The note moves from privated to full. The seated adversary ([[weil is seated as the opponent the plumbing deserves]]) now carries a precise, quotable mechanism, and the withdrawal family's split along the book's householder-versus-renunciation fork is confirmed at the text.

## What the reading did not settle

- **The French, and the whole volume.** Read via a reproduction of the "Decreation" section in the Crawford translation and the SEP's framing, not *La Pesanteur et la grâce* whole or in French; the aphoristic order of Thibon's arrangement and the surrounding "Self-effacement" and "Void and compensation" sections await a fuller reading before manuscript use.
- **Whether Weil's God-directed room and the book's life-directed room are the same act aimed differently, or two different acts.** Both contract to make room; the book's room opens for a life and Weil's for God. Whether that is one move with two destinations, or two moves that only rhyme, is the comparison a fuller reading — and the tzimtzum reading still queued — would test.
