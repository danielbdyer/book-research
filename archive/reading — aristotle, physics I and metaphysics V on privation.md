# reading — aristotle, physics I and metaphysics V on privation

**What was read.** Aristotle, *Physics* Book I, chapters 7–9 (the account of the three principles of coming-to-be — form, matter, privation), and *Metaphysics* Book V (Delta), chapter 22 (the philosophical lexicon's entry on privation, *steresis*). Read in the public-domain English translations published at the Internet Classics Archive (classics.mit.edu): the *Physics* in the Hardie and Gaye translation (1930), the *Metaphysics* in the Ross translation, both retrieved 2026-08-09. This addresses reading-queue tier 2, entry 5.

**How it is cited.** By Aristotle's stable book-and-chapter locators, which hold across every edition: `Aristotle, Physics I.7–9` and `Aristotle, Metaphysics V.22`. The Bekker line-numbers (Physics I.7–9 ≈ 189b30–192b4; Metaphysics Δ.22 ≈ 1022b22–1023a7) are given from general knowledge and should be confirmed against a printed critical edition. Quotations were taken from the fetched translations and are marked below; they were not read in the Greek, and *steresis* should be checked against a scholarly edition before any manuscript use.

**Why this reading.** [[the privation move is an independent arrival across five fields rather than a book coinage]] names Aristotle as the oldest of the five arrivals and rates the convergence centerpiece-grade, but the claim stood on the corpus and general knowledge, unread. The queue's failable question: does Aristotle support the *therapeutic* direction of the book's move — naming the absence points to the remedy — or only the *analytic* one, that change is intelligible because privation is a principle of it? The stake: the book's signature move (`Burnout = Obligation − Sanctuary`; "the minus sign tells you what to restore") either has a twenty-four-century warrant for what it *does*, or only for what it *sees*.

## What the reading settled

1. **In the *Physics*, privation is analytic and nothing more — settled.** Privation is one of the three principles of coming-to-be, but it enters *per accidens*: "the privation, on the other hand, and the contrary are incidental in the process" (I.7). It does not persist through the change — "what is not an opposite survives (for 'man' survives), but 'not-musical' or 'unmusical' does not survive" (I.7) — and it has the weakest ontological standing of the three: "the privation in its own nature is not-being… the matter is nearly, in a sense is, substance, while the privation in no sense is" (I.9). Here privation only makes change *intelligible* (a thing comes to be out of what it lacks); there is no language of restoration or remedy. On the *Physics* alone, the answer to the queue's question is "only what it sees."

2. **In *Metaphysics* V.22, privation is directional — the stronger and more surprising half.** Every sense of *steresis* Aristotle catalogs is defined relative to **what the thing naturally has**: "if something has not one of the attributes which a thing might naturally have"; "if, though it would naturally have the attribute, and when it would naturally have it, it has it not"; and "the violent taking away of anything is called privation" (all V.22). So Aristotelian privation is not a bare logical absence but a *deficiency relative to a thing's nature* — it references the natural state the thing lacks. This is most of the structure the book's move depends on: a lack is a lack *of something proper to the thing* (burnout lacks sanctuary, and sanctuary is what belongs), so naming the absence already names what is missing-and-natural.

3. **The gap the book crosses on its own — settled, and it is the finding.** Neither text takes the last step from "a lack of what is natural" to "therefore restore it," as a therapeutic prescription. Aristotle supplies the ontology of the directional lack; the book supplies the clinical imperative ("the minus sign tells you *what to restore*"). So the twenty-four-century warrant covers the *structure* of the move — privation as a directed lack of the natural — and not its *therapeutic use* as such. The book's move is a faithful extension of Aristotle, not a restatement of him.

**Verdict for the convergence.** Aristotle is a genuine independent arrival, and a stronger one than "worth one sentence of lineage" allowed: *Metaphysics* V.22 grounds the directionality that the clean "analytic only" reading of the *Physics* would have missed. But he is a warrant for privation-as-directional-lack, not for privation-as-prescription. The convergence holds and is bounded: the readings of the other four arrivals decide whether "directional lack" is the shared structure or whether some of them reach the prescriptive step Aristotle does not.

## What the reading did not settle

- **The Greek, and the printed editions.** This is a reading of two fetched public-domain translations, not of the Greek; *steresis* and the V.22 senses should be confirmed against a scholarly edition (e.g., the Revised Oxford Translation / Barnes, and Ross's *Metaphysics* commentary) before any of these quotations reaches the manuscript. The "toward restoration" gloss in finding 2 is the reader's inference from V.22's "what it naturally has," not a phrase Aristotle uses.
- **The other four privation arrivals.** Marcia, Minuchin, the attachment literature, and the *malas* mapping (Kṣemarāja) remain unread; this reading settles only the Aristotle entry of the five.
- **Whether the therapeutic step has any ancient warrant elsewhere.** The medical tradition (privation/health in the Hippocratic and Galenic line) is the natural place the prescriptive move might have a classical source; it was not searched here.
