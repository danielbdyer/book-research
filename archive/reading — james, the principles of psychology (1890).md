# reading — james, the principles of psychology (1890)

**What was read.** *The Principles of Psychology* (New York: Henry Holt, 1890), Vol. I, chapter VIII "The Relations of Minds to Other Things" (the acquaintance / knowledge-about passage) and chapter IX "The Stream of Thought" (the "fringe" and "feelings of tendency" apparatus). Read at the primary text in two independent full-text transcriptions of the 1890 Holt first edition — York University's *Classics in the History of Psychology* (psychclassics.yorku.ca) and Project Gutenberg #57628 — which agree character-for-character on every quoted sentence and place each page-break at the identical word, so the pagination below is firm rather than approximate. Not read: the rest of the *Principles*, and *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902), whose "fruits" test is the James chair's other pole and stays a separate, unread work.

**How it is cited.** By volume and page in the 1890 Holt first edition: `James, Principles of Psychology, I:221`. Where a phrase is load-bearing the page is given exactly (I:221 for acquaintance, I:255 and I:258 for the fringe).

**Why this reading.** The felt-shift concern's implicit-knowing notes cited James from memory for two things: the distinction between a knowing had *through* contact and a knowing accumulated *about* a thing, and a felt sense of a whole that is present before it can be worded. The failable question: does the *Principles* carry both, in the wording the notes use? It carries both — and the reading corrects one thing the notes must not claim.

## What the reading settled

1. **Acquaintance vs. knowledge-about — grounded, and it is Grote's before it is James's.** James states the distinction plainly (I:221): "There are two kinds of knowledge broadly and practically distinguishable: we may call them respectively *knowledge of acquaintance* and *knowledge-about*," illustrated by "I know the color blue when I see it, and the flavor of a pear when I taste it … but *about* the inner nature of these facts or what makes them what they are, I can say nothing at all. I cannot impart acquaintance with them to any one who has not already made it himself." All elementary natures "must either not be known at all, or known in this dumb way of acquaintance without *knowledge-about*" (I:221). This grounds the James pole of `[[there is a knowing-through as well as a knowing-about and the book asks to be dwelt in until its words go transparent]]`, whose other pole (Polanyi's indwelling) is already read, so that note moves from privated to **full**. **A both-halves detail to keep:** James does not claim the coinage — he footnotes it "Cf. John Grote: *Exploratio Philosophica*, p. 60; H. Helmholtz, *Popular Scientific Lectures*, p. 308–9" (I:221 n.). The distinction originates with **John Grote** (1865); James is a self-flagged debt (a "Cf." pointer), so where the book leans on the *phrase* the credit is Grote's, and Grote's primary is unread.

2. **The felt "fringe" of a not-yet-articulated whole — grounded.** In "The Stream of Thought" James names the penumbra of felt relations around every image: "Every definite image in the mind is steeped and dyed in the free water that flows round it" (I:255); consciousness is "in very large measure constituted of *feelings* of *tendency*, often so vague that we are unable to name them at all" (I:254); and he coins the term — "Let us use the words *psychic overtone, suffusion,* or *fringe*, to designate the influence of a faint brain-process upon our thought, as it makes it aware of relations and objects but dimly perceived" (I:258), the fringe being "part of the *object cognized*" (I:258 n.), a "felt fringe of relations" (I:259). Most usefully for the concern, the anticipatory felt whole: "We all of us have this permanent consciousness of whither our thought is going. It is a feeling like any other, a feeling of what thoughts are next to arise, before they have arisen" (I:255–256). This supports the claims that wordless knowing carries a felt sense of a whole before its parts are worded (e.g. `[[the reader carries an implicit sense of the whole and each part carries that whole forward]]`), James standing beside Gendlin and Merleau-Ponty as a third arrival on the felt-whole.

3. **Prior — yes; richer — no.** James ranks acquaintance as foundational and ineffable but *barer*, not fuller: "Feelings are the germ and starting point of cognition, thoughts the developed tree," and "Through feelings we become acquainted with things, but only by our thoughts do we know *about* them" (I:222). But: "Knowledge *about* a thing is knowledge of its relations. Acquaintance with it is limitation to the bare impression which it makes" (I:259). So James grounds that acquaintance is *prior* and *cannot be conveyed in words* — not that it is a richer knowing than articulation.

## What the reading did not settle

- **The "richer / more" framing is not Jamesian and must not be attributed to him.** James ranks acquaintance the *barer* knowing (I:259). The signature phrase "we can know more than we can tell" is **Polanyi's** (*The Tacit Dimension*, 1966), not James's; where a note pairs them, the *phrase* is Polanyi's and James is cited only for the acquaintance/ineffability/priority structure. Any note that leaned on James for acquaintance being *richer* has been corrected to the prior-and-ineffable reading.
- **John Grote is owed a reading.** The acquaintance/knowledge-about distinction is Grote's coinage (*Exploratio Philosophica*, 1865); if the book uses the distinction as a named borrowing, Grote's primary is unread. Helmholtz (also in James's "Cf.") is likewise unread here.
- **The "fruits" test stays a separate work.** The James chair's other pole — value judged by fruits, and the transiency mark on mystical states — is *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902), not read in this pass; the felt-shift surface's James pole ("the truth of a state shows in its fruits") still rests on that unread work.
