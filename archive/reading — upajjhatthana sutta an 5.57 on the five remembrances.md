# reading — upajjhatthana sutta an 5.57 on the five remembrances

**What was read.** The Upajjhaṭṭhana Sutta (AN 5.57), "The Five Remembrances," in Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu's freely-distributed translation at Access to Insight, retrieved 2026-08-10. The Maraṇassati suttas (AN 6.19–6.20), the Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta (SN 22.59) on not-self, and Dhammapada 277 on impermanence were named in the note but not separately read here. This addresses reading-queue tier 1b, entry 39.

**How it is cited.** `Upajjhaṭṭhana Sutta (AN 5.57)`, by the stable Pali Canon locator. Quotations are Ṭhānissaro's and are marked below; read in translation, not the Pali, and the terms (*maraṇasati*, *anicca*, *anattā*) should be confirmed against a scholarly edition before manuscript use.

**Why this reading.** [[buddhist impermanence faces loss as real rather than as veiled presence]] holds that Buddhist death-mindfulness faces loss as real and constitutive, with nothing abiding behind the change to recover — the independent second arrival for the finite, pairing with Heidegger. The failable question: do the suttas face loss as real (death, separation from all that is dear) rather than as veiled presence?

## What the reading settled

1. **Loss is faced as real and constitutive — confirmed.** AN 5.57 gives five facts everyone should reflect on often: "I am subject to aging, have not gone beyond aging"; "I am subject to illness, have not gone beyond illness"; "I am subject to death, have not gone beyond death"; "I will grow different, separate from all that is dear and appealing to me"; and "I am the owner of my actions, heir to my actions… and have my actions as my arbitrator." Death is certain, and separation from all that is dear is certain — loss faced directly, not read as the concealment of something still present. And the reflection is not morbid but medicinal: it counters the intoxications of youth, of health, and of life. This is the finite the recognition doctrine — nothing lost, only concealed — has no organ for.

**Verdict for the note.** Buddhist death-mindfulness is a genuine independent arrival at the finite, confirmed at AN 5.57: it faces loss as real and constitutive. With Heidegger's being-toward-death it makes the finite a convergence — one pole from the book's own foundation, one wholly outside it, mirroring the aletheia and pratyabhijñā pairing. The note moves from privated to full. The refusal — *anattā*, no abiding self to recover, which cuts against "you already know everything you are" — is the tradition's well-established doctrine, cited by locus (SN 22.59) but not separately read here.

## What the reading did not settle

- **The Pali, and the *anattā*/*anicca* suttas.** Read in Ṭhānissaro's translation, not the Pali; AN 6.19–6.20 (*maraṇasati* proper), SN 22.59 (not-self), and Dhammapada 277 (impermanence) were named but not separately read, and the *anattā* refusal is held from the tradition's doctrine rather than from a fetched text.
- **Whether the medicinal framing softens the finite.** AN 5.57 reflects on loss to loosen clinging, not to grieve; whether that is the same facing-of-loss the book's clearing wants, or a different use of it, is a fuller reading's question.
