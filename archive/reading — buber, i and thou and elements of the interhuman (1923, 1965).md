# reading — buber, i and thou and elements of the interhuman (1923, 1965)

**What was read.** Martin Buber, *I and Thou* (*Ich und Du*, 1923), read whole at the full Ronald Gregor Smith translation (1937), retrieved 2026-08-24 from the Internet Archive text of the Smith edition — a genuine primary read of the whole work, which upgrades the vault's earlier Buber grounding beyond the tree fragment (`archive/reading — buber, i and thou on the tree (1923).md`). The asymmetric-relations material Buber develops in "Elements of the Interhuman" and the "Education" essay (*The Knowledge of Man*, 1965; *Between Man and Man*, 1947) was read via a reputable scholarly profile — Kalman Yaron, "Martin Buber," *Prospects* (UNESCO: International Bureau of Education) XXIII.1/2, 1993 — not at the primary essays, and is marked secondary below. The German (*Ich und Du*) was not read; a proper copyright edition awaits before any quotation reaches the manuscript, per the vault's Buber precedent.

**How it is cited.** `Buber, I and Thou, Part One / Part Two / Part Three` by the work's three-part structure, the stable locator across editions (Smith's pagination is not cited, being edition-specific); quotations are Smith's and are marked. The interhuman material is cited `Yaron 1993 (on Buber's "Elements of the Interhuman")` and flagged secondary, since the primary essays were not opened. Where Smith and Kaufmann render a line differently the Smith wording is given (Smith: "In the beginning is relation"; Kaufmann: "In the beginning is the relation").

**Why this reading.** The `[[martin buber]]` chair holds that the book's whole vocabulary of the between runs on Buber's I-Thou while its load was assigned by the mesh grid at 65–75% and "untested against a primary reading," and it names three owed questions: whether the between is ontological in the strong sense the book needs; how the necessary lapse of every Thou into an It bears on a curriculum that aims at the relation; and the exact form of the argument against Heidegger. The reading queue (tier-7 entry 86) sharpens these to the one that bears on the whole book: **does Buber concede any reliable preparation for the between, or is it strictly ungraspable** — the test every device must pass, "does it turn Thou to It?" The failable question: does *I and Thou* state the between as prior-to-both ontology and the self as constituted in the meeting; and does Buber concede a preparation for the meeting while refusing its production?

## What the reading settled

1. **The between is ontological in the strong sense, and the self is constituted in the meeting — confirmed at the text.** Buber's two primary words are not two attitudes toward a prior self but two ways a self comes to be: "There is no I taken in itself, but only the I of the primary word I-Thou and the I of the primary word I-It" (Part One), and "Through the Thou a man becomes I" (Part One). The relation is prior to its terms — "In the beginning is relation" (Part Three) — and is not the product of either party. This is the strong ontology the book's vocabulary of the between depends on, stated in Buber's own words rather than derived, and it grounds `[[a person becomes an I through being met by a You so the encounter constitutes the self rather than costing it]]` on its Buber side.

2. **Entry 86 answered — Buber concedes preparation but refuses production, and the distinction is exactly the book's condition-design.** The meeting cannot be produced by technique or will: "The Thou meets me through grace — it is not found by seeking" (Part One), and "Every means is an obstacle. Only when every means has collapsed does the meeting come about" (Part One). Yet it is not passive either — "Concentration and fusion into the whole being can never take place through my agency, nor can it ever take place without me" (Part One), because "my speaking of the primary word to it is an act of my being … The primary word I-Thou can be spoken only with the whole being" (Part One). So there **is** a reliable preparation — the turning toward, the availability, the whole-being readiness — and there is **no** reliable production: the meeting comes by grace. This is precisely the book's Book I facilitation posture, which stands two steps back from the outcome and tends the conditions for the other's own recognition rather than producing a transformation; Buber arrived at the distinction first. In asymmetric settings — education, psychotherapy — he names the preparation concretely: "making him actually present," the distinction of acceptance from confirmation ("affirming his being 'as he has been created to become'"), the "primary distance" that lets an independent other exist, and the helper who "does not forfeit anything of himself" (`Yaron 1993`, secondary). The refusal is kept and sharp: the preparation is availability, never a technique that manufactures the meeting.

3. **The necessary lapse bears directly on the curriculum — the chair's second question, answered.** "But this is the exalted melancholy of our fate, that every Thou in our world must become an It. … As soon as the relation has been worked out or has been permeated with a means, the Thou becomes an object among objects" (Part One). The lapse is the fate of every relation, not a failure of one; and the mechanism of the lapse is exactly *the application of a means*. A curriculum that aims at the relation is coherent in Buber's terms only while it prepares availability, and it turns its own object to an It the moment it is held as a means that produces the between — which is the live warning the book's register of "protocols, agreements and rotations" (the chair's own phrase) must answer. This is the reading that makes "does it turn Thou to It?" an operational test rather than a slogan.

4. **The Heidegger disagreement — its structure grounded, its explicit text still owed.** *I and Thou* establishes the positive claim beneath Buber's later objection: all genuine disclosure is *address*. "All real living is meeting" (Part One), and even the ground of being is met, not merely thought — "the eternal Thou" is addressed and "by its nature cannot become It" (Parts One and Three). Buber's disclosure therefore always has someone being addressed in it, which is the ground of his charge that Heidegger's disclosure of Being is impersonal and has no one addressed. Since the book borrows disclosure from Heidegger (aletheia) and the encounter from Buber, this disagreement runs through its foundations rather than beside them. The explicit critique — "What Is Man?" in *Between Man and Man* (1938) — was not read here; the *structure* of the disagreement is grounded from *I and Thou*, the exact argument still owed.

**Verdict for the chair.** Buber is confirmed **candidate, load thesis-bearing**, the load now upgraded from a 65–75% grid proposal to a primary reading of *I and Thou* whole; the seating stays the author's. Two of the chair's three questions are settled at the primary text (the between is strong ontology; the lapse bears via the means-clause), the third grounded in structure (the Heidegger disagreement) with its explicit text owed. Both halves of the correspondence are now quotable from *I and Thou* rather than derived from memory. The finding that most changes the vault: the book's condition-design is not in tension with Buber but is his own distinction between prepared availability and grace-given meeting — with his refusal kept as the test the curriculum must keep passing.

## What the reading did not settle

- **The primary "Elements of the Interhuman" and "Education" essays.** The asymmetric-relations material — making present, acceptance versus confirmation, primary distance, the non-forfeiting helper — was read via Yaron's scholarly profile, not the primary essays in *The Knowledge of Man* and *Between Man and Man*; those await a reading before any of it is quoted.
- **The explicit argument against Heidegger.** "What Is Man?" (*Between Man and Man*, 1938) is unread; the structure of the disagreement is grounded from *I and Thou*, the exact form of the argument is not.
- **The German, and manuscript quotation.** *Ich und Du* was not read; the Smith translation grounds the research, and a copyright edition (Smith or Kaufmann) is owed before any line reaches the book.
- **The companion poles of the three grounded notes.** Each is a convergence in which only the Buber pole is now read: Hegel's *Anerkennung* (§178) for the self-constituted-in-meeting note; Benveniste, Rosenzweig, and Levinas for the address-summons note; and Winnicott's potential space at its own text (*Playing and Reality* ch. 7) for the third-region note. They stand at `privated`, one witness grounded, the rest owed.
