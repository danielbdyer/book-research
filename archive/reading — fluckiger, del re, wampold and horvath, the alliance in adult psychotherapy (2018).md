# reading — fluckiger, del re, wampold and horvath, the alliance in adult psychotherapy (2018)

**What was read.** Christoph Flückiger, A. C. Del Re, Bruce E. Wampold and Adam O. Horvath, "The Alliance in Adult Psychotherapy: A Meta-Analytic Synthesis," *Psychotherapy* 55(4), 2018, pages 316–340. Read complete — twenty-six pages of the advance-online version, about 19,600 words including the study tables — from the copy the Society for the Advancement of Psychotherapy publishes, retrieved 2026-08-07, extracted with pypdf.

**How it is cited.** By the article's own section names and reported statistics, since the advance-online pagination differs from the issue's: "Flückiger et al. 2018, 'Is the Alliance–Outcome Relation a Causal Factor?'". Quotations were taken verbatim; the extraction renders mathematical symbols as entity codes (`/H11005` for an equals sign) and those are repaired in quotation. Nothing else is altered.

**Why this paper, and why it was not in the queue.** The author asked on 2026-08-07 for the source most likely to support the book's claims with measured evidence. The vault had nothing at all on the alliance literature: a search across `notes/`, `ops/`, `archive/` and `provenance/` for *alliance*, *common factors*, *Wampold*, *Horvath*, *Norcross*, *working alliance* and *contextual model* returned one passing mention on the Rogers evaluation and one in a provenance file. That absence is itself notable, because this is the literature that bears most directly on the corpus's participation law — the claim that what transforms a person is enacted participation inside a held container, and that nothing transforms from the audience.

The question the reading was set, in the failable form: **does the alliance–outcome relation survive the confound that early symptom improvement produces better alliance ratings?** It fails in the book's favour only if the association is largely explained by prior improvement, in which case the relational claim would gain a correlation that may run backwards.

## What the reading settled

**1. The size of the finding.** "a meta-analysis of 295 independent studies that covered more than 30,000 patients (published between 1978 and 2017) for face-to-face and Internet-based psychotherapy… The overall alliance–outcome association for face-to-face psychotherapy was r = .278 (95% confidence intervals [.256, .299], p < .0001; equivalent of d = .579)" (abstract). The authors state its robustness in the same place: "This relation remains consistent across assessor perspectives, alliance and outcome measures, treatment approaches, patient characteristics, and countries" (abstract). Their own summary of the body of work: "It is certainly among the richest bodies of empirical research on psychotherapy process outcome" ("Therapeutic Practices").

**2. The confound was tested directly, and it did not explain the finding away.** The section "Is the Alliance–Outcome Relation a Causal Factor?" takes up "the possibility that the alliance is merely an epiphenomenon, a consequence of intake symptom severity and early related changes during therapy (DeRubeis & Feeley, 1990)." Sixty-six of the studies reported both the raw correlation and a partial correlation adjusting for intake characteristics and early symptom change. "Our results indicated there were no statistically significant differences between zero-order and partial correlations (Q(1) = 1.651; p = .199)… (for zero-order correlations, r adjusted = .25; for partial correlations, r adjusted = .22). These results lend support to the hypothesis that the association between alliance and outcome is not primarily an epiphenomenon linked to intake characteristics and related early therapy gains." Beside that, they cite session-by-session work finding "that within-patient changes in the alliance is associated with subsequent symptom changes," naming Falkenström, Ekeblad and Holmqvist 2016, Feeley, DeRubeis and Gelfand 1999, Strunk, Brotman and DeRubeis 2010, Xu and Tracey 2015, and Zilcha-Mano et al. 2016.

**3. The authors state the limit of what this can ever be, and the limit is structural rather than temporary.** "longitudinal predictor analyses do not imply that there is an experimental causal relation between alliance and outcomes in therapy… Obtaining direct evidence of a causal experimental relation for the class of dyadic interpersonal variables, including the alliance, [is] not possible because it is ethically and conceptually not possible to randomize patients to treatment conditions where these variables are manipulated (e.g., to a high and low alliance condition)." What they claim on the strength of the longitudinal evidence is stated exactly: "there is robust empirical evidence that the alliance (measured during therapy) is a moderate causal facilitative factor for therapy outcomes at the end of therapy."

**4. The relation holds at the same size when the exchange is mediated and largely written.** The hypothesis going in ran the other way: "It has been repeatedly hypothesized that the alliance is probably less important in Internet-based therapy than in standard face-to-face therapies." The result: "The overall weighted average effect size was r = .275 (95% CI [.205, .344], p < .0001); equivalent of d = .572… quite similar to that found for face-to-face psychotherapy," across 23 independent samples and 1,178 clients, and the internet effect sizes were more homogeneous than the larger set. Their closing line on it: "Whatever aspects of the alliance are captured in Internet therapies, the alliance appears to relate to outcome, in a quantitative sense, similarly to face-to-face psychotherapy" ("Limitations of the Research"). The study table shows the treatments are mostly internet-delivered cognitive behavioural therapy, with alliance rated by the client in the first weeks, plus one video-conferencing and one telephone study.

**5. The effect follows the practitioner rather than the patient.** "there is meta-analytic evidence that a considerable proportion of the alliance–outcome correlation is strongly impacted by the therapist (Baldwin et al., 2007; Del Re et al., 2012)," and the moderator table records the same result as a ratio: between-therapist variability exceeds within-therapist variability, across 69 studies. This paper carries that finding from the 2012 analysis rather than recomputing it.

**6. The practice recommendations are specific and several match the book's own.** The authors close with a list, and three items sit close to material the corpus already carries: "Address ruptures in the alliance directly and immediately"; "Assess regularly from the client's perspective the strength or quality of the alliance"; and a note that disagreement between the practitioner's rating and the client's "is not something negative."

## What this does and does not do for the book

It supplies measured support for a mechanism the book describes. It does not support the book, and no evidence can, because nobody has read the book. That distinction is the vault's standing rule (`ops/methodology/authorial stance.md`), and it matters more here than usual, because a result of this size is the easiest thing in the vault to overstate.

The honest transfer question is left open by the reading and is sharper for it. Every study in this meta-analysis has a therapist in it. Internet-based cognitive behavioural therapy is mediated and often asynchronous, and it is still a person reading what a client wrote and answering it. A book answers nobody. So the internet finding narrows the distance between the book's form and the evidence without closing it, and what it rules out is one specific objection — that a relationship must be face-to-face and synchronous to do the work.

## What the reading did not settle

- **Whether anything in this literature reaches a reader alone with a text.** The nearest available test is the internet-therapy subset, and every case in it has a responding human.
- **What the alliance is.** The authors name the problem themselves: "Given the diversity in what researchers call the 'alliance,' we probably have collected and summarized different kinds of idiographic and nomothetic understandings," and they call the paper "a birds-eye view."
- **The therapist-effects result at its own source.** Baldwin, Wampold and Imel 2007 and Del Re et al. 2012 are cited here rather than read, so finding 5 above stands on this paper's summary of them.
- **Wampold's own theoretical argument.** *The Great Psychotherapy Debate* (Wampold and Imel 2015) is the contextual model this meta-analysis sits inside, and it is unread.
