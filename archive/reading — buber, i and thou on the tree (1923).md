# reading — buber, i and thou on the tree (1923)

**What was read.** The tree passage from Martin Buber's *I and Thou* (*Ich und Du*, 1923), Part One, in Walter Kaufmann's translation — read via reputable sources quoting it, including the Focusing Institute's posted extract ("Martin Buber — I Contemplate a Tree," focusing.org) and The Marginalian, retrieved 2026-08-10. The full *I and Thou* is under copyright in-container and was not read whole; the German was not read. This addresses reading-queue tier 1b, entry 38.

**How it is cited.** `Buber, I and Thou, Part One (the tree)`, by the stable section locator. Quotations are Kaufmann's and are marked below; the full text and the German (*Ich und Du*) await a copyright edition before manuscript use.

**Why this reading.** [[buber's tree extends the i-thou relation past the human]] holds that the tree passage is the chain-link for the non-human blind spot — the unmined half of the seated thinker whose I-Thou already carries the book's relational ontology — and that Buber withholds a mind from the tree, so the book needs a category for participation without cognition. The failable question: does the tree passage extend I-Thou past the human, and does Buber withhold attributing consciousness to the tree?

## What the reading settled

1. **I-Thou extends past the human — confirmed.** In the tree passage, "it can also happen, if will and grace are joined," that in contemplating the tree one is drawn into a relation and "the tree ceases to be an It"; the relation "is mutual." The between is not restricted to persons — a tree can be met as Thou. This is the chain-link the non-human blind spot needed, and it grows the book's *own* relational vocabulary (Buber's I-Thou) to the more-than-human rather than importing a stranger.

2. **A Thou without a mind — the hedge is confirmed, verbatim.** Buber refuses to attribute consciousness to the tree: "Does the tree then have consciousness, similar to our own? I have no experience of that." And: "What I encounter is neither the soul of a tree nor a dryad, but the tree itself." So the tree is a real Thou, and yet Buber withholds a mind from it and leaves its reciprocity an open question. The book that admitted this would need a category for participation without cognition — a witness that is neither a thinker nor a human recognizer — which is what the poems' two cats already are.

**Verdict for the note.** The tree passage is a genuine, low-cost and high-warrant arrival for the non-human blind spot: it is the unmined half of a seated thinker, and it extends the field's own ontology past the human. The refusal is confirmed and is the finding — Buber's tree is a Thou without a mind. The note moves from privated to full. Read via reputable sources quoting the Kaufmann translation, not the full primary.

## What the reading did not settle

- **The full primary, and the German.** Read via quoting sources, not *I and Thou* whole; the surrounding argument of Part One and the German (*Ich und Du*) await a copyright edition.
- **Whether "participation without cognition" is Buber's or the book's to build.** The category the book would need is named by the collision; whether Buber's own account (relation without attributed consciousness) already supplies it, or the book must build it, is a fuller reading's question.
