# reading — schwitzgebel, the unreliability of naive introspection (2008)

**What was read.** Eric Schwitzgebel, "The Unreliability of Naive Introspection," *The Philosophical Review* 117(2), April 2008, pages 245–273. Read complete — thirty pages of the offprint, about 10,900 words — from the copy at gwern.net/doc/philosophy/mind/2008-schwitzgebel.pdf, retrieved 2026-08-07, extracted with pypdf.

**What was not read, and why it matters little here.** The reading queue's address for this entry is *Perplexities of Consciousness* (MIT Press, 2011), the chapters on naive introspection. That book was not read. The 2008 article is the paper the book's central chapter of the same title develops, it carries the argument the queue's question asks about, and it is freely available where the book is not. Every claim drawn from this reading cites the article rather than the book, and the book's other chapters — on visual imagery, on peripheral experience, on the constancy of tactile experience — remain unread.

**How it is cited.** By the journal's page numbers, which the offprint carries: "Schwitzgebel 2008, p. 259." The extraction runs words together at line ends and inside headings ("stancesofextendedreflection"), and those spacing artifacts are repaired in quotation. Nothing else is altered.

**Why this paper.** The counterpoint round of 2026-08-06 named him the sharper of the two canonical attacks on the book's verification standard, and the Nisbett and Wilson reading of 2026-08-07 sharpened the reason: Nisbett and Wilson exempt current sensations from their finding, and Schwitzgebel is understood to deny exactly that exemption. The queued question was whether his unreliability claim covers bodily and interoceptive report specifically, or is drawn mostly from vision, imagery and inner speech.

## What the reading settled

**1. The question is answered in the hard direction, because he leads with emotional and bodily experience.** His first extended case, section iii, is emotion, and the features he says elude us are the bodily ones. He asks whether joy "is sometimes in the head, sometimes more visceral, sometimes a thrill, sometimes an expansiveness — or, instead, does joy have a single, consistent core"; whether "emotional consciousness is simply the experience of one's bodily arousal, and other bodily states, as William James (1981 [1890]) seems to suggest"; and whether "emotional experience is consistently located in space (for example, particular places in the interior of one's head and body)" (p. 249). His verdict on that case: "in the case of emotion the very phenomenology itself — the 'qualitative' character of our consciousness — is not entirely evident… Even the grossest features of emotional experience largely elude us. Reflection doesn't remove our ignorance, or it delivers haphazard results" (p. 249). His own summary of scope names emotion first: "we're prone to gross error, even in favorable circumstances of extended reflection, about our ongoing emotional, visual, and cognitive phenomenology" (p. 259).

**2. He refuses the present-tense refuge by name.** Section iv opens on exactly the move the Nisbett and Wilson carve-out permits: "Still, you might suggest, when we attend to particular instances of ongoing emotional experience, we can't go wrong, or don't, or not by far. We may concede the past to the skeptic, but not the present" (pp. 250–251). His answer is that the claim "just seems blatantly unrealistic" (p. 251), and the experiment he runs on the reader asks whether introspection reveals a present emotion "as clearly as visual observation reveals the presence of the text before your eyes," including "its viscerality or cognitiveness, its involvement with conscious imagery, thought, proprioception, or whatever" (p. 251).

**3. He does grant a narrow exemption, and it is not the one the book needs.** "Some aspects of visual experience are so obvious it would be difficult to go wrong about them" (p. 252), and on pain: "It is hard, seemingly, to go too badly wrong in introspecting really vivid, canonical pains and foveal colors" (p. 259). He then refuses to let that class carry the argument: "to use these cases only as one's inference base rigs the game," and he names the cracks even there — "confusion between mild pains and itches or tingles," "the football player who sincerely denies he's hurt," "the difficulty we sometimes feel in locating pains precisely" (p. 259). The secure cases he allows are a vivid canonical pain and a bright foveal color, and a felt shift is neither.

**4. His own position is that introspection is necessary as well as unreliable.** Section ii states it: he holds "that the introspection of current conscious experience is both (i) possible, important, necessary for a full life, and central to the development of a full scientific understanding of the mind, and (ii) highly untrustworthy" (p. 246). He places the meditative traditions and Titchener as holding both claims too, and states his difference with them precisely: they "take comfort in optimism about introspection 'properly' conducted — so they hardly qualify as general skeptics or pessimists," and his question about them is "Might they be right in their doubts but less so in their hopes?" (p. 246).

**5. He names the repair the book would need, calls it interesting, and says the evidence was not there.** "Perhaps the right kind of learning, practice, or care could largely shield us from error — an interesting possibility that merits exploration! — but I see as yet no robust scientific support for such hopes" (p. 259). His footnote 21 lists where he had looked: classical introspective training in his own 2004 paper, careful interviewing about randomly sampled experiences in Hurlburt and Schwitzgebel 2007, Schooler and Schreiber 2004 on the state of the science, and "Very recently, there has been some promising work on meditation: see Lutz, Dunne, and Davidson 2007" (p. 259 n. 21).

**6. His explanation for introspective overconfidence is the absence of correction, and his worked example of being corrected is another person.** "Because no one ever scolds us for getting it wrong about our experience and we never see decisive evidence of error, we become cavalier. This lack of corrective feedback encourages a hypertrophy of confidence" (pp. 259–260). The case he tells against himself is domestic and it turns on a second person: his wife says he seems angry about the dishes, he reflects sincerely and finds no anger, "But I'm wrong, of course, as I usually am in such situations: My wife reads my face better than I introspect" (p. 252).

## What the reading corrected in the vault

Until this reading, the vault treated Schwitzgebel as the broader of the two attacks and expected his breadth to be the problem. The reading shows the problem is his aim rather than his breadth. He is not a general skeptic about inner life, and he says so: he thinks introspection is necessary for a full life. What he denies is precisely the reliability of the report the book's standard depends on, and he denies it with emotion, viscerality, and bodily location as his first and most developed case. So the exemption the Nisbett and Wilson reading found — that current sensations are private facts knowable "with near certainty" — is not a settled position in the literature; it is one side of a live disagreement, and this paper is the other side, published thirty-one years later and aimed at that exact claim.

The vault also treated his objection as one that "lands whichever way" the Nisbett and Wilson question fell. That is right, and the reading adds what it lands on. Nisbett and Wilson attack the report of a cause; Schwitzgebel attacks the report of a present bodily state. Between them they cover both halves of what the book asks a reader to do, and the two attacks do not overlap.

## What the reading did not settle

- **Change-detection specifically.** Schwitzgebel attacks judgments about the character and even the presence of a current emotional experience. He never treats the narrower comparative judgment that something is different now from a moment ago, which is what Gendlin's criterion asks for. The implication runs against the standard — a person who cannot reliably say whether they are angry is poorly placed to say whether something shifted — and it is an implication rather than an argument he makes.
- **Whether the training evidence has since arrived.** He set the condition in 2008 and the vault holds one later result that meets its description: Petitmengin and colleagues, *Consciousness and Cognition* 22, 2013, in which subjects guided by elicitation interview detected a manipulation in 80 percent of cases. That paper has not been read here, and whether it is the "robust scientific support" he asked for is the question it now has to answer.
- **The rest of *Perplexities of Consciousness*.** The chapters on visual imagery, on peripheral experience, and on constant tactile experience are unread, and one of them — the tactile chapter, on whether you continuously experience your feet in your shoes — bears on the book's practices more directly than anything in this paper.
