# reading — vygotsky, mind in society chapter 4 (1978)

**What was read.** L. S. Vygotsky, "Internalization of Higher Psychological Functions," chapter 4 of *Mind in Society: The Development of Higher Psychological Processes*, ed. Cole, John-Steiner, Scribner and Souberman, Harvard University Press 1978, pages 52 to 57. Read complete, from a scan of the Harvard edition published by Florida Atlantic University (home.fau.edu/musgrove/web/vygotsky1978.pdf, retrieved 2026-08-08). Chapter 6, on learning and development, sits in the same scan and was not read here beyond the opening pages that follow chapter 4's last line.

**How it is cited, and one caution about page numbers.** By chapter and by the lettered list Vygotsky uses to state the internalization process, because those are stable: "Vygotsky 1978, ch. 4, item (b)." The scan's character recognition interleaves the running heads with the body text and prints the page numbers out of order, so a page citation taken from it cannot be trusted on its own; the twice-appearing law is conventionally cited at page 57 of this edition, and this reading does not independently confirm that. The recognition also mangles words throughout, printing "COil·ditiolled rdlcxcs" for "conditioned reflexes" and "illtrapersonal" for "intrapersonal"; those errors are repaired silently in quotation and nothing else is altered.

**Why this reading.** The correspondence program named this the form's deepest exposure. If the higher functions become a person's own only by first existing between people, then a reader who never had a facilitator may be unable to internalize a held relationship from letters, and the exposure would be structural rather than fixable. The question had two halves: whether Vygotsky's law covers self-regulating functions of the kind the book's practice is, or only tool-mediated cognition — where the transfer to facilitation would be analogy rather than arrival.

## What the reading settled

1. **The law covers self-mastery, in Vygotsky's own classification, so the transfer is an arrival rather than an analogy.** He divides mediated activity into tools and signs and separates them by what they are aimed at: "The tool's function is to serve as the conductor of human influence on the object of activity; it is externally oriented… The sign, on the other hand, changes nothing in the object of a psychological operation. It is a means of internal activity aimed at mastering oneself; the sign is internally oriented" (ch. 4, at the figure-4 discussion). Mastering oneself is the category, and the instances he names when he states the law are not tool use: "This applies equally to voluntary attention, to logical memory, and to the formation of concepts" (item b). Voluntary attention is a presence-and-return function by any reading, which is the book's own practice described in another vocabulary.

2. **The law itself, in his words, demands an actual relation.** "Every function in the child's cultural development appears twice; first, on the social level, and later, on the individual level; first, between people (interpsychological), and then inside the child (intrapsychological)… All the higher functions originate as actual relations between human individuals" (item b). The word *actual* is his, and it is the word that bears on a book.

3. **The other person's response is constitutive rather than helpful, and the pointing example is where he shows it.** A child reaching for an object out of range is at first only reaching. "When the mother comes to the child's aid and realizes his movement indicates something, the situation changes fundamentally. Pointing becomes a gesture for others. The child's unsuccessful attempt engenders a reaction not from the object he seeks but from another person. Consequently, the primary meaning of that unsuccessful grasping movement is established by others." He closes the example by making the other's uptake definitional: the movement "becomes a true gesture only after it objectively manifests all the functions of pointing for others and is understood by others as such a gesture." Somebody has to notice and answer, or the function does not form.

4. **Internalization is slow, and the external form has to keep running while it happens.** "The transformation of an interpersonal process into an intrapersonal one is the result of a long series of developmental events. The process being transformed continues to exist and to change as an external form of activity for a long time before definitively turning inward… they take on the character of inner processes only as a result of a prolonged development" (item c). He adds that for many functions the external stage never ends: "For many functions, the stage of external signs lasts forever, that is, it is their final stage of development."

5. **His refusal is the direct contrary of the book's central finding, and it is not a side remark but his thesis.** On Vygotsky's account the higher functions are acquired from outside and were not present beforehand: "The internalization of socially rooted and historically developed activities is the distinguishing feature of human psychology, the basis of the qualitative leap from animal to human psychology." He states the replacement in the strongest available terms — "Psychological processes as they appear in animals actually cease to exist; they are incorporated into this system of behavior and are culturally reconstituted and developed to form a new psychological entity." The book's finding is that deep change is disclosure of something already present rather than acquisition of something new. These are contraries about the same class of function, and the vault keeps the refusal whole rather than softening it.

## What the reading did not settle

- **Whether writing can occupy the interpsychological position.** Vygotsky never considers a text, a letter, or any absent partner. Everything he analyses has a live person who responds in the moment, and his own example turns on the mother reacting. What follows for a book is inference from his conditions and not his finding.
- **Inner speech.** *Thinking and Speech* is where he works out how external speech turns inward, and the queue named it as the second address. Unread here.
- **Chapter 6 and the zone of proximal development.** The chapter that would bear on whether a text can operate ahead of a reader's current level sits in the same volume and was not read.

## What the reading changes in the vault

The correspondence's exposure is confirmed rather than dissolved, and it is confirmed with its mechanism attached: the law reaches exactly the kind of function the book's practice trains, and it requires a responding other, over long time, with the external form still running. Three findings the vault already held press from the same side — the standard's two attackers both blame the absence of correction by another person, the second person the book needs splits into a judge it cannot ship and a mirror it can, and every measured case of a written exchange keeping its effect has a live responder. Vygotsky adds a fourth and makes the set developmental rather than merely epistemic. The claims are [[the internalization law reaches self-mastery and demands a relation that answers]] and [[vygotsky's higher functions are acquired from outside rather than disclosed from within]].
