# reading — nisbett and wilson, telling more than we can know (1977)

**What was read.** Richard E. Nisbett and Timothy DeCamp Wilson, "Telling More Than We Can Know: Verbal Reports on Mental Processes," *Psychological Review* 84(3), May 1977, pages 231–259. Read complete — twenty-nine pages, about 21,200 words — from the scanned reprint published at California State University Long Beach (home.csulb.edu/~cwallis/382/readings/482/nisbett saying more.pdf, retrieved 2026-08-07), extracted with pypdf.

**How it is cited.** By the journal's own page numbers, which are stable across every reprint: "Nisbett and Wilson 1977, p. 236." The scan's page headers carry those numbers and were used to check the mapping at both ends. Quotations were taken verbatim from the extracted text; the extraction inserts stray spaces inside words and breaks hyphenated line ends ("intro­spective," "refe rent"), and those artifacts are repaired in quotation. Nothing else is altered.

**Why this paper second.** It is the first entry in tier 1 of `ops/reading queue.md`, which is the tier the author's own statements make load-bearing, and its question inherits directly from the first primary reading: Gendlin's 1964 paper certifies that a process moved rather than that a content is true, which narrows what introspection must deliver from describing an inner state to noticing change against no-change. The queued question was whether the confabulation finding reaches a standard that only asks a person to notice change.

## What the reading settled

**1. The carve-out exists, it is explicit, and it names current sensations.** In the section "Confusion Between Content and Process" the authors state what people do know about themselves: "we do indeed have direct access to a great storehouse of private knowledge… The individual knows a host of personal historical facts; he knows the focus of his attention at any given point in time; he knows what his current sensations are and has what almost all psychologists and philosophers would assert to be 'knowledge' at least quantitatively superior to that of observers concerning his emotions, evaluations, and plans" (p. 255). They then name the confusion their whole paper is about: "The only mystery is why people are so poor at telling the difference between private facts that can be known with near certainty and mental processes to which there may be no access at all" (p. 255). The paper's target is stated in its own abstract as "higher order cognitive processes" and in its first conclusion as "the effects of particular stimuli on higher order, inference-based responses" (pp. 231, 233). A report that a sensation is present now sits on the near-certainty side of their own line.

**2. The paper attacks change-detection directly, in a section devoted to it, and the vault did not know this.** Pages 235 to 237 carry a section titled "Awareness of the Existence of a Change Process." It opens by drawing the distinction the vault's narrowed standard rests on: "There is an important difference between awareness of the existence of an evaluation or motive state and awareness of a changed evaluation or motive state" (p. 235). It then reports two studies in which people underwent large changes and did not experience them as changes. Bem and McConnell (1970) asked subjects a week later what their earlier attitude had been; the experimental subjects' attitudes had moved substantially and "they reported that their current attitudes were the same as their premanipulation attitudes. Thus subjects apparently changed their attitudes in the absence of any subjective experience of change" (p. 236). Goethals and Reckman (1973) turned pro-busing high school students against busing in one group discussion, and the converted students recalled their original opinions as having been more anti-busing than the students who had actually started anti-busing recalled theirs. The authors quote the original report: "No subject reported that the discussion had had any effect in changing or modifying his position" (p. 237). The paper's own summary of the two studies: "even when they can report on such states, they may not report that a change has taken place in these states" (p. 237).

**3. The paper's own account predicts that a check made later is worse rather than better.** Under "Removal in time": "Perhaps chief among the circumstances that should decrease accuracy in self-report is a separation in time between the report and the actual occurrence of the process" (p. 251). The authors note that in almost all the research they review, subjects were questioned "immediately after its occurrence, often within seconds" (p. 251), and that with distance "the vagaries of memory may allow the invention of factors presumed to be present at the time the process occurred… the more removed in time the report is from the process, the more stereotypical should be the reported explanation" (pp. 251–252).

**4. Accuracy produced by a tradition's rules is not access, in their own words.** "we may say that people will be accurate in reports about the causes of their behavior and evaluations wherever the culture, or a subculture, specifies clearly what stimuli should produce which responses, and especially where there is continuing feedback from the culture or subculture concerning the extent to which the individual is following the prescribed rules" — and immediately after: "such accuracy cannot be regarded as evidence of direct access to processes of evaluation. It is evidence for nothing more than the ability to describe the formal rules of evaluation" (p. 254). Their standard for what would count as awareness is set in the same terms: "verbal report which exceeds in accuracy that obtained from observers provided with a general description of the stimulus and response in question" (p. 251).

**5. A felt shift is an intermediate result, and the paper names taking one for the process as a specific error.** "we are often capable of describing intermediate results of a series of mental operations in such a way as to promote the feeling that we are describing the operations themselves" (p. 255). Their example is Maier's subject who had genuine "imagery of monkeys swinging from trees" before solving a problem: the imagery was real, it may even have helped, and "it is scarcely reasonable to propose that such imagery was the process by which the problem was solved" (p. 255).

**6. The paper allows that reports are often right, and says when.** "reports will be accurate when influential stimuli are (a) available and (b) plausible causes of the response, and when (c) few or no plausible but noninfluential factors are available" (p. 253). On the scope of their own theory: "We do not wish to imply that all or even most a priori causal theories are wrong. Verbal reports relying on such theories will typically be wrong not because the theories are in error in every case but merely because they are incorrectly applied in the particular instance" (p. 248).

## What the reading corrected in the vault

The vault has held one position on this paper since the blind repeat pass of 2026-08-06, and it is recorded at [[introspective access is the felt shift's undefended premise]]: "the sharper enemy is Schwitzgebel, because Nisbett and Wilson's verified core concerns reports about the causes of behavior, not reports of current experience." Half of that survives the reading and half does not. The half that survives is the carve-out, which is real and stronger than the vault knew, because the authors name current sensations themselves rather than leaving the exemption to be inferred. The half that fails is the conclusion drawn from it. The paper does not confine itself to causal reports: it devotes a titled section to the failure of change-detection, which is the exact discrimination the narrowed certificate rests on, and that section is invisible to any characterization made at citation depth. The correction was available only by reading the paper.

## What the reading did not settle

- **Whether the change-blindness result transfers to a within-session bodily comparison.** Both studies measure a person's memory of a prior *attitude* across one to two weeks. The felt shift is a comparison of a present bodily state against one held minutes earlier. The paper does not test that case, and its own reasoning about removal in time cuts both ways here: the immediate comparison sits in the region it calls more accurate, and the diachronic check sits in the region it calls less accurate.
- **Whether introspective accuracy is trainable.** The paper is silent on training. It contains no discussion of trained introspection, of practice, or of expert observers of their own states, and it does not cite the introspectionist tradition. Petitmengin's elicitation-interview result, which the vault holds from the counterpoint round, therefore contradicts nothing the 1977 paper claims; it answers a question the paper never asks.
- **Schwitzgebel.** The attack on reports of current experience, which is where the book actually stands, remains unread. Nothing in this reading substitutes for it, and the carve-out found here is precisely what he is understood to deny.

## The disagreement this reading opened

The vault holds two defenses of the felt shift against its fluent counterfeit, and this reading shows that they ask for opposite timings. Against a fluent page producing a bodily yes, the vault's repair is to make the discrimination slower and check it across days ([[a genuine felt shift changes what can be said next]]). Against the change-blindness finding, the paper's own reasoning says the later check is the weaker one, because a person comparing a present state against a remembered earlier state is doing the thing Goethals and Reckman's subjects failed at. The disagreement between the two defenses is recorded as its own tension and is not resolved here.
