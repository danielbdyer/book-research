# source — the poems

Sixteen poems, numbered and untitled, written across the author's life and supplied to the vault on 2026-08-03 with the framing: "the source material I've been writing all my life. Many (not all — number 16 as the divergent poem) of these predate the book, but certainly any phrases you see like 'the youngness is just beginning' are actually quotes from my poems and not the other way around."

The collection runs from a first poem about the author's father and the concept of home ("danny, this is your daddy speaking") through love poems, grief poems, and poems of self-instruction, to the sixteenth — the one poem that does not predate the book, written from inside its era, about the move across the country and the sentence that finally leaves the architecture.

## Standing

This document has **primary** standing under `ops/methodology/source standing.md`, a third standing created for it: the author's own prior work, upstream of the book, which the corpus quotes and descends from. A primary source asserts nothing about the book; the datum is the book's use of it. The corpus itself names this collection's existence — the ripeness rule's own parenthetical referent is "the author's own archive of poems and drafts" (atlas §V.3) — so the archive the house rule was written about is now in hand.

The direction of quotation is the standing's whole point, and the author states it: for poems 1–15, a phrase shared between poem and corpus originates in the poem. The sixteenth is exempted by the author's own flag and reads as the reverse flow — the book's language returning to the life.

## What the collection is

This section is the first reading and governs the next one, per `ops/methodology/reading a source on its own terms.md`. The collection is a body of work in its own right and is read as one before it is compared with anything.

Sixteen poems, unedited, carrying their own misspellings — a personal archive rather than a prepared book. Their recurring subject is a temperament: a man who loves by taking other people inside himself, who therefore cannot tolerate endings, and whose care for others turns out to be his own wanting said in a register nobody has to answer. Four findings are extracted as claims. The address arrives late and stabilizes slowly — five of the first ten poems have no second person at all, the first stable *you* is the speaker himself, a real name appears only at thirteen, and the last poem asks a plural, public question that can be refused ([[the collection moves from poems with no addressee to a request that can be refused]]). Endings are refused as a considered position, stated as a theory in poem seven and reversed once, in poem fourteen ([[the poems treat incompletion as a position rather than a defect]]). The register travels from elaborate to bare, and the last poem diagnoses the elaborate one as a building that let longing arrive dressed ([[the collection's last poem names its earlier ornateness as an evasion]]). And the caretaking is named by the collection itself, three times across decades, with its cost accounted and its usefulness credited ([[the poems diagnose a caretaking that converts wanting into service]]).

The collection is bound by return rather than by argument. The golf course paved over in the first poem is walked past in the last; the bird noticed on a windowsill in poem twelve becomes the final poem's word for presence itself; nests, weather, open palms, melody against lyrics, and the source of light all come back carrying more than they left with.

## What the collection shares with the corpus

This is the comparison pass and it runs second. It is a record of correspondences, not an account of what the poems are, and it is kept here rather than in `notes/` so that it cannot be mistaken for one. The concerns the book systematizes stand in these poems in the author's unassisted voice, years earlier: the father and home, with welcome as the father's first act (#1); shared perception as the ground of knowing ("thank goodness we share that perception," #6); surrender and effort held in one breath ("complete surrender / and trying one's very hardest," #7); endings that are not endings, "composure in the face of lack of closure" (#7); letting go without forgetting, and the caretaking bind ("we first must learn how to treat the otherness within ourselves right," #8); recognition and clearing in one couplet ("a crescendo clearing the mist / a recognition therewith," #10); writing from the now, and flowers from manure (#11); the return as practice, in a litany — "even leaving, beginning again; even older, beginning again; even crying, beginning again" (#12); shared seeing as completion — "we saw it because you saw it and you shared it to me" — with the kettle in the same poem (#12); grief, forgiveness of self, and the looking-glass (#13, #14); and already-there — "the inside is not required to be entered from the outside since I am already there" — with "Please try to see yourself forever as a source of light in this world" (#15). The figures of the book — the tuning fork, the orchard, the gold thread, the house with no ceiling — appear nowhere in the collection: the figures are seed-era, and what the poems hold is the layer beneath the figures.

## A tested negative: the collection holds no weaving vocabulary

The figures of the book — the tuning fork, the orchard, the gold thread, the house with no ceiling — appear nowhere in the collection, and for one figure the absence was tested rather than observed. The gold thread's provenance note keeps an open limit: whether the rainbow-to-gold turn was first made with the friend whose image it was, in a poem, or in the seed's off-record composition. Searched with word boundaries across all sixteen poems, *thread*, *needle*, *sew*, *weave*, *stitch*, *loom*, *cloth*, *gold*, *golden* and *rainbow* return one hit — "i want to stay in the thread" (poems #16), a message thread, in the one poem that postdates the book. The poems branch of that limit closes; the finding is carried at [[the gold thread is the rainbow thread re-owned]].
