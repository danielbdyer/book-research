# reading — al-qushayri, the risala on state and station (knysh 2007)

**What was read.** Abū ʾl-Qāsim al-Qushayrī, *Al-Qushayrī's Epistle on Sufism (Al-Risāla al-qushayriyya fī ʿilm al-taṣawwuf)*, translated by Alexander D. Knysh, reviewed by Muhammad Eissa (Garnet Publishing / The Center for Muslim Contribution to Civilization, Reading, 2007; ISBN 978-1-85964-185-9), read whole on 2026-09-02 as the practice-model program's reading A8. The sections the findings rest on are Chapter 2, "An explanation of the expressions used by this [Sufi] community" (pp. 75–110), the chapter on satisfaction (*riḍā*) inside Chapter 3 (pp. 205–207), and the concluding "Spiritual advice for Sufi novices" (pp. 403–416). Printed page numbers were recovered from the running feet, and two independently hosted scans place the key sentence on the same printed page, so every citation below is **Grade A**. The Arabic of the formula, فالأحوال مواهب والمقامات مكاسب, is attested in Arabic-language treatments of the *Risāla* and is held at **Grade B** (verbatim, no printed page verified here); the rhymed couplet often quoted beside it also circulates attributed to al-Jurjānī's later *Taʿrīfāt*, so the vault cites Knysh and not the couplet.

**How it is cited.** `Knysh 2007, p. 78` — by translator and printed page, with the Arabic term in parentheses where the sentence turns on it. One correction to the brief this reading was given: von Schlegell's *Principles of Sufism* (1990) translates Chapter 3 alone, the forty-three station chapters, and does not contain the terminology chapter where *ḥāl* and *maqām* are defined; Knysh is the right text, and Rabia Harris's *Sufi Book of Spiritual Ascent* would be the alternative.

**Why this reading.** Two notes rested on the mesh grid's characterization of the distinction — the chair [[al-qushayri]] and the isomorph [[state and station were distinguished a millennium before the lattice named them]] — both `privated` on exactly that, and the practice-model survey had leaned on the distinction a third time, to sort the author's own reflection: his event as a visited state, "a level above" as station language. The reading was set three questions that could fail: whether the text makes the distinction as a diagnostic a practitioner can run or only as a taxonomy a shaykh applies; whether a state can become a station; and whether the unbidden/earned language is the text's own.

## What the reading settled

1. **The formula is verbatim and promotable to Grade A.** "States are [divine] gifts, whereas stations are earnings. States come without asking, whereas stations are acquired by the sweat of one's brow. The possessor of a station is firmly established in it, whereas the possessor of a state can be taken up out of his state [at any moment]" (p. 78). "Sweat of one's brow" is Knysh's own rendering, not the vault's paraphrase. The definitions sit beside it: the station is "the good manners which the servant of God realizes after he has entered it. He can arrive at it by means of his own actions, by fulfilling its requirements through [concerted] effort and self-imposed strictures in worshiping [God]" (p. 77); the state is "something that descends upon the hearts [of the mystics] regardless of their intentions, their [attempts to] attract it, or their [desire to] earn it. This can be [the states of] joy, grief, expansion, contraction, passionate longing, vexation, awe or need" (p. 78). The station is not purely earned either: "one cannot enter a station unless one witnesses God – Most High – placing him into that station" (p. 77; Knysh's note 14 reads the progress as "both the wayfarer's personal effort and God's continual assistance").

2. **The distinction is taught to the aspirant as a first-person tool, and the shaykh is required for formation rather than for reading a state.** Chapter 2 names its addressee — "By this explanation we intend to facilitate the understanding of the meaning of these terms by those who want to follow their [Sufi] path and their custom" (p. 75) — and both definitions are cast as tests on the experiencer's own intention, duration, and tenure. Where the text stages the exact question, the disciple's inner criterion wins and the master concedes: "'If I find my heart satisfied with God Most High, I learn that He too is satisfied with me!' The master said: 'You have spoken well, young man!'" (p. 207). The person in a state is called protected, not deceived: "In his state of drunkenness he is protected by God without applying his own effort, while in his state of sobriety he protects himself [from error] through his own actions" (p. 94). The shaykh requirement is real and is about formation: "the novice must be educated in good manners by a [Sufi] master. If he does not have one, he will never succeed. Abu Yazid said: 'He who does not have a master, Satan is his leader'" (p. 405); "The tree that has grown on its own without a gardener will bring forth foliage, but will never bring any fruit" (al-Daqqāq, p. 406). The capacity to discern is forfeited by conduct rather than by lacking a master: "if a man partakes of a forbidden food, he is no longer able to discern between angelic inspiration and devilish whispering" (p. 106). So the vault's recorded refusal — that a person in a state is the worst-placed judge of it, which is why a shaykh is required — is not in the text; the refusal that is in the text is of self-formation, not self-reading.

3. **A state does not become a station; it becomes stability, and the one item he traces across the boundary travels the other way.** He reports and resolves the disputed case of *riḍā*: the Khurasanians call it a station, the Iraqis a state, and "One can combine these two teachings and say: 'At the beginning [of the Sufi path], the servant acquires satisfaction, so it is a station, while in the end it becomes a state, and, therefore, can no longer be acquired'" (p. 206) — station first, state after. On duration he sides against the lightning maxim: "Those who say that the states perdure are correct. A certain experience may become a man's personal portion in which he will grow" (p. 79), and the name for a state that stays is *tamkīn*, stability — "once he has arrived, he becomes stable" (p. 100), "continually becoming stable in a state that is higher than the one he has occupied before" (p. 102).

4. **A single ordered path, not two independent axes.** States are ranked and gated on prior progress — contraction and expansion "come after the servant has advanced above the state of fear and hope" (p. 79) — and a state is ranked against a station on one scale: "expansion is above the station of hope" (pp. 81–82). Access to a state is conditioned on stations fulfilled ("they attain the drinking [of true meanings] through fulfilling the requirements of their spiritual stations," p. 95; "drunkenness is experienced only by those wont to experience ecstatic states," p. 93), and *riḍā* appears both inside the chapter titled "the stations" and "among the states" (p. 79), which is possible between two segments of one road and not between two orthogonal axes. Knysh's glossary reads it the same way: "*Hal*: spiritual state which the mystic experiences on his way to God" (p. 419). What survives intact is the non-transfer rule the book uses as its bypass guard — a state does not establish the person who receives it — and the maxim behind it: "The spiritual states are like lightning. When they last, this is nothing but self-deception and the prompting of one's lower nature" (Abū ʾl-Ḥasan b. al-Ṣāʾigh, p. 59; the anonymous form at p. 78).

5. **The gift is not unconditioned by the labour.** "Divine visitations (wāridāt) come from prayers (awrād). If one does not engage in the prayers on the outside, one will have no divine visitation inside" (p. 84). *Unearned* is true of the individual visitation and false of the standing capacity to receive one, which is nearly the inverse of the modern claim that any state can occur at any structure — a reason to be careful about recruiting him as the millennium-earlier witness to that claim rather than to the narrower rule.

6. **The author's "a level above" sorts as a high state, not a station.** The recognition he describes — "always already there, but just now opened-with" — meets the definition of a *ḥāl* word for word (p. 78). But "above" in this text names the altitude of a visitation, not its tenure: awe is above contraction, contraction above fear, and "expansion is above the station of hope" (p. 81). What makes something a *maqām* is that it is held and paid for, not that it is high. So the phrase, read through the *Risāla*, describes a visitation of high rank rather than a standing, and reading it as a claim about the author's standing would be the category error the text itself warns against. This is a finding about the idiom; whether the author means a station or a relation is his sounding (B2 in `ops/practice-model ecology — candidate next steps.md`).

## Corrections to the vault's wording

| The vault's line | The corrected line |
|---|---|
| "A person can be visited by an exalted state while occupying a low station, and the visitation changes nothing about where they stand" ([[al-qushayri]]) | Second clause his (p. 78); first clause contradicted (pp. 79, 95). Replace with: a state does not establish the person who receives it. |
| "A person may be visited by any state at any time" (the isomorph note) | Replace with: a state may be visited on a person who has built nothing that holds it. |
| "The two are not points on one scale" | Reverse: "expansion is above the station of hope" (p. 81). |
| "the same structure as the modern claim that states and structures are independent axes" | Narrow: the same non-transfer rule, a different geometry — transient visitations along a single ordered path. |
| "a person in a state is the worst-placed judge of what it means, which is why a shaykh is required" | Not in the text. He would refuse self-formation, not self-reading (pp. 75, 207, 405–406). |
| "Read the Risala's chapter on ahwal and maqamat" | There is no such chapter; the definitions are two adjacent entries in the terminology chapter (pp. 77–78), with the substance distributed across pp. 75–110, 100–102, and 206. |
| "the stations are moral and devotional achievements (repentance, patience, trust, contentment) rather than developmental capacities" | Confirmed by Chapter 3's contents (pp. 111–338), with the caveat that the chapter titled "the stations" contains items he elsewhere calls states. |

## What the reading did not settle

- **Whether the corpus's own use of *ahwal* and *maqamat* was drawn from a source or arrived at second-hand** — the third question the chair's evaluation left open. It is a question about the corpus, not the *Risāla*, and the reading cannot answer it; it decides whether this is an arrival the book found or one it inherited.
- **The Arabic** stays at Grade B until a printed Arabic page is in hand.
- **Al-Junayd's *Rasāʾil*** were not part of this reading; the earlier arc reading (`archive/reading — al-junayd and al-qushayri on fana and baqa.md`) stands as it was.
