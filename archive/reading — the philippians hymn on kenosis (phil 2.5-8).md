# reading — the philippians hymn on kenosis (phil 2:5-8)

**What was read.** The Christ-hymn of Philippians 2:5–8, in the public-domain King James Version and the English Standard Version in parallel, with the Greek of verse 7 confirmed at the interlinear (Biblehub, retrieved 2026-08-10). The verb *kenōsis* is a noun formed from the hymn's verb *ekenōsen*; the hymn itself is the primary text, read whole for these four verses. This addresses reading-queue tier 1b, entry 46.

**How it is cited.** `Philippians 2:5–8`, by verse, and `Philippians 2:7 (ekenōsen)` for the verb. Quotations below are the KJV and ESV as marked; the Greek phrase is given from the interlinear.

**Why this reading.** [[christian kenosis makes room by self-emptying rather than by addition]] holds that the Pauline *kenōsis* reaches the make-room-by-withdrawal shape from a first-century source — the divine empties itself out, self-emptying is the shape of love and incarnation — and that it belongs to the withdrawal family that splits along the book's householder-versus-renunciation fork, leaning to the renunciation side. The failable question: does the hymn make self-emptying (*ekenōsen*) the shape of the movement, by subtraction rather than addition, and on which side of the fork does it fall?

## What the reading settled

1. **The self-emptying verb — confirmed at the Greek.** Philippians 2:7 reads ἑαυτὸν ἐκένωσεν, "himself emptied," rendered "emptied himself" (ESV) and "made himself of no reputation" (KJV). Strong's glosses *kenoō* as "to make empty, i.e. to abase." The movement is a pouring-out, not an accretion: the one "in the form of God" (2:6) reaches servant-form and death by subtracting status, "took upon him the form of a servant" (KJV 2:7), "he humbled himself, and became obedient unto death, even the death of the cross" (KJV 2:8). So the make-room-by-withdrawal shape is confirmed at the text, in the direction the note claims — room and love made by emptying rather than by adding.

2. **The refusal holds, and it is Christological.** The emptying is God's act in the incarnation — "who, though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped" (ESV 2:6) — a cosmic and salvific descent, not a psychological technique the book's register can assume. Its dominant reading is self-renunciation and condescension, which pulls it toward Weil's decreation and the renunciation pole rather than toward the book's restored first person. So it stays company for the container that withdraws and adversary-adjacent on the self.

3. **One refinement the reading forces — the hymn is offered as a pattern to imitate.** Verse 5 frames the hymn imperatively: "Let this mind be in you, which was also in Christ Jesus" (KJV), "Have this mind among yourselves, which is yours in Christ Jesus" (ESV). So the note's "cosmic and salvific event rather than a psychological practice" is too clean: the hymn is Christological *and* held up as an ethical pattern the community is to reproduce. But the pattern is downward — self-emptying, humbling, obedience — so it still leans to the renunciation pole and does not reach the restored first person; the refinement sharpens the fork rather than crossing it.

**Verdict for the note.** *Kenōsis* is confirmed as the Christian member of the dissolution family the book names beside *anatta* and *fanā*: the make-room-by-self-emptying shape reached from a first-century source, by subtraction. The note moves from privated to full, with the verse-5 refinement folded in — the hymn is offered as a pattern to imitate, but a downward pattern that keeps it on the renunciation side of the book's fork.

## What the reading did not settle

- **The Greek beyond the one verb.** Read the four verses in two English translations with the verb 2:7 confirmed at the interlinear, not the whole hymn in Greek; the disputed exegesis of *harpagmos* ("a thing to be grasped," 2:6) and whether the emptying is of divine attributes or of their exploitation was not adjudicated and does not bear on the note's claim.
- **Whether the imitable pattern reopens the seat.** Verse 5 offers the emptying as a pattern for the community, which is nearer the book's practical register than a purely Christological event would be. Whether that nearness makes *kenōsis* more than adversary-adjacent — a limited company for a downward move the book also makes before its return — is a question the tzimtzum reading and a fuller treatment of the withdrawal fork would test.
