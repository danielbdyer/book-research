# indirect communication is required only where the object is a capacity to enact rather than a fact to know

Indirection is not a stylistic preference but is dictated by its object. Kierkegaard's 1847 lectures, *The Dialectic of Ethical and Ethical-Religious Communication*, divide communication of knowledge — a transferable objective content that passes intact at second hand — from communication of capability, an ethical or ethical-religious matter in which the receiver already holds the capacity and the task is to actualize it rather than to learn a new fact. Only the second requires indirection: a capability cannot be handed over, so the communicator can do no more than occasion its exercise. This gives the book's whole indirect method its reason, since the book's object is a capacity the reader enacts rather than a proposition the reader assents to, and a direct statement would deliver the words while missing the thing. The distinction is Kierkegaard's coinage; the finding rests on secondary sources, the 1847 lectures being unopened here and queued in `ops/reading queue.md`.

## Substantiation

- Dig 1 records the split as knowledge (*Viden*, direct, transferable) versus capability (*Kunnen*, actualized only in the receiver's own doing), and states the operative asymmetry: "you cannot hand someone a capability; you can only occasion its exercise" (transmission dig 1, 2026-08-13, F2).
- The dig's sources are the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy and "Kierkegaard and Dialogue: The Communication of Capability" [S]. The 1847 lectures survive in the *Journals and Papers* and are named in dig 1's readings-to-queue.
