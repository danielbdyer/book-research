# the practice forms specify when to begin and not how to tell one is finished

The executed draft gathers twelve forms — which it calls kata, after the martial-arts term for a pattern practised until it is no longer practice — and specifies each the same way: a name, the condition that calls for it, and the gesture. Dropping the Anchor returns attention to the body when the somatic loop is operating in chaos; Scanning the Horizon re-orients when dissociation has thinned reality; the Dead Stop ceases forward motion when the system is overspeeded. Bowing to the Gatekeeper meets a protective part and asks what it is protecting; Stripping the Story sets the narrative aside; Naming the Blood locates the felt sense and names it without interpretation. Wiping the Mirror separates the person present from the person being reacted to; Looping to Zero returns to the basic posture mid-conversation; Calling the Room names what is alive between the people in it. Cutting the String severs a tie to the past that has become a leash; the Backward Glance honours the arc without dwelling; the Vertical Drop goes beneath the surface of the present moment (abridged ch14).

What the specification does not supply is a test. For most of the twelve the draft asserts an effect rather than naming a signal — the Anchor "returns you to the ground," the Scan "re-orients you," the Glance "integrates without trapping" — and three are exceptions that show what the rest are missing: the protective part, met with respect, "will usually relax"; "the naming itself shifts the felt sense"; and after Looping to Zero, "the other person will feel the difference." Those three name something a practitioner could check while doing it. The other nine name what the form is for.

The curriculum states its own endpoint and leaves it equally unmarked. "The point of the Kata is not the Kata. The point of the Kata is the Clearing they help maintain. When the Clearing is steady, you do not need the Kata explicitly… The Kata become invisible" (abridged ch14). The forms are declared to be for outgrowing; nothing states how a practitioner would know it had happened, or distinguishes a form that has become invisible through mastery from one that has been quietly dropped.

## Substantiation

- Abridged ch14 is the only source that gives the twelve with their instructions. The treatment lists the same twelve by name, and its most useful addition is a genre rather than a gloss: they are "generically, a dhāraṇā collection — twelve curated doorways from the same genre as the 112," which places them in a documented Kashmir Śaiva form rather than in a coinage. It also records one form's provenance: "Naming the Blood is mātṛkā-work verbatim and McLaren's practice in miniature" (arc-gloss: ch14).
- The treatment records the manuscript decision that produces the gap, as a preserved build-note: "this chapter has the most room to expand; full technical instruction belongs to the companion volume — the book keeps the vocabulary, the companion keeps the drills" (arc-gloss: ch14). The missing tests are therefore deferred rather than overlooked, and the deferral is to a volume that does not exist.
- The corpus places one further practice beneath all twelve which the executed draft's list does not contain: McLaren's channeling, "the third way between repression and venting" (atlas §VII.3).
- The survey's version of this finding is only half right against the executed draft, and the half it gets right is the useful one. It reports that "the book's twelve kata currently teach shu" and that a curriculum should tell its student "that the forms are for outgrowing, and roughly when," citing the Japanese arts' three stages — shu, keep the form; ha, break it; ri, leave it (space-around: around the practice). The draft does state that the forms are for outgrowing. It supplies no roughly-when, and the survey's connecting observation stands: a form maintained past ripeness is the practice section's version of the corpus's own rule against indefinite preservation.
