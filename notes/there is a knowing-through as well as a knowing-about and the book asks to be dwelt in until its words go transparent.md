# there is a knowing-through as well as a knowing-about and the book asks to be dwelt in until its words go transparent

The reader gains a second way of holding the book — not as an object of study whose propositions they collect and can recite, but as a lens they look *through* until the terrain itself appears. Once a tool is indwelt, attention runs from it to the world, not to it; the words stop being things examined and become the means of seeing. What the reader gains is the graduation from studying the material to living from it — the moment the sentences become transparent, the reader is no longer reading about their life but seeing their life through what the reading gave them, which is the only way the book could ever be true rather than merely learned. Its use to the writing is a criterion for the book's own success that is not comprehension but transparency.

## Substantiation

- William James (The Principles of Psychology, 1890): the distinction between "knowledge of acquaintance" and "knowledge-about" — "I know the color blue when I see it, and the flavor of a pear when I taste it… but about the inner nature of these facts I can say nothing at all." Acquaintance is had *through* contact, not accumulated *about* a thing. Polanyi (The Tacit Dimension; Personal Knowledge, 1958): "indwelling" — we interiorize the particulars and attend *from* them *to* the focal whole; the word, once dwelt in, becomes an extension of the knower, no longer noticed in itself.
- Shadow, kept strictly as shadow: Polanyi's pianist who turns her focal attention onto her own fingers falters — attending *to* what should be dwelt *in* can dissolve the performance. Stated affirmatively, the gain is the transparency, the deadening of over-analysis only the far edge of that gift.
