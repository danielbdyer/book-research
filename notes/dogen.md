# dogen

Dōgen Kigen (1200–1253), the founder of Japanese Sōtō Zen, holds that practice and realization are not two things but one — *shushō-ittō*. Zazen is not a technique performed in order to become enlightened later; it is the enactment, now, of a realization already present. The book states its anti-instrumentality clause — a practice expresses a realization rather than causing it, so an exercise is not a means to somewhere ([[the practice is the expression of realization rather than a means to it]]) — in Dōgen's voice, and a reading of his tradition's authoritative gloss on *Bendōwa* now grounds the clause at its source ([[dogen holds that the practice is the realization enacted rather than its means]]).

## Why this seat

He is seated as a **reference-lineage** thinker, beside David Loy as the roster's other Buddhist voice, and not as a source-tradition figure: the book's own descent is Trika, and Dōgen is the lineage the book reaches to for one specific doctrine rather than the tradition it comes from. The seat carries a **thesis-bearing** load because the clause is stated in his voice and, until this round, rested on him alone — structurally the single-bearer situation the verification standard was in with Gendlin (the verification standard rests on one name and cannot be defended by company). The load assignment is candidate 29's proposal in `ops/queue/claim candidates.md`, and the author has now made the seating.

What has changed the single-bearer exposure is the reading round itself. The clause now anchors a three-tradition convergence, all three read at the text: Dōgen for the contemplative arrival, Deci and Ryan's undermining effect for the empirical arrival ([[deci and ryan measured that instrumentalizing an intrinsic activity corrupts it]]), and Aristotle's *energeia* for the philosophical arrival ([[aristotle distinguishes activity complete in itself from movement toward an outside end]]). So Dōgen bears the clause as its stated voice while the other two give it independent company — the thesis-bearing load is no longer a single point of failure.

## What he would refuse

Read at an authoritative source quoting *Bendōwa*, not the full text; the refusal is bounded by that reading. The note first read Dōgen's *hongaku* (original enlightenment) as cutting against the book, but his relation to original-enlightenment thought is contested inside his own tradition — the critical-Buddhism reading (Hakamaya) holds that he criticized it. The genuine remaining tension is narrower: Dōgen's zazen is practice "without the goal of becoming a buddha," while the book promises transformation and stages an arc toward a peak, so the fork is goallessness against a promised destination. His own lineage sharpens the same edge, since Zen's *makyō* instruction refuses even a genuine experience as self-certifying ([[zen's makyo instruction refuses the self-certifying experience]]).

He guards the flank the book most needs guarded, which is why the company is worth more than the tension costs. Dōgen "was deeply concerned that the teaching of 'the self is originally buddha' might lead people to disregard practice," and held that "we practice precisely because we are already buddha." Already-there-ness requiring practice rather than cancelling it is the book's own posture — reading is remembering, and still a practice — so he meets the book on the point it most needs company for.

## What a fuller reading would settle

A complete translation of *Bendōwa* would confirm the surrounding argument, and the *Genjōkōan* would test whether the goallessness is as flat against a promised arc as the tension states or is itself completed by a return the book would recognize. The seating is the author's and is made; the reading that would deepen it is queued.
