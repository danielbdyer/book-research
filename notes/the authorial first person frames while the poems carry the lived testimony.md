# the authorial first person frames while the poems carry the lived testimony

Every first-person-singular sentence in the executed draft falls into one of three kinds, and those three kinds account for all of them with nothing left over. The first kind frames an act the author is performing on the page — offering, pointing, saying — as in "I want to offer you an image now" (abridged ch1) and "There is one last thing I want to say" (epilogue); the draft contains ten of these. The second kind puts the first person in the reader's mouth, so that the I belongs to a reader speaking a recognition rather than to the author speaking about himself, and the draft contains seven of these ([[the executed draft scripts the reader's recognition in the reader's own first person]] records that device on its own). The third kind is the author's own lived testimony, and the draft confines it almost entirely to the three poems it sets inside the prose — "I came down from the mountain carrying fire in my open hand. It did not burn" (the interlude after ch12) — with exactly two exceptions in prose: "There is a phrase that came to me once, years ago" (ch2) and "I have known people for whom a single profound experience marked the beginning of a decade-long process" (ch12).

This distribution is evidence in a disagreement the vault records and does not settle. The three drafts speak in three different grammatical persons, and the abridged edition's version of that choice is now specified down to the sentence: its prose first person introduces, offers, and points, and the account of what the author has actually lived is carried by the poems rather than by the prose around them.

## Substantiation

- Every first-person-singular instance in the extracted draft was enumerated and sorted, giving ten framings, seven sentences spoken for the reader, and two prose testimonies alongside the poems; the specimens above are quoted from that extraction.
- Anyone can check this by classifying each first-person-singular sentence as a framing act (offering, pointing, giving, saying), as speech written for the reader, or as the author's lived testimony. Testimony outside the poems amounting to more than the two named sentences would break the claim.
- The disagreement this evidence bears on is that the mythic edition speaks as a plural we, the abridged edition as a singular I, and the treatment in neither person, with the corpus naming the middle voice without settling which person carries it ([[the drafts disagree about which person the book speaks in]]). Settling it stays the author's to do.
