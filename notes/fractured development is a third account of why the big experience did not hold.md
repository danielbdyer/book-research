# fractured development is a third account of why the big experience did not hold

The book's reader had the big experience and it did not hold, and the vault holds two mechanisms for why, side by side: the state was interpreted by a structure it did not change ([[an enormous experience is interpreted by the structure it did not change]]), or the window of plasticity it opened was spent on whatever the person was already doing ([[the plasticity window reorganizes only what attention is on]]). Wilber's own texts, read 2026-09-02, supply a third that neither of those states. Development runs in lines — "the numerous developmental lines are to some degree independent modules, and these modules can and do develop in relatively independent ways (but not totally independently)" ("Waves, Streams, States, and Self," p. 4) — and a person can be far along in one and nowhere in another. His name for the case is the stone Buddha: "The 'stone Buddha' phenomenon — where a person can stay in extraordinary states of formless absorption for extended periods — and yet be poorly developed, or even pathologically developed, in other lines and realms, is an extremely common phenomenon, and it happens largely because integral development has not been engaged, let alone completed" (p. 10). The failure is located somewhere different again: not at the experience, not in the days after it, but in the parts of a life that were never developed at all, which the experience did not reach because there was nothing there for it to reach.

The account bears on the book's engine rather than on its arc. The engine's question is "which cylinder is misfiring?", and Wilber's psychograph exists to ask a structurally similar question of a person — "we need to be aware of our strengths… as well as our weaknesses (where we do poorly or even pathologically)" — with a warning close to the book's own: "More than one leader, spiritual teacher, or politician has spectacularly crashed through lack of an understanding of these simple realities" (*AQAL Journal* 1(1), 2006, pp. 9, 11). What he adds that the book does not say is the institutional half: "what usually happens is that development is partial or fractured, and this fractured development is taken as the paradigm of natural and normal spiritual development, and then students and teachers alike are asked to repeat the fracture as evidence of their spiritual progress" ("Waves," p. 11). A reader whose breakthrough did not hold may have been taught, by the culture that gave them the breakthrough, that the fracture was the attainment.

## Substantiation

- The three accounts are held beside one another and not merged, under the same rule that keeps the first two apart: choosing among them would settle by preference a question the author has not been asked. They differ on where the failure sits — at the experience (an unchanged interpreting structure), after it (a window spent), or beside it (lines never developed) — and each implies a different thing to do next.
- The precedent is for the question, not the ideal. Wilber's lines are not the book's cylinders and he denies the ideal the title names ([[the title's idiom names the integration of the three loops rather than parallel lines of development]]); what carries over is the diagnostic move of asking a person which capacity is undeveloped rather than how high the experience went.
- The same finding arrives from the state-and-station side: al-Qushayrī holds that the question to put to a visitation is not its height but whether anything was built that could hold it (Knysh 2007, pp. 78–79; [[al-qushayri]]). The stone Buddha is that question asked of the lines rather than of the stations.
