# weil is seated as the opponent the plumbing deserves

The book's commitment to ordinary life — the corpus's name for it is "transcendence with plumbing," the Householder Axiom recorded at [[the tuesday is the temple]] — has a genuine opponent, and the book seats her inside itself. Simone Weil's position (decreation, attention as the purest generosity, affliction that cannot be transformed but only borne) runs directly against the householder commitment, and she is seated in the reference lineage at full strength. The atlas states the logic at §XI.2g: the axiom is "kept honest by its worthiest adversary," and §IX.8d records the seating.

The tension is deliberately unresolved. Weil also does second duty among the corpus's expansions of the five theories of where suffering lives (atlas §IX.5), where she represents suffering that is borne rather than dissolved. The classical form of that counterweight — Aristotle's distinction between privation (a missing ingredient) and affliction (a presence that must be borne) — was evaluated 2026-08-06 and holds an evaluation note at [[aristotle on privation]], which states the affliction objection as the inheritance the book takes on with the move; the primary text remains unread.

## Substantiation

- Atlas §XI.2g records the tension; §IX.8d records Weil's seat and her double role.
- First-telling, "The honesty," discloses the arrangement to the reader: the book keeps its strongest opponent in-house.
- Walked IX carries her position summary and the expansion of the five-theory map to include positions holding that some suffering must be borne.
- **The seated opposition now carries decreation's confirmed mechanism, added 2026-08-10.** The seat above names Weil's decreation without stating what it is. A reading of *Gravity and Grace* (the reading record is `archive/reading — weil, gravity and grace on decreation (1947).md`, and the finding is at [[weil's decreation un-creates the self and reaches the opposite of the restored first person]]) supplies the concept at the text. Weil defines it in "Decreation" as "to make something created pass into the uncreated," marked off from destruction as "a blameworthy substitute for decreation," and grounds it in "Renunciation" as "Imitation of God's renunciation in creation… God renounces being everything. We should renounce being something." The mechanism sharpens the opposition rather than softening it: the "I" is un-created permanently and the room is made for God, the exact opposite of the book's first person restored on the last page, so the adversary the book keeps in-house now holds a precise, quotable position rather than a summary. The treatment under trial is unchanged — the reading arms the seat and does not resolve the tension, which is the author's act — and the once-flagged formulation "We participate in the creation of the world by decreating ourselves" was confirmed at two independent sources and is usable with its translation provenance (Crawford, 1963).
