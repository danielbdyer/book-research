# a large change in evaluation can happen without being experienced as change

Nisbett and Wilson devote a titled section to change-detection, and its finding reaches the book's verification standard where the standard is narrowest. The section opens on the distinction the narrowed standard rests on: "There is an important difference between awareness of the existence of an evaluation or motive state and awareness of a changed evaluation or motive state" (1977, p. 235). It then reports two studies.

Bem and McConnell (1970) measured attitudes, changed them by an experimental manipulation, and asked subjects a week later what their attitude had been before. Control subjects recalled their earlier attitudes accurately. The subjects whose attitudes had moved did not: "they reported that their current attitudes were the same as their premanipulation attitudes. Thus subjects apparently changed their attitudes in the absence of any subjective experience of change" (p. 236). Goethals and Reckman (1973) took high school students with measured opinions on school busing and put each into a discussion with a confederate arguing the opposite side. Most of the pro-busing students converted, and when asked to recall their original opinions they recalled having been more anti-busing than the students who had actually begun anti-busing recalled being. The original report is quoted: "No subject reported that the discussion had had any effect in changing or modifying his position" (p. 237). The paper's summary of both: "even when they can report on such states, they may not report that a change has taken place in these states" (p. 237).

The consequence for the book is specific and it was not visible before this reading. Gendlin's narrowing had looked like a retreat to safer ground, because noticing change is a weaker demand than describing an inner state accurately. This section says that noticing change is a demand with its own documented failure, and that the failure is invisible from inside — the students did not feel uncertain about having changed, they felt certain they had not.

One boundary is part of the claim rather than a defense of it. Both studies measure a person's memory of a prior *attitude* across one to two weeks, and both failures run through that memory: the subject reconstructs the earlier state from the present one. A felt shift is a comparison of a present bodily state against one held minutes earlier, and the paper does not test that case. Whether the finding transfers across that difference is open, and it is the question a defense of the standard now has to answer.

- The section runs from page 235 to page 237 under the heading "Awareness of the Existence of a Change Process," in the paper read complete on 2026-08-07; the reading record is `archive/reading — nisbett and wilson, telling more than we can know (1977).md`.
- The vault's characterization before this reading, made at web-citation depth on 2026-08-06 and recorded at introspective access is the felt shift's undefended premise, held that the paper's verified core concerns reports about the causes of behavior rather than reports of current experience. That characterization missed this section, and no summary of the paper the vault consulted mentioned it.
- The exemption the same paper grants — current sensations count among the private facts a person knows with near certainty — is stated at the confabulation finding exempts current sensations and targets reports of causes. The two claims are the two halves of one reading and neither cancels the other: the paper protects the report that a sensation is present and attacks the report that it is different from before.
- The paper offers one further reason a felt shift cannot certify what produced it: "we are often capable of describing intermediate results of a series of mental operations in such a way as to promote the feeling that we are describing the operations themselves" (p. 255).
