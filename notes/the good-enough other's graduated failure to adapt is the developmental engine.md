# the good-enough other's graduated failure to adapt is the developmental engine

Winnicott's good-enough other is not the one who succeeds completely but the one who fails in the right way at the right rate. Such an other adapts almost wholly to the child at first, and then, in tune with the child's growing capacity to tolerate the gap, fails to adapt in a graduated way. The dig records that this graduated, well-timed failure is the developmental engine: the child's own capacity is built in the space the withdrawal opens, so growth depends on an other who must, virtuously, withdraw rather than on one who keeps meeting every need.

The finding gives a developmental grammar to a move the book already commits to on other grounds. The corpus holds that the facilitator succeeds by becoming less necessary and that the book can only be auxiliary to a sovereign reader; Winnicott's graduated failure names the mechanism by which a withdrawal is developmental rather than abandoning — it must be adapted-to first, and it must be timed to the reader's growing capacity. A book that withdraws too early, or without having first adapted, is not good-enough but failing; a book that never withdraws forecloses the capacity it means to build.
