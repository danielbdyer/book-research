# a bounded sheltering intimacy is the condition under which reverie and the inner image come forward

The reader gains the condition their imagination actually runs on. The images the book trades in do not arrive in open, exposed, instrumental space; they come in sheltered, bounded, intimate places — the small room, the corner, the enclosure that protects the dreamer. What you gain by making a small enclosed space is not merely comfort but the very condition of reverie: build the walls and the images come to inhabit them. Its use to the writing is to ground the book's central architectural choice — a house — in a phenomenology of why bounded intimacy, and not open space, is where the inner image can appear.

## Substantiation

- Bachelard, The Poetics of Space (1958): "the house shelters daydreaming, the house protects the dreamer, the house allows one to dream in peace"; his "topoanalysis" studies the felicitous, sheltering spaces — the house, the nest, the shell, the corner — in which reverie and the poetic image emerge. Bounded intimacy is the workshop of the image, and Bachelard supplies the phenomenology of why a house, of all vehicles, is the one that would let a reader's images come.
- Refusal kept: Bachelard studies the felicitous spaces and brackets the hostile ones; the claim is about sheltering intimacy, not enclosure in general, and he would not have it generalized into "all enclosure is good."
- Standing: a reach, nearly strong given the direct fit to the House with No Ceiling; the reverie/shelter passages are queued, and whether to name Bachelard where the house is introduced is the author's call.
