# today's exhaustion is self-generated and leaves no external authority to resist

Byung-Chul Han argues that the society organized by prohibition has given way to one organized by permission. The disciplinary order ran on negativity — a "you must not," an external authority, a prohibiting enemy the subject could resist. The order that replaced it runs on positivity — a limitless "you can," projects and motivation and optimization — and it has no external Other. Han's consequence is that with no outside authority to resist, the violence turns inward and self-generated: the subject becomes, in his phrase, master and slave in one, exploiting itself in the name of its own freedom. Auto-exploitation, he adds, is more efficient than exploitation by another, because the feeling of freedom attends it and the exhausted person volunteers. The disorders that follow — burnout, depression, attention-deficit — are for Han a surplus of positivity rather than an infection from outside.

This portrait specifies who the book's reader is and why the reader is depleted. The book addresses someone whose earlier breakthrough did not hold and who blames the failure on themselves, and Han supplies the structural reason the self-blame feels natural: there is no external enemy on offer, so the only available culprit is the self. It sharpens the book's refusal of "you are broken," because a reader exhausted by their own limitless "you can" does not need one more improvement project, which is exactly what the founding inversion declines to sell ([[the founding inversion restates the author's own sufficiency inheritance]]). The vault had already flagged this material as present and unused: [[the compound diagnoses may relocate a structural condition inside a person]] names "Han on auto-exploitation as structural" among the counterweights "in the room and never pointed at this question," and this note begins to point it.

Han diagnoses without prescribing, which is the limit the book inherits with him. His argument names no institutional lever and no collective agent; his counter-moves — deep boredom, contemplation, the figure he calls "the idiot," inoperativity — are individual and gestural. The book can carry his portrait of the reader's condition and cannot take a program from him, which is the sense in which the dig rates him directional: the diagnosis travels, the remedy does not.

## Substantiation

- The five-step argument — disciplinary negativity, achievement positivity, the absent external Other, the inward self-generated violence, the neuronal surplus — is reconstructed from *The Burnout Society*; the extensions, smart power exploiting freedom itself in *Psychopolitics* and the lost *vita contemplativa* in *The Scent of Time*, are named the same way. All of it is [S per dig 5]: the dig reached Han through scholarship and reviews rather than an opened primary, so the terms attributed to him here — achievement society, auto-exploitation, master and slave in one — are his reported coinages rather than quotations from a reading, and the primary reading is queued in `ops/reading queue.md` (does Han name any institutional or collective lever, or only individual contemplative gestures?).
- The corpus's own use of Han is narrower than this portrait: it cites his burnout society only as the civilizational-scale form of one formula, obligation privated of sanctuary ([[burnout is obligation minus sanctuary]]). The achievement-subject mechanism is the argument beneath that citation, and it is the part the book has not yet drawn on.
