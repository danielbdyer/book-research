# a schedule can be aligned to the nervous system rather than to the clock

The gesture asserts that a working day can be arranged to the body's actual cycles instead of to institutional time, and that misalignment is a structural cause of distress rather than a personal failing. The vault does not hold this as a practice, but it holds most of what would ground it. The temporal loop is the corpus's account of body and time; chronobiology is already a queued candidate; and the survey states the conflict in a sentence. What is missing is only the step from diagnosis to design.

This note is a stub. Its state is `nascent`: the proposition is the gesture's, not the vault's, and it is recorded so the territory is reachable from the graph rather than only from the inventory in `ops/open corpus.md`. The gesture is a named unit of the earliest contents that no document develops; naming a territory is evidence the project has been to its edge, not evidence it belongs in the book.

## What the corpus supplies

- The survey names the research and the conflict in one entry: chronobiology, covering circadian and ultradian rhythm, with the observation that modern working arrangement "wars against ultradian rhythm" (space-around: around the engine). The entry is on the pending-evaluation list in `ops/queue/candidates.md`.
- The corpus's own material is diagnostic. The temporal loop's misfire is treated at atlas §V and carried at [[distress is a question about machinery not a verdict about character]], which routes distress to a loop and stops there.
- Scheduling as a word appears in the walked document three times and in the first telling once, always about the author's working process rather than as a subject.

## What would close it

Closing it means evaluating chronobiology and then deciding whether the book prescribes arrangements or only diagnoses them.
