# the house one stays in is inner life made walkable that the art of memory and Bachelard's poetics each named

This tells the reader that going somewhere in your own mind and coming back changed is not a figure of speech but a real faculty they already own. The book makes a house of its ideas because the shape of an inner life *is* a house one can walk through — rooms you enter, thresholds you cross, a place you can return to and find still standing. The reader who has ever "gone into" a memory, or found a whole feeling waiting in a particular room, is using an art so old it was the West's first technology of remembering. Reading has always been a kind of walking home, and the reader already knows the way. Its use to the writing is to weld the book's vehicle to its foundation in a single stroke: the house is not a delivery device for the remembering doctrine, it *is* the remembering doctrine.

## Substantiation

- On record: poem #2 as a held container entire, "the room seems to breathe with them – a sanctuary," "a mutual respect that sanctifies the boundary they have not crossed" (poems #2); the seed's House with No Ceiling (April 2025); the first-telling's own conceit that "the rooms are the ideas, made walkable" ([[book-structure]]).
- Inference, offered as a candidate: the classical *ars memoriae*, the method of loci (Cicero, De Oratore; the Ad Herennium; recovered by Frances Yates, The Art of Memory, 1966), built houses of rooms precisely so that walking them *was* remembering — the book's own "reading is remembering" turned into architecture; and Bachelard's The Poetics of Space (1958) names the house as the native topography of the intimate self.
- Standing: a reach — strongest on its tradition side (the walkable-rooms-plus-remembering already *is* the memory art), lighter on "he lived it first," since the poems' room reads as sanctuary rather than explicitly as a mnemonic; he confirms whether the house was ever felt as a structure you *remember by*.
