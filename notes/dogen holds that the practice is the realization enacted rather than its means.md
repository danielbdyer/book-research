# dogen holds that the practice is the realization enacted rather than its means

The book's practice curriculum runs under one clause: a practice expresses a realization already present rather than causing it, so an exercise is not a means to somewhere ([[the practice is the expression of realization rather than a means to it]]). The corpus states it in Dōgen's voice, and a reading of his tradition's authoritative gloss now grounds it at the source. *Bendōwa*'s eighth answer holds that practice and realization "are one and the same," and Dōgen inverts the older Zen reading of non-defilement to require "a practice without anticipating realization" — the book's clause, that the doing is not for a somewhere, in its origin. The reading also deepened the convergence and bounded the refusal, and both are below.

## Substantiation

- The book's half is on record, quoted in Dōgen's voice: "practice and realization are one; zazen is not a means to enlightenment but its expression… you do not do the practice to get somewhere; the doing is the somewhere" (walked IX), cross-referenced into the curriculum as a constraint on every exercise (atlas §IX.8d).
- Dōgen's half is now shown, not only named. From his tradition's authoritative gloss, which quotes *Bendōwa*'s eighth answer directly (the Sōtō Zen Text Project's key-terms essay by Seijun Ishii, translated by Issho Fujita; the reading record is `archive/reading — dogen, bendowa on shusho itto (c. 1231).md`): "To suppose that practice and realization are not one is a view of those outside the way; in Buddha Dharma they are one and the same. Because practice within realization occurs at the moment of practice, the practice of beginner's mind is itself the entire original realization." The essay states the anti-instrumentality form precisely — Dōgen "inverts it to mean that it is necessary to have a practice without anticipating realization." Read at an authoritative source quoting the primary rather than the full *Bendōwa*; a complete translation would confirm the surrounding argument.
- The convergence is deeper than a borrowing. Dōgen guards the flank the book guards: he was "deeply concerned that the teaching of 'the self is originally buddha' might lead people to disregard practice," and held that "we practice precisely because we are already buddha" (*Bendōwa*: "it is not actualized without practice, and it is not attained without realization"). Already-there-ness requiring practice rather than cancelling it is the book's own posture — reading is remembering, and still a practice — so Dōgen meets the book on the point it most needs company for.
- The refusal is kept but bounded by the reading. The note first read Dōgen's *hongaku* (original enlightenment) as cutting against the book; the reading shows his relation to *hongaku* is contested inside his own tradition, since the critical-Buddhism reading (Hakamaya) holds that Dōgen criticized original-enlightenment thought and rejected the "practice after perfection" nuance. The genuine remaining tension is narrower: Dōgen's zazen is practice "without the goal of becoming a buddha," while the book promises transformation and stages an arc toward a peak, so the fork is goallessness against a promised destination. The book's own lineage sharpens the same edge, since Zen's makyo instruction refuses even a genuine experience as self-certifying ([[zen's makyo instruction refuses the self-certifying experience]]).
- What stays open is the company, not the source. The clause is grounded in Dōgen, but the three-tradition convergence that would give it independent arrivals is still to be read — Deci and Ryan's undermining effect and Aristotle's *energeia*. The seating of Dōgen stays the author's.
