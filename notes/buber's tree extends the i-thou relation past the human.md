# buber's tree extends the i-thou relation past the human

The book's relational field is modeled as human recognition — Hegel's *Anerkennung*, what two people do for each other — and the vault has established that the field's real ontology is Buber's I-Thou relation ([[the relational vocabulary's ontology is buber's rather than benjamin's]]). Buber's tree passage is the unmined half of that same thinker: contemplating a tree, "if will and grace are joined," he is drawn into a relation and the tree ceases to be an It. The between extends past the human, and a non-human being can be met as Thou. This is the chain-link for the book's non-human blind spot, and it grows the book's *own* relational vocabulary to the more-than-human rather than importing a stranger. The reading is not done, so the claim is privated.

## Substantiation

- The book's half is on record: the field metabolizes what neither person can alone, and its ontology is Buber's I-Thou (walked VIII; [[the field between two people does work]]; [[the relational vocabulary's ontology is buber's rather than benjamin's]]).
- Buber's half is named, not shown: the "I contemplate a tree" passage is early in Part One of *I and Thou*. To verify at the text; not quoted from memory. The seated Buber note is entirely human I-Thou, and the queued Buber reading is aimed at the relational curriculum, not the tree.
- The refusal is kept, and it names what the book would have to build. Buber hedges: he does not claim the tree has a soul or consciousness, and leaves the tree's reciprocity an open question, so the tree is a Thou without being a mind. To admit it, the book needs a category for participation without cognition — a witness that is neither a thinker nor a human recognizer — which is precisely what the poems' two cats already are.
