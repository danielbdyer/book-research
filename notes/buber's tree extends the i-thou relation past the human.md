# buber's tree extends the i-thou relation past the human

The book's relational field is modeled as human recognition — Hegel's *Anerkennung*, what two people do for each other — and the vault has established that the field's real ontology is Buber's I-Thou relation ([[the relational vocabulary's ontology is buber's rather than benjamin's]]). Buber's tree passage is the unmined half of that same thinker: contemplating a tree, "if will and grace are joined," he is drawn into a relation and the tree ceases to be an It. The between extends past the human, and a non-human being can be met as Thou. This is the chain-link for the book's non-human blind spot, and it grows the book's *own* relational vocabulary to the more-than-human rather than importing a stranger. A reading of the tree passage confirms it.

## Substantiation

- The book's half is on record: the field metabolizes what neither person can alone, and its ontology is Buber's I-Thou (walked VIII; [[the field between two people does work]]; [[the relational vocabulary's ontology is buber's rather than benjamin's]]).
- Buber's half is now shown, from Kaufmann's translation via reputable quoting sources (the reading record is `archive/reading — buber, i and thou on the tree (1923).md`): in contemplating the tree, "if will and grace are joined," one is drawn into a relation and "the tree ceases to be an It," and the relation "is mutual." Read via quoting sources, not the full *I and Thou*.
- The refusal is kept, and it is now verbatim. Buber withholds a mind from the tree: "Does the tree then have consciousness, similar to our own? I have no experience of that," and "What I encounter is neither the soul of a tree nor a dryad, but the tree itself." So the tree is a Thou without being a mind, its reciprocity left open. To admit it, the book needs a category for participation without cognition — a witness that is neither a thinker nor a human recognizer — which is precisely what the poems' two cats already are.
