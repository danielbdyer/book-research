# the imperative to increase the number of choices is too empty to guide a facilitation decision

Heinz von Foerster proposed an ethic that fits the book's facilitation stance in shape and fails it in substance. His imperative — "act always so as to increase the number of choices" — reads as a rule for a helper who should widen a person's options rather than narrow them, and it is paired with a claim the book shares, that ethics is enacted rather than stated. As a direction it points the right way: a facilitator who leaves a person with more moves available has done the non-appropriating thing the book asks. But as a decision procedure it is nearly empty, because it cannot rank the choices it commends and gives no way to prefer one door over another — and a tyrant who maximizes his own options while foreclosing everyone else's satisfies it exactly. The imperative can gesture at a facilitation ethic and cannot adjudicate a real facilitation decision.

The verdict is therefore directional and bounded. Von Foerster's rule belongs in the book as an orientation that names the right axis, not as an instrument that could tell a facilitator what to do when two openings compete.
