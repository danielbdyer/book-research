# katz and burke aim the same trained-vocabulary premise at two different claims

Two adversaries seated against different claims turn out to share one premise. Katz, against the verification standard: "a body trained by the book's vocabulary would produce the felt shift in response to the training rather than to anything beyond it." Burke, against the alphabet: trained incapacity is "the state in which a person's very competence with a vocabulary makes them unable to see what the vocabulary does not name" — and his evaluation already flags that the concept reaches the felt-shift region. The shared premise is that a vocabulary constructs its user's perception, and its human case is the book's own defining second reader, whose integration fluency is a trained vocabulary doing exactly this.

The pairing matters because the book holds one answer that serves both at once: the felt sense can refuse a formulation, and a refusal is the event a pure constructivism cannot supply — a trained yes is cheap, but a trained capacity to reject the trainer's own sentence is a different kind of fact ([[the felt sense rejects formulations and that friction is what separates it from fluency]]). Whether that answer survives is what both queued readings jointly test, and testing them as a pair is cheaper than testing them apart.
