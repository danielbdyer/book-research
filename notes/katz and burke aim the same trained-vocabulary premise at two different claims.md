# katz and burke aim the same trained-vocabulary premise at two different claims

Steven Katz and Kenneth Burke were each brought into the book's company as an adversary, against two different claims, and they turn out to share one premise. Katz, arguing against the book's verification standard, holds that "a body trained by the book's vocabulary would produce the felt shift in response to the training rather than to anything beyond it." Burke argues against the book's alphabet of primitives with his concept of trained incapacity, which is "the state in which a person's very competence with a vocabulary makes them unable to see what the vocabulary does not name" — and the evaluation written for him already notes that the concept reaches into the felt-shift material as well. The premise the two share is that a vocabulary constructs the perception of the person trained in it, and it has a human case in the reader the book defines for itself, whose fluency in the vocabulary of integration is exactly such a trained vocabulary.

Pairing the two objections matters because the book holds one answer that meets both at once: a felt sense can refuse a formulation offered to it, and such a refusal is an event that a purely constructivist account cannot supply. A person can be trained to say yes cheaply, but a body trained by the book's own vocabulary rejecting a sentence that same vocabulary hands it is a different kind of fact, and that answer is stated at [[the felt sense rejects formulations and that friction is what separates it from fluency]], which holds that Gendlin's implicit order is precise enough to refuse a wrong word and that fluency alone never pays that cost. Whether the answer survives is what the two readings queued for Katz and Burke jointly test, and reading them against each other costs less than reading them apart.

## Substantiation

- The part of this claim that rests on Katz is the question written when he was reseated on 2026-08-06, and none of his own work has been read here. The part that rests on Burke is his evaluation's account of trained incapacity, taken from works about him rather than from his own. Both standings are recorded on the two thinkers' notes and carried forward here.
- The answer about friction comes from Gendlin's own 1964 paper, read here directly, where he writes that "his direct referent does the judging."
- The two readings sit in the first and third tiers of `ops/reading queue.md`, the list of readings each stated as a question that can fail. What this claim adds is the instruction that the two questions converge, and that the readings should be done against each other.
