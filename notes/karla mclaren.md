# karla mclaren

Karla McLaren is seated for what the corpus calls the address: the position that an emotion is not a state to be managed but a messenger arriving with something to deliver. "Every emotion is a distinct messenger carrying a gift and a question" (arc-gloss: the five rooms), and the corpus records three of the questions — anger asks what must be protected and what must be restored; fear asks what action must be taken; sadness asks what must be released (atlas §IX.2). At her own text, read 2026-09-02, the instrument is a gifts list plus a pair of *internal* questions — her word — and the pairs have changed. Anger asked "What must be protected? What must be restored?" in 2010 and asks "What do I value? and What must be protected and restored?" since the 2023 revision; fear's single question gained "What am I sensing?"; sadness has two questions in both editions, "What must be released? What must be rejuvenated?", with her instruction "Remember to ask both questions; sadness is not just about loss" — so the corpus keeps exactly the half she warns against keeping. Grief is a distinct emotion in her scheme rather than a grade of sadness, "grief arises when you have no choice about letting go," and asks "What must be mourned? How do I honor what was lost?"

The finding the corpus credits her with is its hardest one: grief is the single emotion that cannot complete in solitude, because it requires a witness and a ceremony structurally rather than preferentially — the grief formula "does not complete in a sealed flask — not as a moral claim, as chemistry" (arc-gloss: ch6), and "McLaren says it as grief's non-negotiable ceremony" (arc-gloss: overlay four). Her own sentence is the other way: "Your grief process can be undertaken individually, or in a community or family group." The solitary route is given as sufficient and the body as its agent — "Your body is a brilliant mourner, and if you trust it, it will convey you into the river of tears and bring you back out safe again." What she requires is ritual, a container and not a person: "create a shrine for the dead (or the loss) so that you can create a container for your mourning," something disposable in it so the ritual can be ended, "a secluded physical space where you can work through your grief in your own time," and community as an option inside the ritual, "Inviting community into your grief ritual… If you include others, let them place their own items in the grief shrine as well." Ceremony yes, witness no. "Another nervous system present while the thing moves through" and the sealed flask are the book's doctrine, not hers; her relational claim is about a grief-impaired culture — "When we see someone actively dropping into grief and mourning, we often turn away… And that's the real death – of compassion, of community, of feeling, and of understanding." And the communal grief ritual the corpus generalizes from her she credits by name: "With my husband Tino, I had the great good fortune to participate in a number of indigenous West African grief rituals with the late, great Sobonfu Somé, who was a ritual keeper from the ancient Dagara tribe" ([[grief cannot complete alone]]).

Her second contribution is a practice, and she claims the coinage — "I worked hard to create a 'middle path' option of channeling the emotion properly." The 2010 definition: "to direct or convey something along a chosen pathway in a conscientious manner. If we can learn to properly channel our emotions, we can begin to work with them in vibrant and ingenious ways. We can interpret the messages our emotions carry and make use of the instincts our emotions contain" (*The Language of Emotions*, p. 33). The corpus's two failure modes are 2010's: repression "shoves that help under the rug"; expression "tends to train our brains into expressing the emotion the next time… we lose the message in the tumult," the puppet and the taskmaster. Her current position withdraws the categorical claim — "Expression and repression aren't problems in and of themselves. They're fine in many instances; they're only problems when they're the only choices you have" — and "venting" is the corpus's word rather than hers. The corpus reads channeling as the middle voice applied to affect, which is what joins her contribution to the speech-act lineage's ([[the affective practice completes a feeling's descent with consent between repression and venting]]).

## The counterweight worth keeping

Two objections stood against her model, and the reading changes both. The first was current science at survey depth: Lisa Feldman Barrett's constructionism "sits in genuine tension with McLaren's discrete emissaries, each with its fixed gift and question," and the survey asked "are the messengers found or made?" (space-around: around the chemistry). She is not in that fight. She cites Barrett as a source — "In her books *How Emotions Are Made* and *7½ Lessons about the Brain*, researcher Lisa Feldman Barrett writes about how we co-regulate each other's emotions" — and her first key is constructionist in spirit, "unvalence your emotions, because there is no such thing as positive or negative emotions." Her own testimony settles found-or-made at two levels: she made the questions ("I worked directly with them, as if they were personalities, and asked them what they were for… That's why I created a verbal question to ask when any emotion comes up"), she has revised them ("I've updated most of the emotions and their questions"), and she refuses any fixed account of what an emotion means in a situation — "I don't have a simple, step-by-step process for dealing with the situation above, and I am really suspicious of people who do." Each emotion's job is stable; the answer is found in the working.

The second arrived from the founding paper of the book's own verification standard: Gendlin holds that an emotion is "sheer," internally one quality, and that emotions "often keep him from sensing that in himself which is the complex ground of the emotion" ([[eugene gendlin]], from the 1964 reading). With her side now read the disagreement narrows. She does not claim the emotion carries pre-formed content; she claims it names the file. What survives is a question of direction — for Gendlin the emotion is in the way of the complex ground and one goes past it; for McLaren the emotion is the correct address of the complex ground and one goes into it — and it is a far more defensible thing for the book to stage between two co-patrons of one chapter than "open the mail" against "the emotion is not the mail."

## Where she sits in the book

She is seated beside Gendlin at the alphabet unit and the chemistry unit, and the corpus names the mortar between them: both found that naming done in the body shifts what is named, which is also the Kashmir Śaiva mātṛkā doctrine (arc-gloss: ch5; walked IX). She supplies the molecule formulas their interrogative face — each formula can be asked a question rather than only read — and she supplies the difficult messengers, jealousy and envy, to the polarity unit (arc-gloss: ch9). Her sharpest refusal is aimed at the same place Hudson's is, the observational stance: "the focus of this meditative practice is to continually watch and calm down and repress your emotions… How in the world are you ever going to become aware of your emotions if you don't know what purpose they serve in your psyche and your life?" Where the two of them part is expression — his second phase is obligatory and moves the body, her 2010 account is that expression costs the message ([[joe hudson]]).

**Load proposal (2026-09-01, confirmed at her text 2026-09-02).** With `names-existing-practice`, proposed by a /verify pass on the twelve chairs the load axis had never been asked of, and the grid's proposal until the author makes it: channeling is the affective practice the book already runs — "the middle voice for affect," a feeling completing its descent with consent between repression and venting — and the book loses the practice's name and its co-patron if she leaves, not its verification standard. The reading removes one thing the proposal had leaned on: grief's need for a witness is not her admission but the book's own doctrine, so the load rests on the practice alone.
