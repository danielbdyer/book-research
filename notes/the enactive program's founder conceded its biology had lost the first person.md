# the enactive program's founder conceded its biology had lost the first person

The clearest evidence that the autopoietic biology loses the first person comes from inside the program that built it. Francisco Varela, its co-founder, later turned to neurophenomenology — a research program designed to fold disciplined first-person reports back into the science of the brain — and that turn is itself an admission that the biology as first stated had let the first person go. His co-author Humberto Maturana drifted the other way, toward a self-sealing rhetoric of a biology of love that resists disconfirmation. The split is the finding: one founder repaired the loss by importing method the biology lacked, the other insulated the theory from the objection, and neither path preserves the original account's claim to have kept the lived interior. For a book tempted to cite the autopoietic biology as a science of experience, the founders' later careers are a warning that the experience was not in it.

The concession is indirect rather than declared, which is why it is worth recording. The program does not announce that demoting the felt report guts a verifying experience; it demonstrates the loss by the corrective one of its founders felt compelled to build.
