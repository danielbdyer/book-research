# the always-there hides by being always there and wittgenstein said so without heidegger

The book's foundation is that truth is un-forgetting and what is sought was never absent ([[aletheia and pratyabhijna converge on truth as un-forgetting]]). It needs an account of why something never absent goes unnoticed, and its own answer is that the structures nearest to hand are the ones a person cannot see — the wall, the door, the floor of a room are invisible from inside it precisely because they are always there.

Ludwig Wittgenstein states that as a general epistemic principle in *Philosophical Investigations* §129: the aspects of things most important to us are hidden by their simplicity and familiarity, and one fails to notice something because it is always before one's eyes. He wrote it in 1953, from inside a tradition with no Heidegger under it and no contact with the recognition school. The mesh grid calls it "the single cleanest found correspondence in the grid" (`mesh grid: tier 3`), and adds the observation that the book does not know it has it.

Its value is specific rather than general, and it is the reason this note exists at all. The sort's central finding about the book's supporting company is that its independence fails — three of the four names around the founding convergence are in conversation with the same German ([[the convergence's independence fails where three of its names share one teacher]]). This is one of the few arrivals that passes the test cleanly.

## Substantiation

- Read at the primary text (§129, Anscombe 2nd/3rd ed.): "The aspects of things that are most important for us are hidden because of their simplicity and familiarity. (One is unable to notice something—because it is always before one's eyes.) The real foundations of his enquiry do not strike a man at all. Unless that fact has at some time struck him.—And this means: we fail to be struck by what, once seen, is most striking and most powerful." The vault's earlier paraphrase had re-punctuated the parenthetical into a semicolon; Anscombe's text is a period and parentheses, which the note now carries.
- The grid's own framing, which is where the finding's force sits: "That is the WALL/DOOR/FLOOR veto reason, stated in 1953 with no Heidegger under it. Nothing hides like the always-there has an independent arrival and the book does not know it yet."
- The book's version of the principle is architectural rather than epistemic — it names three specific always-there structures and treats their invisibility as the thing to be worked on ([[container-anatomy]]). Wittgenstein's is a claim about noticing in general. The relation is that the book's architecture is a special case of his principle, which is what makes it an arrival at the same finding rather than a restatement of the same words.
- One refusal to keep, now confirmed at the surrounding passage and it is half the finding. §129 sits inside an argument about philosophical method, and the always-there being surveyed is *grammar*: noticing it dissolves a false problem rather than disclosing a truth. Wittgenstein states the method flatly — "Philosophy simply puts everything before us, and neither explains nor deduces anything.—...For what is hidden... is of no interest to us" (§126); "it leaves everything as it is" (§124); the aim is that "the philosophical problems should completely disappear" (§133). The book claims the opposite — that noticing *discloses* something that was there to be disclosed — so the two say one sentence about noticing and mean incompatible things about what noticing yields. He is filed under analytic philosophy, one of three near-empty families in the sort, which is why he was worth reading rather than quoting.
- A second passage read this round is the closer parallel to the book's *floor* than §129 is. §129 is about what goes *unnoticed*; the hinge image is about what is *load-bearing*: "the questions that we raise and our doubts depend on the fact that some propositions are exempt from doubt, are as it were like hinges on which those turn" (On Certainty §341), and "if I want the door to turn, the hinges must stay put" (§343). That is the book's floor exactly — the structure invisible because you stand on it and everything turns on it. The kept difference is sharper here: for Wittgenstein hinges hold fast by our *practice*, not by their truth (§342), so On Certainty offers the book its better architectural analogy at the cost of an even more explicitly anti-ontological, practice-relative grounding. The container-anatomy floor is the book's own version ([[container-anatomy]]).
- The discipline finding this note belongs to: analytic philosophy is one of three near-empty families in the sort, and the grid's reading of that is about the reading rather than the field — "It is the shape of the reading that has happened so far" (`mesh grid: the family-level read`).
