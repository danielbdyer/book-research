# a bond becomes real when it survives your full force without retaliating or collapsing

The reader gains the freedom to stop being careful. The relationship you can actually *use* to grow is the one that has taken your worst — your fury, your fiercest disagreement, your attempt to push it away entirely — and is still standing there, unretaliating, the next morning. Then, and only then, does the other stop being your fantasy of them and become gloriously real, a separate person outside your control whom you can love *because* they are not made of glass. You are permitted your whole self, edges included. Its use to the writing is to teach that a reader's aggression is not the enemy of intimacy but its test-and-gate.

## Substantiation

- Winnicott, "The Use of an Object" (1969): the subject destroys the object in fantasy, the object survives the destruction without retaliation, and this survival moves it from "subjective object" (my projection) to a real object I can use — "the subject says: 'I destroyed you'… From now on the subject says: 'Hullo object! I destroyed you. I love you.'" Reality and love arrive through survived aggression, not despite it. Nietzsche, Zarathustra ("On the Friend"): "in a friend one should have one's best enemy… you should be closest to him in heart when you resist him." Chavruta and Proverbs 27:17: "as iron sharpens iron, so one person sharpens another" (b. Ta'anit 7a) — the study-pair argues hard because the bond holds.
- Refusal kept: Winnicott means unconscious destruction and non-retaliation, not license for cruelty; Nietzsche's friend braces rather than consoles. Independent (clinic, German philosophy, rabbinic practice).
- Standing: nascent; hold "destruction" as Winnicott meant it so the claim never reads as license for harm.
