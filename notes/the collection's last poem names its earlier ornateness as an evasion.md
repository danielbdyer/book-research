# the collection's last poem names its earlier ornateness as an evasion

The sixteen poems move from an elaborate surface to a bare one, and the movement is wide enough to be measured rather than felt. Poem two is the ornamental extreme and the collection's one poem in standard capitalization and justified paragraphs, built of balanced figures: "a symphony of silent confessions," "The moment their lips meet is a collision of stars," a choice "to savor the wine of their friendship without uncorking the headier bottle of romantic entanglement" (poems #2). Poem four runs past a hundred lines of stacked colons, dashes and parentheses; poem eleven is the most latinate — "letting them pass into narrow / designs or fruitless arrears" (poems #11). The last poem is short-lined, lowercase, anaphoric, and would survive being spoken aloud without a page.

What makes this a finding rather than a stylistic note is that the last poem gives the earlier register a diagnosis. It names the elaborate surface as a building: "somewhere along the way i built a cathedral / without meaning to / a cathedral of care / so intricate / so considered / so acoustically beautiful / that my longing never had to arrive naked / never had to stand in the doorway / shivering" (poems #16). *Acoustically* is the word doing the work. The complaint is not that the cathedral was insincere but that it made a small want sound large and devotional, in "a register that could be mistaken for service" (poems #16). Set against it is the plain alternative, given in full: "because the naked sentence is not hard / you matter to me / i miss you / i want to stay in the thread / three sentences a child could say" (poems #16).

The repudiation is explicit about what is being set down: "the courage now is the size of a text… without ceremony / without manuscript / without the careful architecture of preparation" (poems #16). A body of work that spent fifteen poems building careful architecture ends by naming careful architecture as the thing that kept the sentence from leaving.

## Substantiation

- The poem does not disown the register it diagnoses, and this note keeps that proportion: "and the cathedral is real / the cathedral has saved me more than once / and also — i am tired of paying its full invoice" (poems #16). The finding is a cost accounting, not a recantation.
- One image tracks the same movement across the whole collection and is recorded as this note's own observation: the tremor. In the ornate poem it belongs to the beloved's body — "Her response is a shiver, a delicate quake that travels through her body and into his" (poems #2). In the plain one it belongs to the speaker's own sentence — "the trembling that is the sound of the sentence leaving / the architecture" (poems #16).
- The collection contains one earlier poem that titles itself against ornament, which shows the impulse predates its final statement: "an okay but not great poem" (poems #6).
