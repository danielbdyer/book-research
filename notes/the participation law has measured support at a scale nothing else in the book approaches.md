# the participation law has measured support at a scale nothing else in the book approaches

The corpus states one law it says every practice lineage agrees on: what changes a person is enacted participation inside a held container, and nothing transforms from the audience. The nearest measured version of that claim in the research literature is the relation between the working alliance and the outcome of psychotherapy, and it is the largest evidence base bearing on anything the book asserts. Flückiger and colleagues synthesise 295 independent studies covering more than 30,000 patients published between 1978 and 2017, and report an overall association of r = .278 (95% CI [.256, .299], p < .0001, equivalent to d = .579). They report it holding "across assessor perspectives, alliance and outcome measures, treatment approaches, patient characteristics, and countries," and they call the accumulated work "among the richest bodies of empirical research on psychotherapy process outcome."

The finding matters to the book because of what it holds across rather than because of its size. The relation does not vary meaningfully by which school of therapy is being practised. That is the participation law's own content in measured form: what predicts whether a person changes is the quality of the collaboration they are inside, rather than the technique being applied to them.

The obvious way to dismiss the whole literature was tested in this paper and did not work. If people who were already improving simply rated their therapists more warmly, the correlation would be an artifact running backwards. Sixty-six of the studies reported both the raw correlation and a partial correlation adjusting for intake characteristics and early symptom change, and the two did not differ significantly (Q(1) = 1.651, p = .199; raw r = .25, partial r = .22). The authors' conclusion is that the association "is not primarily an epiphenomenon linked to intake characteristics and related early therapy gains," and they cite session-by-session studies finding that within-patient changes in alliance precede symptom change rather than following it.

## Substantiation

- All figures and quotations come from the paper read complete on 2026-08-07; the record with the section addresses is `archive/reading — fluckiger, del re, wampold and horvath, the alliance in adult psychotherapy (2018).md`.
- The authors state the ceiling on their own claim, and the vault records it with the finding rather than beneath it. No experiment can establish this, because "it is ethically and conceptually not possible to randomize patients to treatment conditions where these variables are manipulated (e.g., to a high and low alliance condition)." What they claim is that the alliance is "a moderate causal facilitative factor," on longitudinal evidence.
- This supports a mechanism the book describes and supports nothing about the book, which nobody has read. The vault does not postulate efficacy (`ops/methodology/authorial stance.md`), and a result of this size is the easiest material in the vault to overstate.
- Every study in the meta-analysis has a therapist in it, so the transfer to a reader alone with a text is not established here. The nearest available evidence is the internet-therapy subset, held at [[the alliance holds its size when the exchange is mediated and written]].
- Nothing on this literature stood in the vault before 2026-08-07. A search across the notes, the operational files, the archive and the provenance reconstructions for *alliance*, *common factors*, *Wampold*, *Horvath*, *Norcross*, *working alliance* and *contextual model* returned one passing mention on the Rogers evaluation.
