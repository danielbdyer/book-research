# the corsair affair shows indirection fails when the author cannot disappear behind the work

Indirection presupposes that the author can disappear behind the work. In 1845–46 Kierkegaard provoked the satirical paper *The Corsair* into caricaturing him, and the sustained public ridicule made him a figure everyone recognized in the street, converting him from a hidden maieutic operator into a spectacle. The device failed at its precondition: once the person is unignorable, the work can no longer stand in the author's place. Worse, the indirection itself became a way of being admired — Kierkegaard concluded that his own method had become a self-admiring attachment to language, a "demonic temptation" — which is the connoisseur trap closing on the inventor of the devices built to prevent it. The finding matters to a book whose own recorded failure mode is prose that recruits an admiring posture; it rests on secondary scholarship, the primaries unopened here and queued in `ops/reading queue.md`.
