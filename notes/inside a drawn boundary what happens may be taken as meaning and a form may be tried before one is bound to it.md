# inside a drawn boundary what happens may be taken as meaning and a form may be tried before one is bound to it

The reader gains permission, because the boundary changes the status of what happens inside it. Within the frame a thing need not be taken literally or as commitment; it is taken symbolically, provisionally, in the subjunctive "as if." You gain a place to try on a self, a stance, a possibility — and to feel it fully — without yet having to be it, which is the precise room the book's whole arc needs, since it walks a transformation you undergo before you decide what to keep. Its use to the writing is to name why the book can move a reader through a whole arc with nothing yet at stake: the master question made structural.

## Substantiation

- Milner: the frame "marks off an area within which what is perceived has to be taken symbolically," while what falls outside it is taken literally — the boundary an ontological switch, inside meaning, outside fact (1952; On Not Being Able to Paint, 1950). Turner: the liminal is culture's subjunctive, the mood of "as if" and "may be," "a realm of pure possibility" in which "novel configurations of ideas and relations may arise" and a form can be tried before it is fixed (The Ritual Process, 1969; "Betwixt and Between," 1967). The augur's *templum* is the same cut-out field within which alone the signs could be read.
- Refusal kept: Turner insists the subjunctive is bounded in time and must be exited into the indicative — you try the form inside the liminal precisely because you will step out and then decide.
