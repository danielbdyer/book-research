# the later wittgenstein treats philosophy as therapy that dissolves confusions rather than a doctrine that advances theses

The later Wittgenstein offers a second model for a book that transmits nothing to store. He treats philosophy as treatment — dissolving confusions rather than advancing theses: "The philosopher's treatment of a question is like the treatment of an illness" (*Philosophical Investigations* §255), and "there is not a philosophical method, though there are indeed methods, like different therapies" (§133). The device blocks the craving for a doctrine to hold. Its cost is that no positive result survives the treatment: the author leaves the reader with a dissolved problem and nothing to carry away, nothing to admire, and nothing to cite. For a book whose failure mode is the reader collecting sentences instead of practicing, a method that leaves nothing collectible is a candidate discipline rather than a decoration.

## Substantiation

- Read at the primary text (Anscombe 2nd/3rd ed.): "there is not a philosophical method, though there are indeed methods, like different therapies" (§133) and "The philosopher's treatment of a question is like the treatment of an illness" (§255). The success criterion Wittgenstein states is the disappearance of the problem, not a sensation — "the philosophical problems should completely disappear... Problems are solved (difficulties eliminated), not a single problem" (§133).
- The open question the dig raised — whether "therapy" names a verification standard (a problem dissolving felt as relief) or only a metaphor — resolves, on the primary text, toward metaphor and method. Both remarks are marked as simile ("like"), and the criterion is the problem's disappearance rather than a bodily relief; the phenomenology of "peace" is gestured at ("gives philosophy peace," §133) but left unelaborated. So the text neither supports nor builds a felt-relief standard, which keeps this note distinct from the felt-shift verification material rather than fused to it. The transmission-dig entry (2026-08-13, D7) and the Stanford Encyclopedia remain the note's origin; the primary now carries it.
