# an apple grown from seed yields a wilding rather than its parent

Apples are so heterozygous that a seed almost never reproduces the fruit of its parent tree. Every named variety persists only as a clone, grafted as living wood onto rootstock, and an orchard grown from seed yields wildings rather than copies. The panel offered this as an uncomfortable and useful resonance at the book's sparsest seam, the question of what participation can mean for a reader holding a printed page alone. Text is seed propagation and contact is grafting, so a book that cannot graft should expect every reader to grow a wilding rather than a copy of what the author grew. The discomfort is kept rather than dissolved: the book cannot transmit its variety. Yet its own premise absorbs the finding better than most, because "you already know everything in this book" addresses a reader who already has rootstock, and a wilding is a failure only if the goal was transmission of the variety, which this book disavows ([[a framework succeeds by increasing the diversity of authentic insights, not the uniformity of conclusions]]; [[nothing transforms from the audience]]).

This resonance accompanies the book's premise rather than testing it: horticulture is company for the claim that a reader grows their own recognition, not evidence that reading works. The panel named no outside route for it, so it stays drafting material held where a writing session will reach for it.
