# the required second person splits into a judge and a mirror

The thinkers in the book's company who refuse the solitary reader, a count across the company that now approaches nineteen of them, do not converge on one missing person. They specify two, and the two are incompatible. The first has the authority to overrule what the reader reports about themselves. Al-Qushayrī's shaykh exists because "a person in a state is the worst-placed judge of what it means"; the Fourth Way's teacher "can see what the pupil cannot"; Britton's protective factor is "the availability of a teacher who can recognize difficulty"; and Zen's dokusan is the interview where a teacher "discriminates makyō from kensho." The second person is forbidden to do exactly that. Ignatius's director stands "in the centre like a balance," and the format's safety is earned "by a companion forbidden to persuade"; Rogers's helper makes "the conditions rather than the interpretation"; and Gendlin's listener does not judge at all, because "Not even the individual himself judges it but… his direct referent does the judging" (1964, fn. 11).

The disagreement belongs to the book because the book's own doctrine of reader sovereignty takes a side. An instrument in service of a life that remains the reader's own can offer the reader a mirror, but it cannot offer a judge without becoming an authority over the reader. The two safety failures the vault has already recorded run the other way: neither the book nor its reader can tell dissolution from regression, and a person inside a state misjudges that state. Those are precisely the cases the judge exists for, and the mirror cannot cover them, because they are the cases in which the reader's own referent is the instrument in doubt. What the Second Chair is for is therefore an open question rather than a settled device, and the question has two halves: which of these two persons is the second chair meant to be, and at which stretch of the arc?

## Substantiation

- The judge side rests on four evaluations, those of al-Qushayrī, Gurdjieff and Ouspensky, Britton, and the record of makyō; the mirror side rests on three, those of Ignatius, Rogers, and Gendlin, the last of whom was read in his own 1964 paper. The positions marked on their notes as derived from works about the thinker rather than read in the thinker's own work carry that standing into this claim.
- The doctrine that forces the choice is [[the book can only be auxiliary to a sovereign reader]], which holds that the book's honest maximum is an instrument in service of a life that stays the reader's own, never an authority over it.
- The failure the judge exists for is [[the book cannot tell dissolution from regression and neither can its reader]], which records that pre-rational and trans-rational states both look non-rational from outside and that the book names no way of telling them apart, and whose own text disqualifies the solitary body as the instrument of that judgment.
