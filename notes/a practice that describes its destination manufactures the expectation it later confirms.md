# a practice that describes its destination manufactures the expectation it later confirms

Listing what conditions a mystic's experience, Steven Katz includes the practices themselves, and the sentence he writes is the nearest his essay comes to attacking a person's report that something in them changed: "Related to these expectations are also future directed activities such as meditation, fasting, ritual ablutions, self-mortification, and so on, which create further expectations about what the future and future states of consciousness will be like. There is obviously a self-fulfilling prophetic aspect to this sort of activity" (Katz 1978, p. 59). Katz makes nothing further of it. The clause serves his argument that experiences differ between traditions, and he never asks whether the anticipated state occurs, whether the practitioner can tell that it occurred, or what a practitioner should do about the arrangement. The observation is therefore his, and every consequence drawn below is the vault's.

The consequence for this book is specific and it is about ordering rather than about content. The book's single verification standard is the felt shift, and the book explains what a felt shift is: what it feels like, how it differs from an idea, what its arrival means. A reader who has been told what the destination feels like before setting out is in the position Katz describes — carrying an expectation the text created — and the reader's later report that the shift arrived is then a report from inside that expectation. This is not the fluency hazard already recorded in the graph, which is about a reader's body assenting to well-made prose; it is one step earlier, at the point where the prose supplies the criterion the body will later be asked to meet. The two hazards compound, and neither is answered by the other.

The design consequence is that a text can decline the ordering without declining the standard. If the destination is staged before it is named — the reader is walked into a candidate event and the text points back at it afterward — then the reader's noticing precedes the description rather than following it, and the description becomes a name for something already had rather than a specification to be met. Whether that ordering survives contact with a real reader is not settled by anything here, and the book's own instrument cannot settle it: the same reader is the one reporting.

## Substantiation

- The reading is recorded at `archive/reading — katz, language, epistemology, and mysticism (1978).md`. The quotation comes from page 59 of the 1978 Oxford edition, and the reading record notes that Katz leaves the clause undeveloped.
- The division of labour in this claim is stated rather than blurred, because the correspondence rule requires it. Katz supplies the observation that a practice generates expectations about future states and that the arrangement is self-fulfilling. The vault supplies the application to a book that describes its own verification standard in advance, and Katz nowhere discusses books, readers, or written instruction.
- The hazard this claim distinguishes itself from is at [[felt shift versus felt confirmation]], where a reader's bodily assent answers the fluency of the prose rather than the truth of the claim. The distinction is order: that hazard runs while the reader reads a sentence, and this one runs before the reader reaches it.
- The book's own defense against a manufactured shift is the felt sense's power to refuse a formulation, which is stated at [[the felt sense rejects formulations and that friction is what separates it from fluency]]. That defense answers a reader who is handed a wrong word. It does not obviously answer a reader who was handed the right word too early.
