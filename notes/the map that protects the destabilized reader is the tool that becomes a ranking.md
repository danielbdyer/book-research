# the map that protects the destabilized reader is the tool that becomes a ranking

Two of the company's safety findings prescribe opposite designs, and no note has put them in one room. For the destabilized reader, the finding is that a map protects: Ingram holds that "vagueness about them is what harms people," and the measured version is that an accurate framework is itself a protective factor. Against handing over maps, the precedent bench's unanimous record: Wilber's "colour hierarchy became a status ladder among his readers"; the Fourth Way's vocabulary-plus-teacher system "acquires authority dynamics its founder did not intend"; and the bench's only good outcome, Hofstadter, succeeded "partly by refusing to deliver a usable framework" — a bargain whose precedent "does not transfer." Bateson's Learning III warning — some who go past the threshold "fall by the wayside… labeled by psychiatry as psychotic" — sits on the protective side's case for why the map matters at all.

The candidate resolution comes from reading the chairs' cases against each other rather than from any one of them: Ingram's protective map is a sequence of expected difficulty — it tells a person where they are on a hard road — while Wilber's toxic ladder is a hierarchy of attainment — it tells a person how high they stand. If that distinction holds, the deliverable can split where the dial cannot: hand over the sequence, withhold the ladder. It is recorded as a candidate treatment, derived and untested, and the design decision is the author's.
