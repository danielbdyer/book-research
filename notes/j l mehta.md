# j l mehta

Jarava Lal Mehta (1912–1988) taught at Banaras Hindu University and later at Harvard's Center for the Study of World Religions, and his study of Heidegger — revised as *Martin Heidegger: The Way and the Vision* (1976) — made him the founding scholar of the India–Heidegger encounter, in full command of both sides. The pointing is his dictum on the Europeanization of the earth, quoted through Wilhelm Halbfass: there is no way but to go along with this Europeanization and to go through it — "only through this voyage into the foreign and the strange can we win back our own selfhood; here as elsewhere, the way to what is closest to us is the longest way back." The wording still needs checking against the printed Halbfass before manuscript use, and the sentence is worth the check: it is the book's road-into-the-valley responsibility in mirror image, and its closing clause is the doctrine of the long way back said decades early.

The refusal kept is Mehta himself: he is the standing refusal of the shortcut. The scholar best positioned to declare the traditions one held instead that the encounter cannot be skipped to, and fusion talk that spares the reader the voyage is what his life's work declines.
