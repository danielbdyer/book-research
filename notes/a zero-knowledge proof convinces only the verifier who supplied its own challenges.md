# a zero-knowledge proof convinces only the verifier who supplied its own challenges

A zero-knowledge interactive proof convinces its verifier completely while a recording of the same proof convinces nobody who watches it afterward. This is a theorem rather than a limitation of the recording: any transcript of such a proof could have been produced by simulation without the secret, so conviction exists only for the party who supplied live, unpredictable challenges during the exchange. The panel read this as the participation law in the form of mathematics. The audience of a completed proof holds only a transcript, and whatever convinced the verifier, the verifier's own unpredictable challenges did it — which is the book's law that nothing transforms from the audience, reached in a domain no curation could have gathered ([[nothing transforms from the audience]]). It lands at the graph's sparsest seam: a book is a transcript, so a book's only route to conviction is inducing the reader to supply their own live challenges, which the letter genre and the facilitation-scaled-to-one thesis reach for from the other side ([[the book can only be auxiliary to a sovereign reader]]).

This resonance accompanies the participation law rather than testing it: a formal result from cryptography is company for the law's shape, never corroboration of it.
