# the confessions is address to a thou that the reader overhears

When the author named a model for the book's epistemic form, he named the *Confessions* (author statement, 2026-08-04). The model turns out to hold more than the first-person grounding it was named for. The *Confessions* is not a narration to a reader; it is an address to God, sustained from its first sentence — "Great are you, O Lord, and greatly to be praised" (Confessions I.1) — and Augustine knows he is overheard. He raises the overhearing directly: "Why then does it matter to me whether men hear my confessions, as if it were they who would cure my diseases?" (Confessions X.3) — and answers that the human readers listening in on an address not made to them are moved differently than an audience addressed head-on would be.

What this gives the open person question is a wider option space, not a lean. The vault holds the question as a live tension — the drafts realize the book's voice as a plural we, a singular I, and an impersonal register, and the author has said directly that first person against second person is undecided. Overheard address is a third position the I/you dichotomy does not contain: the writer speaks with his whole voice to a Thou who is not the reader, and the reader receives everything while being the target of nothing. The form has properties each pole of the dichotomy wants — the unguarded intimacy of I-speech, and the reader's freedom from being handled that second-person address puts at risk. The one confirmed sighting of the book's promised event ran on a related axis: a letter written to its reader a decade before it was read is also received at an angle, address arriving from outside the moment of reading.

This note widens the option space and stops. Which person the book speaks in is the author's decision, listed as open in `ops/methodology/the book's current shape.md`, tier three.

## Substantiation

- Confessions I.1 opens in address and the address never breaks: the entire work is spoken to "you, O Lord," with the reader unaddressed throughout.
- Confessions X.3 is the passage where the form knows itself: Augustine asks what the overhearing readers are for, and keeps them — "the ear of my brothers" — as witnesses to an address made past them.
- The vault's ancestry claim for reading-as-remembering already runs through Confessions X on memory; this note reads the same book for its form rather than its doctrine, and the two readings are independent.
