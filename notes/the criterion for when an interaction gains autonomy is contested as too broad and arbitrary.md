# the criterion for when an interaction gains autonomy is contested as too broad and arbitrary

The mechanism the vault leans on for the third thing arrives with a live dispute the book must carry with it. De Jaegher and Di Paolo say an interaction becomes autonomous when the coupling's regulation targets aspects of the coupling itself, and critics answer that the criterion is too broad: on its wording two cells in a dish, or two states negotiating a treaty, qualify as much as two people in genuine contact. A second charge is that the criterion is normatively arbitrary — it stipulates that coercion destroys the agents' autonomy while ordinary influence does not, without a principled line between them. The stronger constitution claim, that the interaction partly constitutes social cognition rather than merely causing it, is disputed in the same literature, and the evidence base carrying the weight is thin, resting largely on a one-dimensional minimal paradigm. Enactivism argues with itself here rather than agreeing, so the mechanism is a stratum of contested positions and not a settled result.

The dispute does not remove the mechanism's value; it prices it. A book that cites participatory sense-making for the third thing inherits a criterion whose boundary is under attack, and honesty requires carrying the attack alongside the citation.
