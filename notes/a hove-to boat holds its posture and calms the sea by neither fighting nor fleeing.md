# a hove-to boat holds its posture and calms the sea by neither fighting nor fleeing

A sailboat caught in a storm can be hove-to: its headsail is backed against the wind and its helm is lashed, so it stops sailing without anchoring, holds its attitude to the oncoming seas, and can resume sailing within seconds. Its slow drift to leeward leaves a slick of turbulent water to windward that measurably dampens the waves before they break. The panel found in this the practice posture the book describes — presence without agenda while remaining available to act — stated in the terms of naval architecture ([[unrelated traditions converge on presence without agenda while remaining available to act]]). Two features carry the resonance. The posture is achieved as a configuration rather than as an effort, which meets the paradox of cultivating what cannot be cultivated by making it an arrangement instead of a strain ([[the basic practice posture is a point of return rather than a state]]). And the stillness itself manufactures the protection, since the slick exists only because the boat is neither fighting the sea nor fleeing it.

This resonance accompanies the practice posture rather than testing it: a sailing configuration is company for the posture the traditions already converge on, not evidence that the posture works.
