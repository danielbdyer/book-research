# the trained-vocabulary objection arrives independently from experimental social psychology

The objection the vault treats as the deepest form of the fluency problem turns out to have two unconnected bearers. Steven Katz's constructivism holds that a mystical report is shaped by the tradition that produced the reporter, so a body trained in the book's vocabulary would countersign the training rather than anything beyond it. Nisbett and Wilson reach the same premise from laboratory studies of ordinary self-report, and they state it as a rule about when reports come out accurate: "we may say that people will be accurate in reports about the causes of their behavior and evaluations wherever the culture, or a subculture, specifies clearly what stimuli should produce which responses, and especially where there is continuing feedback from the culture or subculture concerning the extent to which the individual is following the prescribed rules" (1977, p. 254). Their next sentences refuse to count that accuracy as knowledge of oneself: "such accuracy cannot be regarded as evidence of direct access to processes of evaluation. It is evidence for nothing more than the ability to describe the formal rules of evaluation" (p. 254).

The two arrivals share the premise and disagree about almost everything else, which is what makes the agreement worth recording. Katz is a philosopher of religion arguing about mystical experience across traditions; Nisbett and Wilson are experimental social psychologists arguing from studies of shoppers, insomniacs and undergraduates. Neither cites the other, and the 1977 paper precedes Katz's 1978 essay by a year, so neither is downstream of the other.

What the pairing does to the book is sharpen one objection rather than add a second. A reader taught the book's vocabulary, given its practices, and told what a shift feels like has been supplied with a subculture's rules and its continuing feedback, which is Nisbett and Wilson's own recipe for accurate-looking reports that demonstrate nothing but fluency in the rules. The book's existing answer is that a felt sense can refuse a formulation, and a trained capacity to reject the trainer's own sentence is a different fact from a trained yes ([[the felt sense rejects formulations and that friction is what separates it from fluency]]). That answer is now answering two literatures at once, and whether it holds is what both queued readings jointly test.

## Substantiation

- Both passages above appear on page 254 of the paper, which was read complete on 2026-08-07; the record is `archive/reading — nisbett and wilson, telling more than we can know (1977).md`. The paper's standard for what would count as access is stated in the same terms on page 251: "verbal report which exceeds in accuracy that obtained from observers provided with a general description of the stimulus and response in question."

It also belongs to the class of agreements that arrived unlooked-for: the vault queued the 1977 paper to test a different question entirely, and the agreement with Katz was found while reading for the carve-out.
