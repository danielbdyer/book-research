# the poem knew more than the poet did and the book carries that surplus forward as something still becoming

The reader gains a gift that is still alive in their hands — not a closed system handed down complete, but a knowing that was itself formed by carrying-forward and is still carrying forward as they read. There is a chain: the author's felt sense came partly to form in his poems, the book's prose carries that surplus further, and the reader's own implicit is the next link, where it forms again. What the reader gains is participation in an unfinished movement — what reaches them exceeded even its maker's prior knowing, so it can exceed the reader's too, and become in *them* what it could not have been anywhere else. Its use to the writing is to reframe the book's provenance as an asset to the reader rather than a fact about the author.

## Substantiation

- Frost ("The Figure a Poem Makes," 1939): a poem "begins as a lump in the throat, a sense of wrong, a homesickness, a lovesickness," and "no surprise for the writer, no surprise for the reader" — the made thing tells the maker something the maker did not set out knowing; his own name for a poem is "a momentary stay against confusion." Heaney ("Feeling into Words," Preoccupations, 1980): the poet's task is "to get his own feeling into his own words," poetry as *feeling into words*. Gendlin supplies the mechanism the poets testify to: the lump in the throat is a felt sense, and finding-the-thought-that-finds-the-words is carrying-forward.
- Company: the poets give the phenomenon from inside the craft, Gendlin the structure of why. Held delicately, since the author's sixteen poems are his own implicit made partly explicit, owned as his and at the distance the record keeps.
- Standing: nascent; Frost's lines are near-verbatim and Heaney's title exact; the application to the author's own poems is personal-provenance, stated only at the specificity the record supplies.
