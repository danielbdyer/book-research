# martin buber

Martin Buber's claim is that a person does not have one basic way of standing toward what is in front of them but two, and that the two constitute different worlds. In the I–It attitude the other is an object of experience: located, described, used, known about. In the I–Thou attitude the other is addressed rather than described, met with the whole of oneself, and not available as an object at all. The I of each pair is a different I. The relation is not a better quality of the same thing; it is a different thing.

Two features of the account bear directly on this book. The relation happens *between* — it is not produced by either party and not located in either, which is the ontological claim the book's whole vocabulary of the between depends on ([[the field between two people does work]]). And it cannot be brought about by will or technique; the Thou is met by grace as much as by choosing, and every Thou necessarily lapses back into an It.

## What he would refuse

Now read at the primary text (*I and Thou*, Smith translation, read whole 2026-08-24; `archive/reading — buber, i and thou and elements of the interhuman (1923, 1965).md`). The refusal that bears hardest is the one built into the account: the I–Thou cannot be produced, sustained, or made into a technique — "The Thou meets me through grace — it is not found by seeking," and "Every means is an obstacle. Only when every means has collapsed does the meeting come about" (I and Thou, Part One). It is not an achievement, and every attempt to hold it converts it into what it is not: "every Thou in our world must become an It … As soon as the relation has been … permeated with a means, the Thou becomes an object among objects" (Part One). A book with a practice curriculum aimed at the relational field is proposing, in a register of protocols, agreements and rotations, precisely the *means* he says turns Thou to It — which is the live test the curriculum must keep passing, not a flat prohibition (see the condition-design finding below).

He would also refuse the solitary form. For Buber the Thou is met, and the paradigm case is another person addressing and being addressed; a reader alone with a book is in the I–It relation to it by his own account, however well the book is written. The vault holds a finding that sits directly on this seam — the book's native genre is a letter read alone ([[the book's native genre is the letter from a friend read alone]]) — and a letter is the one form that might survive his objection, since it is addressed and expects an answer. Whether it does is a real question rather than a rhetorical one.

And on the disagreement with Heidegger: Buber's objection is that Heidegger's account of disclosure has no one being addressed in it — that Being reveals itself impersonally, and that a philosophy of the impersonal cannot ground the encounter. Since the book borrows disclosure from Heidegger and the encounter from Buber, that disagreement runs through its foundations rather than beside them.

## What the reading settled, and what it still requires

The primary reading of *I and Thou* (2026-08-24) settled two of the three questions and grounded the third in structure. **The between is ontological in the strong sense** — "In the beginning is relation" (Part Three), "Through the Thou a man becomes I" (Part One), the relation prior to and constitutive of both terms, not a description of how meeting is experienced. **The necessary lapse bears on the curriculum through its mechanism**: "every Thou in our world must become an It … As soon as the relation has been … permeated with a means, the Thou becomes an object among objects" (Part One), so a curriculum aimed at the relation turns its object to an It precisely when it is held as a means. **The argument against Heidegger is grounded in structure** — Buber's disclosure is always address to a Thou (the eternal Thou "cannot become It," Part Three), so it always has someone addressed in it, the ground of his charge that Heidegger's disclosure of Being is impersonal — but the explicit critique ("What Is Man?", *Between Man and Man*, 1938) is unread.

**The condition-design finding — the reading's most load-bearing result.** Entry 86 asked whether Buber concedes any reliable *preparation* for the between, or whether it is strictly ungraspable, since the whole book's premise is that facilitation tends the conditions. He concedes preparation and refuses production: the meeting "can never take place through my agency, nor can it ever take place without me," because "my speaking of the primary word to it is an act of my being … The primary word I-Thou can be spoken only with the whole being" (Part One). So there is a reliable preparation — availability, the turning toward, the whole-being readiness — and no reliable production, since the meeting comes by grace. That is the book's Book I posture — two steps back, tending conditions rather than producing the outcome — arrived at by Buber first, with his refusal kept intact: the preparation is availability, never a technique. In asymmetric settings he names it concretely — "making him actually present," confirmation over acceptance, "primary distance," the helper who "does not forfeit anything of himself" ("Elements of the Interhuman" and "Education," read via Yaron 1993, secondary; the primary essays owed).

Still owed: the primary "Elements of the Interhuman" and "Education" essays; the explicit Heidegger critique; and the German with a copyright edition for manuscript quotation.
