# the threshold state strips status and leaves a person betwixt and between

Victor Turner named the middle phase of a rite of passage liminality, from the Latin for threshold, and its defining mark is the loss of status. The dig carries his description that threshold people are "neither here nor there; they are betwixt and between" the positions assigned by law, custom, convention, and ceremony (Turner, "In and Out of Time"). A person in the liminal phase has left one classified position and not yet entered the next, so they hold no rank, and Turner likens the condition to death and to the womb — an unmade state out of which a remade one can come.

The finding deepens a structure the book already uses. The corpus builds the reading experience on Arnold van Gennep's three-part rite of separation, threshold, and reincorporation, and Turner supplies the phenomenology of the middle term: the threshold is not merely a transition between two settled states but a stripping of status that makes the person unmade. It also names the precondition for the anti-structural levelling Turner calls communitas, which requires a congregation of exactly these statusless, liminal people. For the book's arc — the onset and deepening that unmake the reader before the return remakes them — liminality is the state the middle chapters induce.
