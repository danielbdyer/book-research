# a word at madhyama can be perfected forever a word at vaikhari can be answered

The corpus inherits a three-level account of speech from the grammarian Bhartṛhari — paśyantī, meaning as a single felt flash; madhyamā, the fully formed but unspoken sentence; vaikharī, the word made flesh, breath, ink, sound reaching another person — named together once in the *Vākyapadīya*'s first book (I.142 in Iyer's numbering) and described only in verses the *Vṛtti* quotes; and a fourth level above them, parā, the supreme Word, which the Trika added against Bhartṛhari and identifies with the unlimited I-consciousness, holding it to contain the three lower levels actually rather than potentially — "All the three… exist simultaneously in parā" (Parātrīśikā-vivaraṇa, Singh p. 157). The corpus states the attribution correctly at atlas §VI, "Bhartṛhari's three, crowned with a fourth by the Śaivas," and walked VI's "Above the whole ladder, or beneath it, or as it: *aham*, the I that speaks" is closer to the tradition than any gloss of parā as bare potency. Two teachings hang on the ladder. First, the atlas records that the descent does not degrade the word: "the descent is not degradation — vaikharī is parā at full extension" (atlas §VI). A primary reading (2026-09-02) places that teaching: it is Abhinavagupta's, not Bhartṛhari's, and it is ontological rather than valuational. Bhartṛhari's *Vṛtti* ranks the soundless above the sounded — "To the two which have resonance, that which has none at all is superior" (VP 1.164*) — and describes liberation as the ascent back through the levels, so the ladder in the *Vākyapadīya* is one the practitioner climbs. Abhinavagupta supplies the clause: parā "is present in all experients always in her integral nature… uniformly in all states i.e., even at the level of paśyantī, madhyamā and vaikharī," and "up to vaikharī… this parāvāk… continues pulsating" (Singh pp. 8–9). Even there the ranking survives — he calls vaikharī "gross" at *Tantrāloka* III.236 — so the clause holds as a claim that the descent loses no presence, which is a real holarchy, and not as a claim that the uttered word is the goal. Second, the atlas states the distinction this note takes as its title and ranks it as the corpus's central distinction about speech (§VI).

The distinction has one consequence. An unspoken sentence can always be changed, which removes every risk from it; the walked document draws the conclusion that it is "perfectible forever, and therefore safe, and therefore, in a precise sense, not yet real" (walked VI). The spoken sentence cannot be revised, and being answered is what makes a word a deed. The corpus applies the distinction to the manuscript's own condition; that application has its own note, [[seventeen chapters rest at madhyama]].

## Substantiation

- Atlas §VI presents the ladder and applies the distinction at three scales at once — the book, the ask, and the sentence, all instances of expression awaiting consent.
- The attribution and the valence are read at primary (the record is `archive/reading — bhartṛhari's levels of speech and kṣemarāja on saṅkoca (vākyapadīya i; pratyabhijñāhṛdayam).md`). Bhartṛhari's own "fourth" runs the other way from the Trika's: his *Vṛtti* quotes Ṛgveda I.164.45, "Three of them are kept in a cave and do not move. Men speak the fourth part of the word," so his *turīya* is the spoken quarter, the lowest; the Trika's parā is the highest, and Somānanda argued for it against him (*Śivadṛṣṭi* II, at secondary via Padoux p. 191). What Bhartṛhari does keep is that the descent does not defile — vaikharī "coloured by adventitious impurities, is not really affected by them" (1.170*), paśyantī "like the last digit of the moon, is never overwhelmed" (1.168*) — which is the not-damaged half of the book's clause without the completing half. A numbering caution travels with every citation: the levels verse is I.142 in Iyer, 143 in Pillai, and 1.159 in the GRETIL e-text.
- Walked VI carries the reasoning: the unspoken sentence is revisable, therefore safe, therefore in a precise sense not yet real.
- First-telling, "Left in the tin": "the last line of the book is addressed to the reader, and it's written so that it can be answered. By the middle of the book, you'll understand that being answerable is the highest thing a sentence can be."
- Atlas §VI seats a Western ethics of answerability alongside the ladder: Bakhtin (the no-alibi-in-being), Rosenstock-Huessy ("respondeo etsi mutabor" — I respond, although I will be changed), and Arendt (the who of a person disclosed only in action before witnesses).
- The same tradition supplies a name for what sits between the spoken word and the answer. Its teaching on junctions holds that the concealed shows itself at the crossing between two states rather than inside either one, and walked IX applies it to exactly this interval: "the trembling between a sentence spoken and the answer arriving" ([[the gap between two states is where the concealed shows itself]]). The silence after an answerable sentence is therefore a junction rather than a failure.
