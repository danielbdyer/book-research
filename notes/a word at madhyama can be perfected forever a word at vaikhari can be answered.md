# a word at madhyama can be perfected forever a word at vaikhari can be answered

The corpus inherits a four-level account of speech (from the grammarian Bhartṛhari, extended by the Trika tradition): parā, speech as pure potency; paśyantī, meaning as a single felt flash; madhyamā, the fully formed but unspoken sentence; vaikharī, the word made flesh — breath, ink, sound reaching another person. Two teachings hang on the ladder. First, descent is not degradation: the embodied word is the supreme word at full extension, not a dilution of it. Second, the distinction this note takes as its title, which the atlas ranks as the corpus's central distinction about speech.

The consequence: the unspoken sentence is infinitely revisable, therefore safe, therefore — in the corpus's precise sense — not yet real. The spoken sentence cannot be revised, and answerability is what makes a word a deed. The corpus applies the distinction to the manuscript's own condition; that application has its own note, [[seventeen chapters rest at madhyama]].

## Substantiation

- Atlas §VI presents the ladder and applies the distinction at three scales at once — the book, the ask, and the sentence, all instances of expression awaiting consent.
- Walked VI carries the reasoning: the unspoken sentence is revisable, therefore safe, therefore in a precise sense not yet real.
- First-telling, "Left in the tin": "the last line of the book is addressed to the reader, and it's written so that it can be answered. By the middle of the book, you'll understand that being answerable is the highest thing a sentence can be."
- The Western ethics of answerability the corpus attaches: Bakhtin (no alibi in being), Rosenstock-Huessy ("respondeo etsi mutabor" — I respond, although I will be changed), Arendt (disclosure before witnesses).
