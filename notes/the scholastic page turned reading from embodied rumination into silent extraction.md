# the scholastic page turned reading from embodied rumination into silent extraction

Ivan Illich reads a bundle of medieval page technologies as the last time the physical page rewired what reading is. Hugh of St. Victor's reader, around 1128, still moved through a page as through a vineyard: reading aloud, ruminating, using the body and the memory. Within a century that reader was replaced. To speed the retrieval of a wanted passage, Peter Lombard's generation and its successors added chapter titles, keywords underlined in red, quotation marks, and marginal source references, and the wider period produced the subject index, chapter-and-verse division, running headers, cross-references, alphabetical ordering, silent reading, and the layout craft (*ordinatio* and *compilatio*) that lets a text be navigated rather than recited. Illich holds alphabetization the most revolutionary of these, a technology of systematization that spawned indices, dictionaries, and encyclopedias.

The net effect, on Illich's reading, is that the text detached from the codex into a searchable object: voice and body dropped out, and reading turned silent, visual, individual, and extractive — a page treated as a mine to be sifted for a wanted object rather than a vineyard to be walked. The finding is the historical precedent a claim about the book's own medium requires. It establishes that the page has rewired reading before, at the level of embodiment and attention, which is the register in which the book proposes to slow reading down again — its own contract instructs the reader to read slowly and to treat the urge to skip ahead as information.
