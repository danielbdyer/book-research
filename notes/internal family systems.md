# internal family systems

Internal Family Systems is Richard Schwartz's therapy model: the psyche is naturally multiple; there are no bad parts; the parts causing trouble are protectors running outdated jobs, guarding exiles who carry the original pain; and beneath the parts is Self, characterized by eight qualities — curiosity, calm, clarity, compassion, confidence, courage, creativity, connectedness. The space-around survey identifies it as the largest near-neighbour not yet addressed and warns that its absence "will eventually look like an omission" (space-around: around folded not broken). This evaluation checks the correspondences, the state of the evidence, and what the corpus already does with the model. The seating decision is the author's.

## The pointing

The sentence a drafting session would quote is Schwartz's claim about Self, because it is the same move the book makes about its basic practice posture, and it is made in a clinical register: "the Self does not need to be developed or built up. It emerges spontaneously when parts open space for it" (*No Bad Parts*). The book states the identical structure as a paradox — cultivating what cannot be cultivated — and answers it by defining the posture as a return rather than a state. Schwartz answers it by relocating the work: nothing produces Self, so the whole intervention is addressed to the protectors instead.

The four correspondences the survey claims hold at the primary text. Protectors correspond to the book's figure for the guarding part, which it calls the Gatekeeper; no-bad-parts corresponds to the contraction doctrine; unblending corresponds to meeting that part with respect rather than force; and Self corresponds to the basic practice posture, described in nearly the same terms. The second of these is the closest: Schwartz's finding is that a protective part met with respect rather than overridden will eventually relax, which is the book's own ethic toward protection stated as a clinical result.

## What the evaluation found that the survey did not

The premise of the survey's warning is out of date. IFS is absent from all four descriptive sources and from the vault's roster, but it is not absent from the project. The treatment names Schwartz in the identity-loop constellation (arc-gloss: ch7). The executed draft goes considerably further: it credits the model with "a powerful and clinically useful vocabulary for the inner population of parts," names managers, firefighters, exiles, and the eight Cs, and states Schwartz's finding as its own medicine for that loop — "you do not banish the manager who has been running your life; you befriend it, you learn what it was protecting, you thank it, and you ask if it would be willing to relax" (abridged ch7). The psychedelic-moment material uses the vocabulary again for the gatekeeper's panic (abridged ch12), and the sources note lists both primary texts among the influences on the Stance and the Kata (abridged: a note on sources).

So the book is already speaking the dialect. What it has never done is state where it stands, which changes the task: the open work is a positioning passage placed where the borrowing happens, not a seat added to a roster.

## The refusal worth keeping

The counterweight is evidential rather than doctrinal, and the numbers are specific enough to use. A 2025 scoping review of the IFS literature found twenty-seven studies meeting inclusion criteria, of which two were randomized controlled trials, seventeen were case studies, five quasi-experimental, and three qualitative — against the hundreds of trials behind cognitive behavioural therapy. For post-traumatic stress the controlled evidence reduces to a single uncontrolled pilot. Clinicians also raise a practice-level risk the book inherits directly: parts work handled carelessly can destabilize people with complex trauma.

This bears on how the model can be named accurately. Citing IFS points at a vocabulary and a practice ethic that many readers already speak; it does not point at a settled clinical result, and a citation that reads as though it does would be saying more than the literature says. The distinction matters for pointing rather than for argument: Schwartz's finding about protectors is a clinician's observation offered in a clinician's terms, and naming it as that is both accurate and enough.
