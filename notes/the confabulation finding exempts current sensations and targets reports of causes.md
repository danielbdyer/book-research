# the confabulation finding exempts current sensations and targets reports of causes

The paper most often cited against the idea that a person can read their own interior states does not deny that a person can read their own sensations. Nisbett and Wilson say so in their own voice, in the section where they explain why the illusion of introspective access persists: "we do indeed have direct access to a great storehouse of private knowledge… The individual knows a host of personal historical facts; he knows the focus of his attention at any given point in time; he knows what his current sensations are and has what almost all psychologists and philosophers would assert to be 'knowledge' at least quantitatively superior to that of observers concerning his emotions, evaluations, and plans" (1977, p. 255). The sentence that follows draws the line their whole paper is about: "The only mystery is why people are so poor at telling the difference between private facts that can be known with near certainty and mental processes to which there may be no access at all" (p. 255).

What the paper does attack is stated in its abstract as "higher order cognitive processes" and in its first conclusion as reports about "the effects of particular stimuli on higher order, inference-based responses" (pp. 231, 233). The act they show failing is a person explaining why they responded as they did — why they liked the stockings, why they chose the job, why they could not sleep. The act the book's verification standard asks for is a person noticing that something is present in the body now. Those are two different acts, and the paper puts them on opposite sides of its own line.

This bounds the objection rather than dissolving it, and the boundary has a named cost. The corpus does put the body to work as a judge of causes in at least one place, seating it as the referee between the field's competing accounts of where suffering lives, and a report of that kind asks exactly what the paper says people cannot deliver. Where the book asks the body to certify what happened rather than what is present, the 1977 finding applies at full strength.

## Substantiation

- All three passages above appear at pages 255, 231 and 233 of the paper, which was read complete on 2026-08-07; the reading record with the locators and the citation grammar is `archive/reading — nisbett and wilson, telling more than we can know (1977).md`.
- The vault's prior position, taken at web-citation depth on 2026-08-06 and recorded at [[introspective access is the felt shift's undefended premise]], was that the paper's core concerns causal reports rather than present experience. This claim confirms that half and states the evidence for it in the authors' own words rather than by inference from the abstract.
- The other half of the prior position did not survive the reading, and it is stated separately at [[a large change in evaluation can happen without being experienced as change]], because the paper attacks change-detection in a section of its own.
- The paper's own test for what would count as introspective awareness is stricter than accuracy: "verbal report which exceeds in accuracy that obtained from observers provided with a general description of the stimulus and response in question" (p. 251). A felt shift reported by the person who had it clears that bar trivially, because no observer with a description of the situation can report another person's present sensation at all.
