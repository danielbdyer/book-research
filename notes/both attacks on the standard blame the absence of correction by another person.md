# both attacks on the standard blame the absence of correction by another person

The two papers the vault holds as the strongest published attacks on its verification standard reach the same explanation for why people trust their own readings of themselves, and neither cites the other. Nisbett and Wilson give it a heading, "Inadequate Feedback," and a worked case: an insomniac who believes stress keeps him awake "will always be able to find evidence supporting the view that his life situation is currently stressful," and a good night's sleep in the worst week of his life does not disturb the theory, because he can attribute that to exhaustion (1977, p. 256). Schwitzgebel reaches the same place thirty-one years later and states it as the reason for the confidence itself: "Because no one ever scolds us for getting it wrong about our experience and we never see decisive evidence of error, we become cavalier. This lack of corrective feedback encourages a hypertrophy of confidence" (2008, pp. 259–260).

What makes the agreement useful rather than only interesting is what it implies. If the mechanism of the error is that nothing corrects the reading, then the repair is something that can correct it, and the only correcting instrument either author supplies is another person. Schwitzgebel's own worked example is domestic: his wife says he seems angry about being left the dishes, he reflects sincerely and finds no anger, and "I'm wrong, of course, as I usually am in such situations: My wife reads my face better than I introspect" (2008, p. 252).

So the book's Second Chair gains an argument from its two sharpest opponents rather than from its own lineage. The device already has a lineage in the Talmudic study partner, a mechanism in Gendlin's listener, and a four-century precedent in the Ignatian companion. What it did not have was a reason drawn from the literature that attacks the standard, and this is one: on both attackers' own diagnosis, the solitary reader is in the exact condition that produces unearned confidence.

## Substantiation

- Both passages appear in papers that were read complete on 2026-08-07, and the two records are `archive/reading — nisbett and wilson, telling more than we can know (1977).md` and `archive/reading — schwitzgebel, the unreliability of naive introspection (2008).md`.
- The two arrivals are independent in the ordinary sense and in the vault's stricter one. Nisbett and Wilson are experimental social psychologists writing in 1977; Schwitzgebel is an analytic philosopher writing in 2008 whose paper cites their tradition for a different purpose. Neither derives the feedback diagnosis from the other, and the agreement was not looked for — the vault queued both papers to test whether the standard survives, not to collect agreements.
- The convergence is held as company rather than as corroboration, per `ops/methodology/authorial stance.md`, and it is recorded here because it bears on a design decision rather than because it counts as proof.
- One caution belongs to the claim. Neither author proposes a second person as a remedy, and neither says a listener would make introspective reports accurate. What they supply is the diagnosis; the inference to the Second Chair is the vault's, and it is stated here as an inference.
