# set and setting

The framework holds that a drug does not have an experience; a person in a place does. *Set* is what the person brings — expectation, intention, personality, state — and *setting* is the physical and social environment. Timothy Leary coined the pair in the early 1960s to account for why the same compound produced ecstasy in one room and terror in another; Norman Zinberg, working two decades later on controlled use of heroin and other drugs, made it a research framework and published it as *Drug, Set, and Setting* (1984), where it is joined by a third term, the drug itself, and used to explain why most users of most substances do not become addicted. Ido Hartogsohn's later scholarship traces how set and setting shaped the whole American psychedelic era.

The corpus's own survey states the correspondence and marks it as new: "the Generative Triad is a general theory of set and setting. Wall, Door, Floor are what 'setting' is made of; the Stance is what 'set' is made of. The book is, among other things, the first set-and-setting manual for ordinary Tuesdays. That sentence might belong in the book" (space-around: around the word psychedelic). It names Hartogsohn as "the scholarly spine" and neither Leary nor Zinberg anywhere in the eight documents.

**The relation here is mis-addressed credit.** The insight is not missing: the corpus found the correspondence and recommended it for the book. What is missing is that the framework has authors, a date and a research literature, and that the one name the survey does print is the current scholar rather than either originator. The six relations are set out at [[the apparatus descends from the settlement the book positions itself against]].

The identification also cuts both ways, which the survey does not say. If the Triad is a general theory of set and setting, then set-and-setting research is evidence about the Triad, including the parts of it that would be uncomfortable: Zinberg's finding is that *rituals and social sanctions*, not individual insight, are what make controlled use possible, which relocates the operative factor outside the person and into the group's norms.

**What the framework would refuse.** The generalization, in its strongest form. Set and setting was developed to explain variance in drug experience, and its extension to ordinary life is exactly the move Hartogsohn's own history shows the 1960s making and paying for. Zinberg in particular would refuse the individual address: his controlling variable is the community's rules, which is the structural-cause position the corpus already houses anonymously and now has two more names for.

**The liability.** Leary is the least respectable citation available in this territory and his name carries the whole 1960s failure the book is trying not to repeat. That is an argument for citing Zinberg, whose work is sober, clinical, and about heroin rather than enlightenment — and for naming Leary once as the coiner rather than as an authority.

**One half of this correspondence is quoted and the other is not.** The survey's side is above, anchored. Set and setting is described here from secondary knowledge in the vault's own words, and neither *Drug, Set, and Setting* nor anything of Leary's has been read in this vault. The address is Zinberg's chapters on the three variables and on controlled use.

## What the reading has to settle

Three questions, in the order that decides the most.

- Whether Zinberg's third variable, the drug itself, has an analogue in the book's architecture or whether its absence is the point of the reclamation ([[psychedelic names a movement not a substance]]).
- Whether the rituals-and-sanctions finding is usable or is an objection.
- Whether the survey's identification survives contact with the primary framework or is a resemblance.
