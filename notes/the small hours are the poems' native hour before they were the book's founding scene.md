# the small hours are the poems' native hour before they were the book's founding scene

The book promises its reader that recognition will arrive, and the record documents that event happening exactly once: at 4am, while the author read a decade-old letter from a beloved friend, alone. The poems turn out to own that hour. Poem #7: "in crisp / 4 am bliss, all closed / in and crying – all / breathing and trying." Poem #12: "i don't care that it's 3am." Poem #16 timestamps itself: "current is 1:13 a.m." The seven corpus documents, checked whole, contain no small-hour timestamp at all: the corpus commits to the scale of the day — the Tuesday, the kettle — and never treats the hour, while the poems return to it three times across the collection's span.

The finding gives that scene a history in the author's own writing. When recognition arrived at 4am, it arrived at the hour his own record had already established as the hour of "crying… breathing and trying." What the scene consisted of has been catalogued elsewhere in the vault; what this claim adds is one fact about it, that its hour was not incidental. The author's writing had lived there for years.

## Substantiation

- Poems #7, #12, and #16, quoted above, were verified in the extracted text, and the absence of any small-hour timestamp from the seven corpus documents was checked by word-bounded search across the extractions.
- The record of the 4am scene is the fourth addendum of the provenance reconstruction (§II.2), held under the conditions governing personal material. The scene's standing in the vault is recorded at [[the book's native genre is the letter from a friend read alone]], which finds that the book's one documented occurrence of its promised event happened over a letter read alone and that the book's plainest telling already exists as letters to one friend.
- The same direction of flow extends to the scale of time each layer commits to. [[the tuesday is the temple]] records the corpus's axiom that the book asks for no renunciation and holds ordinary daily life to be the full site of the work; the poems' attention to the middle of the night is the hour-scale complement to that day-scale commitment, and the corpus never took it up.
