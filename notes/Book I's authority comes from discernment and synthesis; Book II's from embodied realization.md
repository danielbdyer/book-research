# Book I's authority comes from discernment and synthesis; Book II's from embodied realization

The differentiation of the two books resolves the author's earlier worry about proving authority by giving each book a different authorial burden. In the first book, the author does not need to have discovered a universal theory of transformation; he needs to demonstrate that he has participated deeply enough across traditions, practices, systems, and encounters to offer a trustworthy ecology to other facilitators, so its authority comes from discernment and synthesis. In the second book, the burden changes: the author needs to demonstrate that he has actually lived the questions he invites the reader to inhabit, so its authority comes less from comprehensiveness and more from embodied realization. The author's own authorship process is therefore structurally relevant to both books, but differently — as breadth of discernment for the one and as lived realization for the other.
