# the poems diagnose a caretaking that converts wanting into service

The collection returns three times, decades apart, to the same account of the speaker's own kindness, and it is not a flattering one. Poem eight states the mechanism plainly and marks the direction of the sentence: "i would care for the world over if it meant i could feel at peace in my own mind" (poems #8) — care for others made instrumental to his own peace. The catalogue that follows is entirely about being received: "how i wanted to keep them all neat and at peace… how i wanted them to love me" (poems #8). The poem's own verdict on itself is two words: "i felt selfish."

Poem fourteen names it as an idolatry and a compulsion rather than a virtue: "my absolutism to love like a naturalized deity has brought me to my knees," and "compulsive thinking towards my compassion has always plagued me" (poems #14). Poem sixteen supplies the exact form, and it is the collection's sharpest sentence about its author: "i am very good at taking care of people / i am very good at listening carefully / at tracking what might land / at shaping myself around what will work" — and then the cost, which is that the caretaking spoke instead of the wanting, "in a register that could be mistaken for service" (poems #16).

The repair the collection reaches for is stated once, at the end of poem eight, and it is a rule about the self rather than about others: "we first must learn how to treat the otherness within ourselves right" (poems #8). The same poem records what made the repair possible — the discovery that release is not erasure: "i had been scared that a letting-go necessarily meant a forgetting. and yet, she was still my friend; my respect for her only grew as i remembered who she was without me" (poems #8).

## Substantiation

- The three statements are separated by long stretches of the collection and arrive in three different registers — narrative recollection (poems #8), retrospective report (poems #14), and spoken address (poems #16) — which is why the recurrence is a finding rather than one poem's mood.
- The last poem's proportion is kept here deliberately: "and the cathedral is real / the cathedral has saved me more than once / and also — i am tired of paying its full invoice" (poems #16). The structure is credited with having worked before it is charged with its cost, and this note does not tidy that order.
- The collection's most exact single line on the resulting confusion is poem sixteen's: "and i had been mistaking love preserved / for presence ongoing / and calling the difference adulthood" (poems #16) — a mechanism, its correction, and the alibi that held it in place, in three lines.
