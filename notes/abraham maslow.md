# abraham maslow

*Peak experience* is Maslow's coinage, introduced in *Religions, Values, and Peak-Experiences* in 1964. He used it to name moments of unitive, self-validating, time-suspending experience, and he attached an argument to it: that such moments are ordinary equipment of a functioning person rather than the property of mystics, and that organized religion is the institutional residue of one person's peak reported to people who have not had one.

Six years later he added a preface correcting himself, and named a second thing — the **plateau experience**. Where the peak is climactic, brief, and given, the plateau is serene, sustained, and built by long living. He argued that the plateau is the more valuable of the two, and that the peak-seeking his own vocabulary had licensed was pointed at the wrong object.

The abridged edition uses *peak experience* as a technical term nine times across chapters eleven and twelve, cites nobody for it, and then states the correction: "The peak experience is not the work. The peak experience is the opening. The work is what you do with the opening" (abridged ch12). To say that, the corpus reaches for the Sufi distinction between *aḥwāl* and *maqāmāt*, states it as "the state visits; the station is built," and credits it. Maslow returns zero across all eight documents.

**The relation here is that the book makes the originator's own correction.** Not a restatement of a construct, which is what Marcia and Minuchin are, and not a lineage cited from the middle down, which is Reich. This is narrower and stranger: the book borrowed a term, inherited the problem the term created, and solved it the way the man who coined it solved it, without knowing he had.

**One half of that correspondence is quoted and the other is not.** The book's side is above, anchored. Maslow's side is stated here from secondary knowledge; no work of his has been read in this vault, and nothing above is his wording. The address for checking it is the 1970 preface and the transcendence essays in *The Farther Reaches of Human Nature*. Until someone reads them, the claim that his correction and the book's are the same correction is proposed rather than established, and it is the whole reason he is here.

## What he would refuse

The hierarchy of needs is absent from this book and he would ask where it went. His account makes peak and plateau available near the top of a prepotency ladder, after safety and belonging are met, which is a claim about *who gets to have these experiences* that the book's householder axiom does not engage.

He would also refuse the plateau read as an achievement of technique. His description is of something that arrives after long living and cannot be arranged, which is the same refusal the book's anti-instrumentality doctrine makes. That makes his objection an ally's rather than an opponent's, and it is the more useful kind here.

## The liability, and the vault's reading of it

Maslow is the founding figure of the human-potential movement, and that is the field walked IX diagnoses for its "radical interiority of cause, its structural inability to hold suffering as meaningful, structural, or genuinely external." Citing him makes the book's descent from that field visible.

The vault's reading, stated as the vault's rather than as a finding: this is a gain. A book that names its parents and says where they went wrong stands better than one that appears to have arrived from nowhere with a new system. The book already has the critique in hand, because walked IX is that sentence.
