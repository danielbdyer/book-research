# the internalization law reaches self-mastery and demands a relation that answers

The book's practices are facilitation moves scaled to one reader, trained until the reader can hold a room for themselves, and the question that decides whether a solitary text can do that has a primary text behind it. Vygotsky's law is that "every function in the child's cultural development appears twice; first, on the social level, and later, on the individual level; first, between people (interpsychological), and then inside the child (intrapsychological)" (Vygotsky 1978, ch. 4, item b). Two things had to be checked before that law could be pointed at a book, and the chapter answers both.

The first is whether the law reaches the right kind of function. It does, on Vygotsky's own classification and in his own instance list. He separates signs from tools by what each is aimed at — a tool "is externally oriented" and works on the world, while a sign "is a means of internal activity aimed at mastering oneself; the sign is internally oriented" — so self-regulation is the category the law is about rather than a stretch from it. And when he states the law he names what it covers: "This applies equally to voluntary attention, to logical memory, and to the formation of concepts" (item b). Voluntary attention is the book's practice under another name, since a posture of presence that one keeps returning to is attention directed on purpose. The transfer from his developmental psychology to the book's practice is therefore an arrival at the same object rather than an analogy between two different ones.

The second is what the law demands of the relation, and here the answer costs the book. Vygotsky writes that "all the higher functions originate as actual relations between human individuals," and his word is *actual*. His worked example makes the other person's response constitutive rather than merely helpful: a child reaching for something out of range is only reaching until "the mother comes to the child's aid and realizes his movement indicates something," after which "the primary meaning of that unsuccessful grasping movement is established by others" — and the gesture "becomes a true gesture only after it objectively manifests all the functions of pointing for others and is understood by others as such a gesture." Somebody has to notice and answer, or the function does not form. He also makes the timing severe: the transformation "is the result of a long series of developmental events," the external form "continues to exist and to change as an external form of activity for a long time before definitively turning inward," and for many functions "the stage of external signs lasts forever."

What this means for a book is inference rather than Vygotsky's finding, because he never considers a text or any partner who is absent. Stated at the strength the evidence supports: a reader who has never had the interpsychological version of this practice with a person who answered is, on Vygotsky's account, missing the stage the inner version is built out of, and no amount of description supplies it. A reader who has had it — anyone who has been well held once, in therapy, in friendship, in a room — is in a different position, and a text may be able to address the inner version they already began.
