# ordinary names keep the deepest structures visible

The three structures were once called Knife, Thread, and Vessel. The author vetoed those names, and the corpus holds the veto as doctrine: "the deepest structures should wear the most ordinary clothes" (atlas §II). The reason is practical. Walked II: if you use ritual-object names, "the reader files them under mythology and walks past the actual walls of the actual room she is sitting in. Name them Wall, Door, Floor, and she cannot leave the building without touching the teaching."

The rule is usable anywhere the manuscript names something. Plain names keep a teaching in the reader's room. Mythic names let her file it away. Walked II ties the rule to the book's larger refusal of renunciation: "The rename enacts the Householder Axiom at the level of vocabulary."

## Substantiation

- Atlas §II: "Formerly Knife / Thread / Vessel — vetoed by the author: 'nothing hides like the always-there.' The rename is itself a doctrine: the deepest structures should wear the most ordinary clothes (≈ Householder Axiom)."
- One caution stays attached. The sentence the veto is remembered by — "nothing hides like the always-there" — was said three nights before the veto, about a different subject. The doctrine is a later distillation, not the reason given at the time. The finding is at [[a recorded reason drifts toward doctrine with distance from the decision]]. It qualifies the origin story, not the rule.
- Both prose drafts still use the vetoed names; the reading instruction is at [[the prose drafts use the vetoed triad names]].
