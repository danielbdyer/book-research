# the poems narrate the not yet lived as already known

The book's front-matter doctrine — you already know this; reading is remembering — was performed in the poems as a grammatical structure before it was stated anywhere. Poem #4 narrates the unlived future in the past tense of accomplished memory: "When I finally learned how to be, / I was thirty three"; "and when I was fifty four I cried / at my son's college graduation"; "I knew at forty-two that living was / for the ages" — and only the final line reveals the position it was all written from: "soon I'll be thirty two." The move recurs at smaller scale in poem #15: "Grow misty-eyed at the thought of old people's reservations at the ballet with me."

The book's doctrine is that its reader already knows its contents and recovers them rather than learning them; the vault's word for that recovery, taken from Heidegger's *aletheia*, is un-forgetting, meaning that what is known but concealed becomes visible again. Poem #4 runs the same movement in the other temporal direction. It writes what has not yet been lived as though it were already remembered, so that the closing line turns the whole poem from a record of the past into something still to come, and does so without altering a word of what precedes it. The book's governing law is that every structural device makes the reading enact what the text explains, and here the enacting came first: the poem performed the doctrine in the author's own craft, unassisted, before any document described it.
