# a signed proof ends perfecting by declaring one impression sufficient

In printmaking the printer pulls trial proofs until the artist signs one *bon à tirer*, "good to pull," and that impression becomes the standard the whole edition must match. The convention exists because proofing has no natural end, and it engineers an exit from perfectibility: a designated moment, a physical object, and a signature that belongs to the artist alone. The panel offered this beside the manuscript's most comfortable room, the perfectible conversation that never has to become answerable ([[the perfectible conversation is the manuscript's most comfortable room]]). The detail worth keeping is that the signed proof is not the best possible impression but the one declared sufficient, so the edition is measured against a declaration rather than against an ideal.

This resonance accompanies the perfectible-room tension rather than testing it: printmaking is company for the shape of a made exit from endless revision, not a prescription. The timing of any such signature is the author's lane, and the vault records the figure to hold rather than to press it ([[ripeness is not refrigeration]]).
