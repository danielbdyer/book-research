# katz's objection reaches what a felt shift proves and not whether anything moved

Steven Katz was moved onto the book's verification standard in the expectation that his constructivism would attack it at its root: if a tradition's concepts shape the experiences its practitioners have, then a reader trained by the book's own vocabulary would produce the book's own felt shift on cue, and the shift would certify the training rather than anything beyond it. His 1978 essay does not make that argument. It makes a narrower one, and it says so on its second page: "it is not being argued either that mystical experiences do not happen, or that what they claim may not be true, only that there can be no grounds for deciding this question, i.e. of showing that they are true even if they are, in fact, true" (Katz 1978, p. 22). What the essay withdraws from mystical experience is evidential force — "no veridical propositions can be generated" from it, and it "is not and logically cannot be the grounds for any final assertions about the nature or truth of any religious or philosophical position" (p. 22). What the essay never touches is whether the person it happened to could tell that it happened.

The distinction matters because the book's own standard already lives on the side Katz leaves alone. The felt shift, at its source, certifies that a process carried forward rather than that a statement corresponds to the world, which is a report of movement and not a claim about truth; and the book's stance withholds evidential claims by the author's own decision, holding the convergence as company rather than as proof. Katz argues that a mystical experience cannot settle a doctrinal question. The book does not ask it to. What remains after his argument runs is the question he declined, which is whether a reader who says something shifted is right that something shifted, and for that question the graph's two experimental adversaries are the sharper instruments: one of them exempts present-tense reports of sensation and aims at reports of causes, and the other builds his case on bodily experience by name and refuses that exemption.

Katz does reach the act of discriminating, in one sentence, and the sentence runs against passivity rather than against accuracy. Arguing that the mystic is not a recording device, he writes that "the creative role of the self in his experience is not analogous to the passive role of the tape-recorder or camera," and that "even in mystical experience, there seems to be epistemological activity of the sort we know as discrimination and integration" (p. 60). He is establishing that discriminating is something the experiencer does, which is the premise his whole essay needs, and he draws no conclusion that what is discriminated is wrong. The answer to the question the vault put to him is therefore that his constructivism reaches a description of an experience far more strongly than it reaches a discrimination of change against no-change, and the gap is his own doing rather than an oversight that a stronger reading would close.
