# whether the medieval page technologies caused silent extractive reading or merely accompanied it is unsettled

Illich's account that the medieval page rewired reading carries a causal claim that a rival scholar disputes. Illich reads the twelfth- and thirteenth-century page technologies — index, alphabetization, silent reading, and the rest — as the hinge of a revolution that turned reading from embodied rumination into silent extraction (the claim is held at [[the scholastic page turned reading from embodied rumination into silent extraction]]). Paul Saenger's history of word-separation dates the onset of silent reading earlier and locates its enabling cause in the separation of words on the page, and he notes that monastic *lectio* and scholastic reading coexisted for a long time rather than one cleanly replacing the other. On Saenger's account the clean twelfth-century rupture Illich narrates is not established.

The question matters because a claim about the book's own medium — that a shift in how text is presented can change what reading does to a reader — leans on whether the medieval case was causal or merely correlational. If the page technologies caused the shift, the precedent supports the proposition that presentation reshapes reading; if they only accompanied a change driven by word-separation or by other forces, the precedent weakens to coincidence. The dig could open neither primary, so the question is recorded as open rather than answered.
