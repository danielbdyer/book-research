# a sealed edge lets intensity concentrate to the point of transformation rather than dissipate

The reader gains the difference between a feeling that drains off half-done and one that finishes its work. In an open space intensity disperses; in a bounded, sealed space it concentrates, and only a concentrated intensity reaches the threshold where it turns into something else. What you gain by containing an experience — not escaping it, not venting it, but holding it inside a made boundary — is the possibility that it completes rather than leaks away. Its use to the writing is an affirmative reason to contain rather than vent: the boundary is the condition under which the experience can finish.

## Substantiation

- Jung: the transformation (the *opus*) requires the *vas bene clausum*, the well-sealed vessel; if it leaks, the contents escape and nothing transmutes; the *temenos*, the "sacred precinct," is the same seal at ritual scale, holding the tension of opposites long enough for a third thing to appear (Psychology and Alchemy CW12; Mysterium Coniunctionis CW14). The mandala as self-made temenos, independently: the psyche spontaneously produces circular, bounded, protective images when under threat of dissolution (CW9i) — that it makes the container unbidden is itself evidence of what the transformation needs.
- First-principles company: any process that depends on crossing a threshold needs a boundary against which the quantity can accumulate; unbounded, it equilibrates with its surroundings and never crosses. The image and the fact keep company; the fact illustrates, it does not prove.
- Refusal kept: the seal is needed because the contents are genuinely potent — the *numinosum*, not a mood — so the vessel is required by the reality of the force it holds, never decorative.
- Standing: nascent; the CW passages are queued.
