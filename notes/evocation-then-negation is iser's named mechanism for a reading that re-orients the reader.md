# evocation-then-negation is iser's named mechanism for a reading that re-orients the reader

Iser names a specific mechanism by which a text produces the participation it seeks: it evokes a familiar assumption in the reader and then negates it, so that an affirmation of the reader's own assumptions leads to the reader's own rejection of them, and a re-orientation follows. The mechanism is page-native — it needs no second person in the room, only a sequence of evoke-then-cancel built into the writing — which is why the dig rates it among the devices most portable to a book read alone. It compels the reader into a creative examination of the reader as well as of the text.

The mechanism carries a built-in cost the book must accept if it uses it: it issues no receipt. The reader who has been re-oriented cannot know what the participation entailed, so the device that produces the shift also withholds any confirmation that it occurred. This is the reader-side face of the book's own limit, that it cannot verify the reader did the work.

## Substantiation

- Iser's 1972 essay, opened directly by the dig [P], states the mechanism at page 295: efficacy is "brought about by the apparent evocation and subsequent negation of the familiar," and an affirmation of the reader's assumptions "leads to our own rejection of them." The dig recorded the page and the phrasing at primary depth.
- Iser states the cost in the same passage, that the reader "cannot know what his participation entails," which the dig also carried at primary depth and which this note holds as the device's price rather than as a separable objection.
