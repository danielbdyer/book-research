# polyphony holds perspectives as genuinely distinct rather than merely equally valid

The reframe conversation gives the word "polyphony" — many voices sounding together while each stays its own — a precise meaning and sets it against a weaker one it rejects. The weak reading is that many perspectives are valid, which the conversation calls too thin to carry the point. The strong reading is that different standpoints disclose different real dimensions of a thing, and the integrity of an inquiry depends on letting those disclosures remain irreducibly different rather than merging them. The conversation's own name for this is "productive non-equivalence": the standpoints are not interchangeable, and their non-interchangeability is what makes them generative, because each shows something the others cannot. This is the positive face of the rule that no standpoint may become sovereign — a facilitator who kept the voices distinct would be doing more than tolerating disagreement, since the difference among them is a source of disclosure and not a problem to be resolved into unanimity.

The term is the author's, and he flagged his own uncertainty about it in the conversation; the note keeps the claim, which is about the structure it names, and holds the word itself as a candidate. What the claim protects is the same thing the comparative method protects: a synthesis that dissolved the voices into one would lose exactly the differences that were disclosing the most.

## Substantiation

The conversation states the contrast directly. The weak reading — "'Many perspectives are valid'" — is marked "too weak" (reframe conversation, 2026-08-13, lines 2576-2578). The strong reading replaces it: "Different loci disclose different dimensions of reality, and the integrity of the inquiry depends upon allowing those disclosures to remain irreducibly different" (line 2582), which the conversation renames the author's task of facilitating their "**productive non-equivalence**" (line 2594). It sits beneath the non-sovereignty rule stated just above it — "**No locus gets to abolish the others**" (line 2568) — so that polyphony is the constructive form of that refusal rather than a separate principle. The author's own use of the word, with his flagged uncertainty about it, is recorded earlier: "that polyphony, um if I'm pronouncing that correctly" (line 436).

Here a locus is a standpoint from which reality is disclosed, the sense the conversation uses when it says each discloses a dimension the others cannot.
