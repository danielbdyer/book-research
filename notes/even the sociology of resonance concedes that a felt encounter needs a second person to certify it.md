# even the sociology of resonance concedes that a felt encounter needs a second person to certify it

Rosa's resonance is meant to name the good relation: being touched, answering in one's own voice, being transformed. He concedes, though, that its felt quality cannot certify itself. Fascist formations, he grants, hold effective techniques of resonance and build what he is willing to describe as zones of resonance, so from inside, an ideological resonance feels like resonance. His guard against this is a discrimination — resonance keeps the difference of the other, whereas echo is fusion and consonance is a silencing harmony — but the dig's finding, following Susen, is that the sort is a normative stipulation rather than a felt distinction: from inside the experience nothing in the feeling marks which one it is. Rosa's own remedy therefore does not rest on the first-person report alone. It requires the first-person report combined with a third-person observer to tell genuine resonance from its ideological counterfeit.

This is resonance sociology arriving, from outside the book's shelves, at the requirement the book already holds from its contemplative traditions and from its adversaries: that a felt shift cannot be certified by the one who feels it and needs a second person. The book's side is stated at [[the second chair is the verification standard's own requirement]] and, from the standard's two sharpest opponents, at [[both attacks on the standard blame the absence of correction by another person]]. Rosa's side is the sociologist's version of the same demand, reached with no contact between them, and its value is exactly its independence: where the adversaries name the second person as the standard's missing correction, Rosa is a friendly witness who needs it too. An independent arrival of this kind is the company the book's central move is built from.
