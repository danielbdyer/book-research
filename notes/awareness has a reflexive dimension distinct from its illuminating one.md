# awareness has a reflexive dimension distinct from its illuminating one

The book holds that awareness does two separable things. One is to illuminate — to make an object of experience present. The other is to turn on itself, so that awareness registers its own operation and asks how it is experiencing rather than only what it experiences. The book takes this pair from the Kashmir Śaiva distinction between consciousness as illumination and consciousness as its own self-apprehension, and its move is to carry that distinction down to the scale of ordinary experience, treating it as something a person undergoes rather than as a metaphysical doctrine about consciousness at large. So the reflexive dimension is not an extra fact known about awareness; it is a capacity of awareness that can be exercised, and the book's interest is in what happens when it is.

## Substantiation

This claim arose in the reframe conversation as the model's structured articulation, engaged by the author; it is recorded as a candidate proposition, not yet substantiated against a primary reading. The claim asserts a correspondence with a tradition, so both sides are marked: the conversation's side is quoted, and the tradition's own side is named through the vault notes that carry its page-level material rather than quoted from memory.

- The conversation's side. The outline names the reflexive movement as a distinct one: reflexive awareness is "the complementary movement toward reflexive awareness/self-apprehension," in which a system "doesn't merely experience" but "can notice: How am I experiencing?" (reframe conversation, § reflexivity). It stages the recursion in steps — "I notice," then "I notice that I notice," then "I recognize something about the structure of my noticing" (reframe conversation, § reflexivity).
- The tradition's side, and the book's move. The outline states the tradition accurately: "In Kashmir Śaiva thought, Prakāśa/Vimarśa distinguishes consciousness as illuminating from its reflexive awareness of itself" (reframe conversation, § reflexivity) — and the distinction is now read at the primary texts. Utpaladeva holds that "the essential nature of light [prakāśa] is reflective awareness [vimarśa]; otherwise light, though 'coloured' by objects, would be similar to an insentient reality, such as the crystal" (Īśvarapratyabhijñākārikā I.5.11, Torella), and "consciousness has as its essential nature reflective awareness" (I.5.13, "citiḥ pratyavamarśātmā"); Kṣemarāja names vimarśa "the spontaneous flashing-forth of the uncontrived 'I'" (Parāpraveśikā, via Singh). So an illumination that could not say "I" would be a mere crystal — the reflexive dimension is what makes light sentient. The founder's own tie between that reflexive awareness and wonder is held, with citations, at [[the recognition school installs wonder at the definition of consciousness]], and the tradition's standing beneath the book at [[the kashmir shaiva tradition underlies the book rather than joining its company]]. The book's own contribution is the change of altitude: the distinction "becomes experiential content, rather than merely doctrine," posed as "What happens when awareness becomes aware of its own participation?" (reframe conversation, § reflexivity). That relocation from doctrine to lived content is the book's re-valuation of the pair, not a claim that the tradition already meant it phenomenologically.
