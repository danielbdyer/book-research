# the threshold is a place one can inhabit rather than only a passage one crosses

The reader gains a real room where we usually see only a gap. The in-between — after you have left the old form and before you have taken the new one — is not empty transit to be hurried through; it is a charged, inhabitable interval whose openness is its gift: pure possibility, the not-yet-fixed, the freedom of the unassigned. You gain permission to dwell in the threshold on purpose for a while, and to receive what only the between offers, because it is bounded and you know you will step out of it. Its use to the writing is to legitimize the book's core wager — that a reader can be walked slowly through a between with no substance and gain from the dwelling itself.

## Substantiation

- van Gennep names the middle phase of every rite of passage the liminal (from *limen*, "threshold"); Turner develops it — liminal beings are "neither here nor there… betwixt and between," and the liminal is "a realm of pure possibility," a fertile chaos out of which new forms are struck (The Rites of Passage, 1909; "Betwixt and Between," 1967; The Ritual Process, 1969). Japanese *ma* (間), independently: the charged interval, the pause that is not the absence of the note but the thing that makes the phrase — the character shows light through the gap of a gate. An aesthetic tradition and an anthropology of ritual on one finding: the interval is a positive presence.
- Refusal kept: Turner insists the liminal is bounded in time and must be exited into reaggregation — permanent liminality is its failure, not its gift; *ma* keeps its own reserve, a felt sensibility resisting method, carried as company across a wide cultural distance.
- Standing: nascent; Turner is solid, *ma* is a reach held as company.
