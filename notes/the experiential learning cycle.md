# the experiential learning cycle

Dewey's argument in *Experience and Education* is that experience is not automatically educative. Some experiences are miseducative — they close off further growth — and what separates the two is whether the experience is reflected on and connected to what follows. He rejected both the traditional model, in which content is transmitted and experience is irrelevant, and the progressive enthusiasm that treated any activity as learning. What makes experience educative is continuity and interaction: it has to lead somewhere and it has to be met.

Kolb formalized it forty years later into a cycle with four stations: concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, active experimentation, returning to experience. The claim carried by the cycle is that skipping a station stalls the learning — an experience unreflected on does not generalize, and a generalization untested does not hold.

**That is the book's integration argument.** "The peak experience is not the work. The peak experience is the opening. The work is what you do with the opening" (abridged ch12), followed by a practice curriculum of twelve repeatable moves. The book supplies the concrete experience and the active experimentation and argues for the two middle stations without naming the tradition that established them. Dewey and Kolb both return zero across the eight documents.

**What the tradition would refuse.** Kolb's cycle is a claim about *skill*, and it has been criticized for exactly the extension the book makes — that a cycle validated on learning to do things transfers to becoming someone. Dewey would press the social half harder: his continuity and interaction are conditions a *teacher* arranges, and his whole educational philosophy is about the environment that makes growth possible rather than about the learner's private discipline. And both would refuse the peak's centrality. Dewey has no category for the transformative episode; on his account growth is unspectacular, cumulative, and made of ordinary experiences well connected — which is either an objection to the book's arc or the strongest available support for its householder axiom, and the reading decides which.

**The load, and the honest size of it.** With `precedent`, and this is the loosest of the provenance finds. There is no borrowed term here, no construct the book states under someone else's word. What there is is a century-old tradition holding the answer to the book's central question — why the enormous experience does not hold — and never appearing. The nearest thing to a construct debt is oblique: Donald Schön, already chaired here for the participation law, is Kolb's contemporary and a direct Dewey descendant, so part of this lineage is seated without its head ([[donald schon]]).

**The evaluation is from secondary knowledge.** What the reading has to settle: whether Dewey's *miseducative experience* names the same failure as the book's un-integrated peak, which would make this a construct correspondence rather than a tradition-level one; whether Kolb's cycle has survived its own literature's criticism well enough to cite; and whether the tradition supplies anything the book's own two mechanisms for the failing peak do not ([[an enormous experience is interpreted by the structure it did not change]] and [[the plasticity window reorganizes only what attention is on]]).
