# the close repeats the opening's exit with one sentence inserted

The executed draft builds its ending out of its beginning by making one insertion. The prologue exits: "The clearing was always there. / Step in." The epilogue exits: "The clearing was always there. / You have always been ready. / Step in." Everything the reader gained by reading the book is carried by that one added sentence, because the clearing is described in identical words at both ends. What the draft claims to have changed is not the clearing but the reader's readiness to walk into it, and that single sentence is the whole of what the book says it accomplished.

The epilogue also contains the draft's only sustained passage of future-tense second person, and that passage walks the reader through their next hour almost entirely in tautologies: "You will close the book and walk into your kitchen. The kitchen will be a kitchen. You will make a cup of tea. The tea will be tea." The entire change the passage claims sits in one adverbial phrase, "very faintly, more present to you than they were before." The tautologies hold the promise down to what the book can honestly deliver: the kitchen and the tea will be exactly what they already were, and the one difference claimed is faint, available only to the reader's own feeling, and easy for that reader to deny.

## Substantiation

- Both exit passages were verified word for word in the extracted text, and the two differ by exactly the inserted sentence. Three tautological predicate pairs were verified in the epilogue, covering the kitchen, the tea, and the sitting.
- Anyone can check this by setting the two exits side by side, and by searching for future-tense second person elsewhere in the draft, which should turn up nothing sustained outside the epilogue.
- The corpus states as the book's design law that the last line is written so that it can be answered, and this pair of exits carries that law out, because the epilogue hands the reader the step rather than taking it. The same ending shape predates the book: four poems close by naming an act — a goodbye, a listening — and leaving it unperformed ([[the poems end by naming the next act and leaving it undone]]).
