# state and station were distinguished a millennium before the lattice named them

The book already declares one distinction as its protection against spiritual bypass: *ahwal* against *maqamat*, states against stations. In al-Qushayrī's formulation, read at Knysh's translation, "States are [divine] gifts, whereas stations are earnings. States come without asking, whereas stations are acquired by the sweat of one's brow. The possessor of a station is firmly established in it, whereas the possessor of a state can be taken up out of his state [at any moment]" (Knysh 2007, p. 78). A state may be visited on a person who has built nothing that holds it, and the visitation does not establish them.

That is the non-transfer rule beneath the modern claim that a state is interpreted by the structure that receives it ([[an enormous experience is interpreted by the structure it did not change]]), reached roughly a thousand years earlier, in a tradition with no contact with either pole of the book's founding convergence. It is therefore an independent arrival in the strict sense the book needs and the sort finds thin: not Heidegger's students, not the recognition school, not anyone reading either. Both texts now read, the arrival is narrower than the grid stated it and better defended. The two schemes agree that a transient visitation is not a durable attainment. They diverge on where each comes from and on whether the two interact: al-Qushayrī's states are gifts along a single ordered path, gated on stations already fulfilled, where Wilber's states are the natural states of waking, dreaming, and deep sleep, available at every stage "because even the lowest stages wake, dream, and sleep" (*Integral Spirituality* draft, p. 52), and where Wilber holds that repeated state practice accelerates growth through the stages. Neither text holds the strict independence of two axes; that phrasing was the grid's.

Two witnesses to one rule is materially different from one. The book currently carries the distinction as a borrowed warning; the sort's finding is that it is a place where the book is, unusually, well supported — and the reading adds the negative half of the independence: Wilber did not take the lattice from the Sufis.

## Substantiation

- **Both halves are read.** Al-Qushayrī at Knysh's 2007 translation, whole, Grade A (the record is `archive/reading — al-qushayri, the risala on state and station (knysh 2007).md`); Wilber at five primary texts — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality* complete, the 2005 draft of *Integral Spirituality*, the 2006 *AQAL Journal* article with real pagination, "Waves, Streams, States, and Self," and Excerpt A (the record is `archive/reading — wilber at the primary texts (ses, the integral spirituality draft, the aqal journal, waves, excerpt a).md`). Allan Combs's own statement of the lattice remains unread; Wilber's account gives himself priority and Combs an independent later arrival at the same idea (draft, p. 51).
- **The Sufi geometry is one road, not two axes.** States are ranked and gated on prior progress — contraction and expansion "come after the servant has advanced above the state of fear and hope" (p. 79) — and a state is ranked against a station on one scale: "expansion is above the station of hope" (pp. 81–82). Access to a state is conditioned on stations fulfilled (p. 95), the visitations "come from prayers" (p. 84), and the one item traced across the boundary, satisfaction, travels station → state (p. 206). A state that endures becomes stability, *tamkīn* (pp. 79, 100–102), not a station. Two independent axes cannot produce the sentence "expansion is above the station of hope."
- **Wilber's geometry is two distinct, non-equatable axes that interact asymmetrically.** "Any person at any stage can have a peak experience of a gross, subtle, causal, or nondual state. But a person will interpret that state according to the stage they are at" (draft, p. 53 — "That bold sentence was for us early researchers the breakthrough and real turning point"). But "with repeated practice of contacting higher states, your own stages of development will tend to unfold in a much faster and easier way… whereupon a passing state has become a permanent trait" (*AQAL Journal* 1(1), p. 12), and the asymmetry: "You can have a peak experience of higher states, because many of them are ever-present. But you cannot have a peak experience of a higher stage, because stages unfold sequentially" (p. 12).
- **Independence of origin, on the negative side.** *Qushayri*, *ahwal*, *maqamat*, and *maqam* occur zero times across the five Wilber texts; "Sufi" occurs once in SES, in a passing list with no states-and-stations content. Wilber does use the word "station," in the ordinary English sense and of the stage — "every stage is also a station in life" (draft, p. 97) — which a vault reader could mistake for Sufi borrowing. It is not.
- The grid's own statement of the finding: "arrived at a millennium before Wilber and entirely independently. States descend as gifts; stations are earned by the sweat of the brow. Two independent witnesses to the same structure is materially stronger than one" (`mesh grid: other names recovered`). The first sentence is confirmed at both texts; "the same structure" is corrected above to the same rule.
- The vault's rule on how that last sentence is used is `ops/methodology/authorial stance.md`, and it applies here without softening: the second witness is recorded as company rather than as corroboration, and the vault does not rank this convergence as stronger evidence than any other. What it does record is a fact about the reading — the rule has an arrival from outside the room, which most of the book's claims do not ([[the convergence's independence fails where three of its names share one teacher]]).
- The book's own use of the distinction is as the bypass guard, which makes this note's second function diagnostic. If the same rule appears in a Sufi manual, a modern developmental lattice, and the book's own architecture, the question of what the book adds to it is live and unanswered here.
- The same finding partially fills a second hole. The sort records that the claim about ordinary life — that this is livable without renunciation — has no independent witness from outside the Indic world, and the Sufi path is lay practice, under a master the text makes non-optional (Knysh 2007, pp. 405–406).
