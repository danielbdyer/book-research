# a practice reconditions the frame rather than clearing it

Steven Katz's 1978 essay makes a claim about what contemplative training does, and it is the claim from that essay that costs the book something. Practice does not empty the practitioner of the concepts they arrived with; it exchanges those concepts for others. In his words: "Properly understood, yoga, for example, is not an unconditioning or deconditioning of consciousness, but rather it is a reconditioning of consciousness, i.e. a substituting of one form of conditioned and/or contextual consciousness for another, albeit a new, unusual, and perhaps altogether more interesting form of conditioned-contextual consciousness" (Katz 1978, p. 57). He states the negative half beside it — "There is no substantive evidence to suggest that there is any pure consciousness per se achieved by these various, common mystical practices, e.g. fasting, yoga, and the like" (p. 57) — and generalizes both at the next turn of the argument: "the mystic even in his state of reconditioned consciousness is also a shaper of his experience; that he is not a tabula rasa on which the 'ultimate' or the 'given' simply impinges itself" (p. 59).

The book's own practice is presence without agenda while remaining available to act, and its instruction is a point of return rather than an achieved state. Katz's finding does not contradict that instruction, and it prices it. A reader who runs the book's practice arrives at a consciousness conditioned by the book's vocabulary rather than at a consciousness conditioned by nothing, which means the reader's later reports come from inside the frame the reading installed. The exposure is sharpest where the book's promise is largest: the doctrine says deep change is disclosure of something already present rather than acquisition of something new, and Katz's account of practice describes acquisition — of a new frame — at exactly the place where the doctrine claims disclosure.

Two things keep this from settling anything. Katz makes an epistemological argument from the mystical literature rather than a measurement, so what he establishes is that no evidence compels the pure-consciousness reading, not that the reconditioning reading is demonstrated. And his own conclusion runs to comparison rather than to efficacy: he is arguing that a Jewish mystic's experience and a Buddhist's are two experiences rather than one, and the reconditioning claim is a step in that argument rather than a verdict on whether practice helps anybody. What the book owes him is a sentence conceding that its practices hand a reader a vocabulary, held beside its claim that what the vocabulary points at was already there.

## Substantiation

- The reading is recorded at `archive/reading — katz, language, epistemology, and mysticism (1978).md`, which names the pages read at their own text. All three quotations come from pages 57 and 59 of the 1978 Oxford edition.
- Katz's argument at these pages runs through yoga across the Indian traditions, and his evidence is that the same technique serves incompatible goals: the Upanishadic practitioner aims at union with Brahman, the Samkhya tradition defines the goal differently, and Buddhism puts the same technique at the service of emptiness while denying both a personal God and a substantial self (pp. 56–58). His inference is that a practice cannot be described in the abstract without becoming an empty abstraction.
