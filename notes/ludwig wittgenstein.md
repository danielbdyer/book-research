# ludwig wittgenstein

The book's foundation is that what is sought was never absent, and that the work is un-forgetting rather than acquisition ([[aletheia and pratyabhijna converge on truth as un-forgetting]]). A foundation of that shape needs an account of why something never absent is not noticed, and the book's own answer is architectural: the wall, the door and the floor of a room are invisible from inside it precisely because they are always there.

*Philosophical Investigations* §129 states the same principle as a general remark about noticing: the aspects of things most important to us are hidden by their simplicity and familiarity, and a person fails to notice something because it is always before their eyes. Wittgenstein wrote it in the late 1940s, published posthumously in 1953, from inside a tradition with no Heidegger in it and no contact with the recognition school.

## Why the sort wants him

Two reasons, and the second is the load-bearing one.

The correspondence itself is unusually exact — not a family resemblance between two vocabularies but the same claim, and the grid calls it "the single cleanest found correspondence in the grid" (`mesh grid: tier 3`), adding that the book does not know it has it.

More importantly, he passes a test the book's company mostly fails. Three of the four names positioned around the founding convergence studied with, translated, or built their systems against the same German ([[the convergence's independence fails where three of its names share one teacher]]). Wittgenstein is outside that entirely, and his discipline is one of three the sort finds near-empty across every load — which the grid reads as a fact about the reading rather than about the field: "It is the shape of the reading that has happened so far, and the grid's most useful output is making that shape visible from outside" (`mesh grid: the family-level read`).

## What he would refuse

Derived rather than sourced; the passage has not been read in the original here, and the refusal is the reason the reading has to be the passage rather than the sentence.

§129 sits inside an argument about philosophical method, and its point is that the confusions being dissolved are confusions produced by language going on holiday — not concealments of anything. Wittgenstein's next moves are that philosophy leaves everything as it is, that it neither explains nor deduces, and that what is hidden is of no interest to philosophy. He would refuse the ontological reading flatly: for him the always-there that goes unnoticed is grammar, and noticing it dissolves a false problem rather than disclosing a truth. The book's whole claim is that noticing discloses something that was there to be disclosed.

That refusal is not a footnote to the correspondence; it is the second half of it. Two people said the same sentence about noticing and meant incompatible things about what noticing yields — which is a genuinely interesting fact and a more honest one than an unqualified agreement would be.

## What the evaluation still requires

§§89–133 read whole, with two questions: whether the remark can be detached from the method it serves without becoming a different claim; and whether *On Certainty*'s treatment of the hinge propositions — the things not doubted because everything else turns on them — is the closer parallel to the book's floor than §129 is. The second may be the more valuable of the two and the grid does not mention it.
