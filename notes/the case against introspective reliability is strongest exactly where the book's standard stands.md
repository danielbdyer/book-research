# the case against introspective reliability is strongest exactly where the book's standard stands

The book asks a reader to tell, from the inside, that something has moved in the body. The philosopher whose whole subject is whether people can tell such things builds his case on that exact material. Schwitzgebel's first extended argument is about emotion, and the features he says elude us are the bodily ones: whether joy "is sometimes in the head, sometimes more visceral, sometimes a thrill, sometimes an expansiveness — or, instead, does joy have a single, consistent core"; whether "emotional consciousness is simply the experience of one's bodily arousal"; whether "emotional experience is consistently located in space (for example, particular places in the interior of one's head and body)" (2008, p. 249). His verdict on that material is blunt: "Even the grossest features of emotional experience largely elude us. Reflection doesn't remove our ignorance, or it delivers haphazard results" (p. 249).

He then refuses the escape the other canonical attack leaves open. Nisbett and Wilson exempt what a person feels now from what a person says about causes, and Schwitzgebel names that move and rejects it: "We may concede the past to the skeptic, but not the present," he writes, and answers that the claim "just seems blatantly unrealistic" (pp. 250–251). The exemptions he does allow are a vivid canonical pain and a bright foveal colour, and he refuses to let them carry the argument, because "to use these cases only as one's inference base rigs the game" (p. 259). A felt shift is neither a vivid canonical pain nor a bright colour.

What keeps this from being a dismissal is his own stated position, and it is a sentence the book can stand beside. He holds that introspection of current conscious experience is "both (i) possible, important, necessary for a full life, and central to the development of a full scientific understanding of the mind, and (ii) highly untrustworthy" (p. 246). He locates the meditative traditions and Titchener as holding both halves as well, and states his difference with them exactly: they "take comfort in optimism about introspection 'properly' conducted," and his question about them is "Might they be right in their doubts but less so in their hopes?" (p. 246). That question is addressed to a book with a practice curriculum, and it is the one the book has to answer.

## Substantiation

- All quotations come from the 2008 paper read complete on 2026-08-07; the record with the page locators is `archive/reading — schwitzgebel, the unreliability of naive introspection (2008).md`. *Perplexities of Consciousness* (2011), which the reading queue names, was not read; the 2008 article is the paper the book's chapter of the same title develops.
- The vault's own evaluation of him, written from the mesh grid's characterization and recorded at [[eric schwitzgebel]], predicted this correctly, including the observation that his project leaves a repair open rather than closing the question. That characterization held at the primary text, which is worth recording beside the Nisbett and Wilson reading, where half of a characterization did not.
- The two canonical attacks do not overlap and together they cover both halves of what the book asks. Nisbett and Wilson attack the report of a cause and exempt the report of a present sensation ([[the confabulation finding exempts current sensations and targets reports of causes]]); Schwitzgebel attacks the report of the present sensation.
- One limit belongs to the claim. Schwitzgebel attacks judgments about the character and the presence of a current experience, and he never treats the narrower comparative judgment that something is different now from a moment ago. The implication runs against the standard rather than being argued: a person who cannot reliably say whether they are angry is poorly placed to say whether something shifted.
