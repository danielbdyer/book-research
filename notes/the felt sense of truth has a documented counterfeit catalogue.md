# the felt sense of truth has a documented counterfeit catalogue

The corpus's one-sentence warning — "the click can lie" — now has its literature, and the literature is a catalogue rather than a caveat. Ease of processing is misread as truth: statements shown in easier-to-read contrast are judged true above chance on presentation alone (Reber & Schwarz 1999), and the fluency account unifies a swarm of such effects — fluent stimuli are judged "more true, more likeable, more familiar, more confident" (Alter & Oppenheimer 2009). Form is misread as accuracy: rhyming aphorisms are judged more accurate than their meaning-identical non-rhyming twins (McGlone & Tofighbakhsh 2000). Decoration is misread as evidence: a non-probative photograph beside a claim raises the rate at which the claim is judged true (Newman et al. 2012). And the feeling of insight itself misfires: in the study built to test it, thirty-seven percent of wrong solutions received aha ratings above the average for correct ones (Danek & Wiley 2017), while artificially induced aha moments made unrelated statements — including false ones — feel more true (Laukkonen et al. 2020). Every entry is the same shape: a bodily-felt yes, produced by the vehicle rather than the cargo.

Two details in the catalogue matter more to this book than the catalogue itself. First, **the discernment is teachable**: the rhyme-as-reason effect *attenuated when participants were warned* to separate poetic form from content (McGlone & Tofighbakhsh 2000) — which means the book can train the very discrimination its prose endangers, and the training has a measured precedent. Second, **the counterfeit is weaker than the genuine article, on average**: the same false-insight studies found aha intensity reliably higher for correct solutions than wrong ones (Danek & Wiley 2017; replicated in Grimmer et al. 2022) — the feeling is not a guarantee, but it is not noise either, which is the honest middle the book's honesty section needs. The claim to write against is "the aha does not *certify*," never "the aha means nothing."

What this catalogue is for, in the book's terms: the second reader — fluent, vocabularied, armored — is precisely the reader most exposed to vehicle-produced yeses. A book that names these mechanisms, in the reader's presence, about its own sentences, converts its most dangerous property into a practice surface.

## Substantiation

- The catalogue's honest edges are recorded with it: the aha-intensity finding cuts both ways and is kept whole above; and the fluency literature's effect sizes are lab-scale, measured on trivia statements and aphorisms rather than on transformative prose — the extension to a book like this one is an inference the vault marks as its own.
- The disfluency inverse exists and matters for the plain-prose treatment: needless complexity lowers judged intelligence and trust, mediated by fluency (Oppenheimer 2006) — plainness is not merely a stylistic virtue but the low-counterfeit register.
- The truthiness effect generalizes beyond photos to words (Newman et al. 2012, same paper), so the mechanism is decorative *context*, not imagery as such — marginal apparatus is not exempt.
