# the executed draft introduces each thinker by the duration of their labor

The convergence-of-thinkers centerpiece has a sentence-level formula in the executed draft. A thinker arrives with the duration of their labor stated first, then the finding compressed to one sentence: "Christopher Alexander spent his career trying to name this. He came to the conclusion, late in his life, that it could not be named. He kept trying anyway" (abridged ch1). "Eugene Gendlin's life work, distilled into a single sentence, was this: what is split off, not felt, remains the same… He arrived at this through decades of empirical work" (abridged ch5). Where a refusal exists, it is kept inside the introduction — Alexander's late conclusion that the quality could not be named is his refusal, seated in the same breath as his finding.

The duration clause does the centerpiece's work in miniature: it certifies that the finding was arrived at rather than asserted, which is the difference between company and citation, and it prices the finding at a career so the one-sentence compression reads as distillation rather than as slogan.

## Substantiation

- Seven introductions verified carrying the duration clause: Heidegger, Alexander, Buber, Gendlin, Meadows, Berry, Oliver, at their chapters in the extracted text; the Alexander and Gendlin specimens are quoted above.
- Checkable: enumerate the draft's in-text thinker introductions and count those with a duration clause ("spent his career," "life work," "the better part of a life") preceding a one-sentence finding; introductions built any other way are the exceptions to find.
- The design law this executes is [[the company is joined by the reader rather than consulted]]: a person whose labor has a length is someone at the table, not an authority in a footnote.
