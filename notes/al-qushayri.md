# al-qushayrī

Abū al-Qāsim al-Qushayrī compiled the *Risāla* in the eleventh century as a defence and systematization of the Sufi path for a readership that had begun to distrust it, and its most durable contribution is a distinction rather than a doctrine. A *ḥāl* — a state — descends: "something that descends upon the hearts [of the mystics] regardless of their intentions, their [attempts to] attract it, or their [desire to] earn it" (Knysh 2007, p. 78). A *maqām* — a station — is built and held: "the good manners which the servant of God realizes after he has entered it. He can arrive at it by means of his own actions, by fulfilling its requirements through [concerted] effort and self-imposed strictures in worshiping [God]" (p. 77). The formula the vault had carried as the grid's paraphrase is the translator's own wording: "States are [divine] gifts, whereas stations are earnings. States come without asking, whereas stations are acquired by the sweat of one's brow. The possessor of a station is firmly established in it, whereas the possessor of a state can be taken up out of his state [at any moment]" (p. 78).

What the text does not say is what the vault had said for it. The two are not two axes. States are themselves ranked and gated on prior progress — contraction and expansion "come after the servant has advanced above the state of fear and hope" (p. 79) — and he ranks a state against a station in one sentence: "expansion is above the station of hope" (pp. 81–82). Access to a state is conditioned on stations already fulfilled (p. 95), and the visitations themselves "come from prayers" (p. 84). What he holds is the narrower rule the book actually needs: a state does not establish the person who receives it.

The practical use of the distinction is diagnostic, aimed at one failure: mistaking a visitation for an attainment. His sources say it as a maxim — "The spiritual states are like lightning. When they last, this is nothing but self-deception and the prompting of one's lower nature" (Abū ʾl-Ḥasan b. al-Ṣāʾigh, p. 59) — and he takes a side against the maxim's momentarism: states can endure, and a state that stays becomes stability, *tamkīn*, "a man's personal portion in which he will grow" (p. 79), rather than a station. The one item he traces across the boundary, satisfaction (*riḍā*), travels the other way — a station at the beginning of the path and a state at its end, "and, therefore, can no longer be acquired" (p. 206).

## Why the sort wants him

The book already declares this distinction as the thing protecting it from spiritual bypass — it names *ahwal* and *maqamat* directly. What it does not currently record is that the distinction is an independent arrival at a rule the book needs for a second job entirely: the same rule, in modern dress, is what explains why an enormous experience leaves an unchanged person unchanged ([[an enormous experience is interpreted by the structure it did not change]]). The book holds it as a warning and could hold it as a mechanism. The reading narrows what the arrival is: the *Risāla* and the modern lattice share the non-transfer rule and not the geometry, since his states and stations lie along one ordered path where the lattice crosses two axes ([[state and station were distinguished a millennium before the lattice named them]]). Two witnesses to one rule, from traditions with no contact, is recorded as company rather than corroboration, under `ops/methodology/authorial stance.md`. He also partly fills a second hole: the claim that this is livable in an ordinary life has no independent witness outside the Indic world, and his path is lay practice — under a required master, which is the price he attaches.

## What he would refuse

Read at the text (2026-09-02); the refusals below are his own words.

**The distinction's detachment from what it is a distinction within.** The terms exist partly to conceal the path from outsiders, "For their realities cannot be assembled by self-exertion or acquired by any deliberate action" (p. 75); the stations are moral and devotional achievements — repentance, patience, trust, contentment, the contents of Chapter 3 (pp. 111–338) — rather than developmental capacities. A book that takes the structure and leaves the destination has taken a diagnostic tool out of the practice that gives it its content.

**Self-formation, not self-reading.** The vault had recorded that he would refuse the reader's authority because a person in a state is the worst-placed judge of it, which is why a shaykh is required. That is not in the text. The distinction is taught to the aspirant as a first-person tool — the terminology chapter is written "to facilitate the understanding of the meaning of these terms by those who want to follow their [Sufi] path" (p. 75) — and where he stages the question directly, the disciple's own inner criterion wins and the master concedes: "'If I find my heart satisfied with God Most High, I learn that He too is satisfied with me!' The master said: 'You have spoken well, young man!'" (p. 207). What he requires the shaykh for is the novice's formation: "the novice must be educated in good manners by a [Sufi] master. If he does not have one, he will never succeed. Abu Yazid said: 'He who does not have a master, Satan is his leader'" (p. 405); "The tree that has grown on its own without a gardener will bring forth foliage, but will never bring any fruit" (p. 406). That refusal is still aimed at a book read alone, and it is a refusal from inside the contemplative literature — but it is a refusal of a road walked without a companion, not of a reader who trusts the body's report.

**The reader who asks the teacher for reasons.** "Whoever asks his teacher 'why?' will not prosper" (p. 340).

**Spectacular experience as evidence of anything.** "When you see a man who is endowed with miracles to such an extent that he can sit on the air, do not be deluded by him until you have tested his [attitude toward] what is permitted and what is prohibited" (p. 33).

## What the reading settled and what it left

The evaluation had asked for three things. The exact wording of the formula is settled at p. 78, Grade A. Whether the tradition treats state–station independence as strictly as the modern lattice is settled the other way: a single ordered path, with states gated on stations and the gift conditioned on the labour, so the isomorphism is the non-transfer rule alone. The third question — whether the corpus's own use of *ahwal* and *maqamat* was drawn from a source or arrived at second-hand — is a question about the corpus rather than the *Risāla*, and stays open; it decides whether this is an arrival the book found or one it inherited. The Arabic of the formula stays at Grade B until a printed page is in hand. The record is `archive/reading — al-qushayri, the risala on state and station (knysh 2007).md`.

## What this sorts — the author's "a level above"

The author's reflection describes a recognition "always already there, but just now opened-with… a level above the disparity of singularly/seminally/individually feeling the recognition, even if I am the only one recognizing" (`reflections/on recognition (2026-08-24).md`). By the *Risāla*'s criteria the recognition sorts as a *ḥāl*, and cleanly: it arrived "regardless of their intentions, their [attempts to] attract it, or their [desire to] earn it" (p. 78), and nothing in his account describes a thing he built. "A level above" does not sort as station language in his idiom. He uses "above" for states — awe above contraction, contraction above fear, intimacy above expansion — and in the same breath ranks a state above a station (p. 81); height names the altitude of a visitation, not its tenure. What would make something a *maqām* is that it is held and paid for. So the phrase, read through this text, describes a visitation of high rank rather than a standing. Two consequences follow. A state that stayed does not become a station here but stability, so if the book wants to say that a recognition which endured became something the reader owns, al-Qushayrī supplies *tamkīn* rather than station as the name. And what he gives the book intact is the guard: the question to put to a recognition is not how high it went but whether anything was built that could hold it — the question [[an enormous experience is interpreted by the structure it did not change]] asks from the other side of the gift-and-earning line. This is a finding about the idiom; whether the author means a station or a relation is his sounding.
