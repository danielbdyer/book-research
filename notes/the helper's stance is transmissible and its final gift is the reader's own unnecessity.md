# the helper's stance is transmissible and its final gift is the reader's own unnecessity

The reader gains the largest affirmative in the lane: the non-doing is not a role reserved for an expert but a stance the reader can learn to hold — toward themselves, and toward others — so that the good helper builds toward being no longer needed. The finger points the reader not only at the moon but at their own capacity to point. What the helper models by standing back is a posture the reader carries out the door, which is why the helper's dispensability is designed rather than regretted. Its use to the writing is the affirmative payoff the book most wants: a reader who learns the facilitator's non-doing becomes able to facilitate their own remembering, and another's.

## Substantiation

- Montessori: dispensability is the goal — the children "working as if I did not exist." Zen: the teacher is to be forgotten once the moon is seen; the method self-cancels. Plato, Theaetetus: Socrates' associates go on to "bring forth" on their own and depart.
- First-principles: if the helper installs nothing and instead frees a capacity already present, then what the person acquires is not content that keeps them dependent but a *stance* they now hold themselves; a stance, once held, needs no external holder — so the helper's own redundancy is entailed by the method, not added to it. This connects to the book's third move, one person's participation making another more able to belong to themselves.
- Refusals kept: Montessori's dispensability is earned through exacting preparation, not achieved by walking away early; Zen's "forget the teacher" is not contempt for the pointing that got you there.
- Standing: a reach; the leap to "the reader becomes their own facilitator" is inference, kept clear of the peer-relationship lane.
