# wonder is not the novice's state to be outgrown but the native air of the awake

A quiet teaching says wonder is for children and beginners — that the grown, the expert, and the finished have moved past it into mere knowing. The pulse says otherwise: amazement is not where a person starts and leaves but where the living stay, and it grows with knowledge rather than dissolving under it, because the more one actually sees, the more astonishing it becomes. This is a permission and a promise at once — a reader is allowed to be amazed, still, at what they know best, and the amazement is the sign of being awake rather than a mark of immaturity.

## Substantiation

- Several traditions place wonder at the root. Plato calls wonder (thaumazein) "the very feeling of a philosopher" (Theaetetus 155d); Aristotle says it is through wonder that people began to philosophize (Metaphysics 982b). The Śiva Sūtra makes it the terrain of the path itself — "vismayo yoga-bhūmikāḥ," wonder is the stages of yoga (I.12). Heschel holds that "to be spiritually alive one must be radically amazed" (God in Search of Man, 1955). All attributed and cited by address, to verify.
- Refusal kept, and the disagreement is the honesty: this is company, not consensus. Aristotle's wonder is meant to be dissolved by knowledge — the geometer would wonder more if the diagonal turned out commensurable (Metaphysics 983a) — which contradicts Heschel and vismaya, for whom wonder deepens and abides. Heschel's amazement points beyond the self and refuses to become a self-administered technique. The book sides with the wonder-that-abides lineage and says so, rather than smoothing the four voices into agreement.
