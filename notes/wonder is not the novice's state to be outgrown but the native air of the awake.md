# wonder is not the novice's state to be outgrown but the native air of the awake

A quiet teaching says wonder is for children and beginners — that the grown, the expert, and the finished have moved past it into mere knowing. The pulse says otherwise: amazement is not where a person starts and leaves but where the living stay, and it grows with knowledge rather than dissolving under it, because the more one actually sees, the more astonishing it becomes. This is a permission and a promise at once — a reader is allowed to be amazed, still, at what they know best, and the amazement is the sign of being awake rather than a mark of immaturity.

## Substantiation

- Several traditions place wonder at the root; two poles are now read at the primary texts. Plato: "wonder is the only beginning of philosophy, and he who said that Iris was the child of Thaumas [wonder] made a good genealogy" (Theaetetus 155d). The Śiva Sūtra makes wonder the terrain of the path itself — "vismayo yogabhūmikāḥ," "The stations and stages of yoga constitute a fascinating wonder" (I.12, Singh) — and Kṣemarāja's Vimarśinī glosses it as the great yogin's "ever-new" (nava-nava) camatkāra, held "constantly" (nityaṃ) and "again and again" (muhur muhuḥ): wonder that deepens and abides rather than a stage outgrown, sealed by the Kulayukti verse "when the Self is known by the Self … the Self beholds wonder in the Self alone." The remaining poles are still owed: Aristotle says it is through wonder that people began to philosophize (Metaphysics 982b, unread); Heschel holds that "to be spiritually alive one must be radically amazed" (God in Search of Man, 1955; from memory) — both grounding owed.
- Refusal kept, and the disagreement is the honesty: this is company, not consensus. Aristotle's wonder is meant to be dissolved by knowledge — the geometer would wonder more if the diagonal turned out commensurable (Metaphysics 983a, unread) — which contradicts Heschel and vismaya, for whom wonder deepens and abides; and Plato's own wonder is a *beginning* to move through rather than a state to keep, so the book's wonder-that-abides is a real divergence from him. Heschel's amazement points beyond the self and refuses to become a self-administered technique. The book sides with the wonder-that-abides lineage and says so, rather than smoothing the voices into agreement.
- Standing: privated — the Plato and Śiva Sūtra poles read at the primary texts (Theaetetus 155d; Śiva Sūtra I.12 with Kṣemarāja's Vimarśinī; records in `archive/`); the Aristotle and Heschel poles still owed. The Śaiva witness is the strongest for the wonder-that-abides side, which the note takes over Aristotle's wonder-dissolved-by-knowledge.
