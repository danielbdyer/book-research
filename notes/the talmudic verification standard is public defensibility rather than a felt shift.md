# the talmudic verification standard is public defensibility rather than a felt shift

The Talmudic tradition verifies a reading by a standard that is not a felt shift at all. When the dig audited each tradition against its own test of whether an understanding has genuinely arrived, the Talmud and its paired study returned a public criterion: the test is whether a student can articulate the *sugya* — the argument-unit of the passage — and hold a reading under the partner's challenge and the teacher's questioning, spoken aloud in the study hall. The evidence that a reading is sound is an argument that survives cross-examination. It is intersubjective, not private; it is produced in front of others and can be defeated by them.

This is a counter-model to the somatic standard the book installs, and its interest is exactly that it differs. Where the book tests understanding by a change the reader feels in the body, the Talmud tests it by a claim the reader can defend in company. The two standards are not rivals to be merged; they mark two ways a tradition can answer the question of whether reading landed. The Talmudic answer requires the second person structurally — there is no defending a reading against no one — which is why this verification standard is one more form of the finding that the reading architectures presuppose a community rather than a solitary reader.
