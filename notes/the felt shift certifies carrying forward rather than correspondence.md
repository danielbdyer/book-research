# the felt shift certifies carrying forward rather than correspondence

The book uses the felt shift as its test for whether anything is true: "nothing enters the lattice the body has not countersigned" (atlas §III), and by the author's statement of 2026-08-04 everything else runs downstream of that standard. The vault has now read the standard's founding paper, and the paper defines the certificate more narrowly than the book uses it.

Gendlin's 1964 definition of a correct symbolization is about what the symbols *do*, not what they *match*. His footnote says it outright: "'correctness' does not imply that a given set of symbols means what the felt referent alone means. Rather, 'correctness' refers to the experienced effect which certain symbols produce" (Gendlin 1964, fn. 9). The same footnote gives the reason: minutes later, a different formulation can interact with the changed referent and be "correct" in its turn. And the shift itself — his term in 1964 is "referent movement" — is defined as a change, not a report: "It is not only a change but a directly experienced 'give' or 'movement' which feels right and welcome. Its tremendous importance lies in the fact that after such a referent movement (even very small), the implicit meanings are now different" (def. 11). Unfolding "does not just inform one about what was involved but, rather, it changes the whole manner in which one experiences" (def. 9).

So in the primary text, the body countersigns *movement*: this word carried the process forward, here, now. The book reads the same signature as countersigning *content*: this claim is true. Those are different certificates, and the difference is not a technicality — on Gendlin's own account a formulation that felt correct is later replaced, which no truth-certificate could survive and a movement-certificate absorbs without strain.

## Substantiation

- The negative half of the criterion is also in the text, and it is the half the book's "either answer honest" rests on: "It is just this referent movement which is usually missing when one talks at oneself… From this lack of referent movement, one knows that nothing has really changed," and "Without reference movement, what is said is 'merely' talk, 'merely' intellectualization, 'merely' hair splitting, or 'merely' reporting" (def. 11). What the absence of a shift tells you is that nothing changed — not that the sentence was false.
- **What this does to the first-ranked objection: it narrows what introspection must deliver.** The tension at [[introspective access is the felt shift's undefended premise]] holds that the standard presupposes reliable access to one's own states. A truth-certificate needs a person to report their inner contents accurately, which is the claim Schwitzgebel attacks. A movement-certificate needs a person to notice a change against no-change — a discrimination, not a description. That is a much weaker introspective demand, and Gendlin's version of the standard therefore has a smaller attack surface than the book's version. The counterweights are still unread, and this narrowing is a candidate defense rather than a verdict.
- **What this costs the book: the standard cannot underwrite doctrine.** If the shift certifies carrying forward, then a reader's shift at a sentence of the book establishes that the sentence moved them, not that it is true — which is precisely the gap the corpus's own counterweight names from the other side, a bodily yes produced by fluent prose ([[felt shift versus felt confirmation]]). Gendlin's definition does not close that gap; it relocates the book's exposure from "the body might misreport truth" to "the book claims truth where its instrument certifies movement."
- The book's auxiliary commitment fits the narrower certificate better than the wider one. A book that is an instrument in service of the reader's own life ([[the book can only be auxiliary to a sovereign reader]]) needs its test to say "this moved you," and can leave "this is true" to the argument it has explicitly declined to rest on. The narrowing is therefore usable rather than merely damaging, and how to use it is the author's decision.
- The finding is from one paper. *Focusing* (1978) and *A Process Model* were not read, and either could state a stronger claim than the 1964 footnote; the reading record lists what remains (`archive/reading — gendlin, a theory of personality change (1964).md`).
