# the practitioner rather than the person being helped carries the alliance effect

The alliance is by definition something two people make, so the natural reading of the alliance–outcome relation is that it measures a joint achievement. The research decomposes it further than that, and the decomposition lands on one side. Flückiger and colleagues state it plainly: "there is meta-analytic evidence that a considerable proportion of the alliance–outcome correlation is strongly impacted by the therapist," citing Baldwin, Wampold and Imel 2007 and Del Re et al. 2012, and their moderator table records the shape of the result across 69 studies as a ratio in which variability between therapists exceeds variability within a therapist's own caseload.

What that means in ordinary terms is the finding. If a particular therapist tends to form strong alliances across their whole caseload, their clients tend to do better. If a particular client happens to form a stronger alliance than that therapist's other clients do, the same benefit does not follow to the same degree. The capacity being measured belongs to the practitioner.

This lands on the book's lived-practice centerpiece rather than on its argument. The corpus already states that the practices are the author's room-holding craft scaled down to one reader, and the claim underneath that design is that holding a container is a skill a person has and can get better at. Here that claim has a measured form: the skill is real enough to show up as between-practitioner variance in outcomes. What the finding does not say is that the skill transfers to a reader holding a container for themselves, which is the whole bet of the practice curriculum and is not tested anywhere in this literature.
