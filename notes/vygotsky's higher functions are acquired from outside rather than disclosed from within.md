# vygotsky's higher functions are acquired from outside rather than disclosed from within

The book reports one finding, that deep change is the disclosure of something already present rather than the acquisition of something new. Vygotsky's chapter on internalization asserts the contrary about the same class of function, and he asserts it as his thesis rather than in passing. "The internalization of socially rooted and historically developed activities is the distinguishing feature of human psychology, the basis of the qualitative leap from animal to human psychology" (Vygotsky 1978, ch. 4). What a person ends up with was not in them beforehand; it was in the relations between them and other people, and it moved. He puts the replacement in the strongest terms available to him: "Psychological processes as they appear in animals actually cease to exist; they are incorporated into this system of behavior and are culturally reconstituted and developed to form a new psychological entity."

This is a real disagreement rather than a difference of emphasis, and its shape is worth being exact about. Vygotsky is not claiming that nothing was there — he is claiming that what was there is superseded, and that the higher function is a new entity built by a history the person did not contain. The book's account runs the other way: the fullness was never absent, the contraction is what changed, and what looks like acquisition is the removal of a concealment. Both accounts explain the same observable, which is a person who can now do something they could not do before, and they explain it by opposite stories about what happened.

The disagreement is not settled here and the vault does not settle it. What is worth recording is where each account is strongest, because the book will have to write a sentence about this. Vygotsky's is strongest for capacities with an obvious social history — the words a person thinks in, the concepts they sort with, the attention they can hold on purpose because somebody once held it with them. The book's is strongest for what it actually addresses, which is a reader who already had the experience and lost it, since a capacity that arrives and then fades is poorly described as an acquired entity and well described as something disclosed and then reconcealed. Keeping Vygotsky's refusal intact costs the book nothing it was entitled to and gives its reader a real opponent to hold the finding against.

## Substantiation

- The reading is recorded at `archive/reading — vygotsky, mind in society chapter 4 (1978).md`. The chapter was read complete in the Harvard 1978 edition, and the record explains why the lettered list rather than a page number is the reliable locator in the scan used.
- The contrary is at the level of the mechanism rather than of the vocabulary, which is what makes it worth keeping. Vygotsky's word for the process is *internalization*, defined in the chapter as "the internal reconstruction of an external operation" — reconstruction, not recovery.
- The companion finding from the same reading, which is what the queue sent this reading to check, is at [[the internalization law reaches self-mastery and demands a relation that answers]]. The two are separate claims because a thinker's refusal and a thinker's mechanism are different things to the book, and the vault's rule is that a refusal is kept so that the thinker survives being cited.
- No efficacy is postulated on either side, and this note does not claim that either account is right.
