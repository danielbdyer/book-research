# zen's makyo instruction refuses the self-certifying experience

The book's verification standard is a bodily signal, and its strongest opponent turns out not to be a skeptical outsider but one of its own honored lineages. Zen names the compelling experiences that arise in practice — visions, blisses, revelations, illusory sensations — *makyō*, and instructs the sitter to dismiss them. Yasutani's introductory lectures define the term and give the instruction whole: "Never be tempted into thinking that these phenomena are real or that the visions themselves have any meaning. To have a beautiful vision of a Buddha does not mean that you are any nearer becoming one yourself, any more than a dream of being a millionaire means that you are any richer when you awake… Whenever makyo appear, simply ignore them and continue sitting wholeheartedly" (Kapleau, *The Three Pillars of Zen*, Yasutani's introductory lectures). What a teacher actually says when a student reports one is recorded in the dokusan transcripts of the same book: "These things have no particular significance—they are neither beneficial nor harmful. Don't embrace them, just go on earnestly with your practice."

The refusal has a precise edge, and keeping the edge is what makes it usable rather than merely bracing. Zen does not refuse experience — the same lecture validates kensho absolutely, and even reads makyō as a sign the sitter is near it. What it refuses is the experience *certifying itself*: verification in this lineage is external and relational — the teacher, in dokusan, discriminates makyō from kensho and tests the genuine article — and even the genuine article is refused as a possession. Yasutani again: "One who becomes attached to what he or she realizes through satori is also still lingering in the world of makyo. So, you see, there are makyo even after enlightenment." The precedent runs to the tradition's founder-figures: Hakuin's first kensho was refused by his teacher, who sent him back to practice.

Two things follow for the book. First, the felt-shift standard now has an opponent worth a full hearing, from inside the company rather than outside it — a refusal from inside is worth three from outside, and the refusals-intact rule means it is kept whole rather than answered. Second, what Zen installs in the experience's place converges with the book's own architecture: the corpus already names the relational field as the second check on what the solitary body reports, and dokusan is that check institutionalized for a thousand years. The lineage that most sharply refuses the self-certifying shift is also the strongest precedent for the book's answer to it.

## Substantiation

- Definition, from the same lectures: "Makyo are the phenomena—visions, hallucinations, fantasies, revelations, illusory sensations—which one practicing zazen is apt to experience at a particular stage in his sitting," and they are "not inherently bad. They become a serious obstacle to practice only if one is ignorant of their true nature and is ensnared by them."
- The older anchors the lecture itself cites: the Śūraṅgama Sutra's fifty kinds of makyō, and Keizan's *Zazen Yōjinki*, which reads visions and penetrating insights as "merely the symptoms of an impairment arising from a maladjustment of the mind with the breath."
- Edition variance is on record: earlier transcriptions read "vision of a Bodhisattva" for "vision of a Buddha," and the Hakuin episode's exact translated phrasing ("cave-dwelling devil," Wild Ivy, trans. Waddell) was not verified at this depth — the episode itself is standard biography.
- The claim's limit: this is one lineage's instruction, not a survey finding. Whether the other lineages the book honors carry parallel refusals is open research, and the convergence with the relational-field check is the vault's observation, not the lecture's.
