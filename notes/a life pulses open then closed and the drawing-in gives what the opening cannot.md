# a life pulses open then closed and the drawing-in gives what the opening cannot

Aliveness breathes. It reaches out into world, brightness, and contact, and then it gathers back into quiet, depth, and the near — and both are the pulse, not the pulse and its interruption. The gift, rarely given, is permission to come back in: a person does not have to hold the expansion forever, because the return is not a loss of the height but the other half of being alive, and the drawing-in carries what the opening never could. A reader who feels this stops grading the exhale as decline and begins to trust the whole rhythm.

## Substantiation

- The tradition figures consciousness as a native two-beat: expansion (vikāsa) and contraction (saṅkoca), Śiva's unmeṣa and nimeṣa, where the closing is the light's own inward phase rather than a fall away from it (Spanda Kārikā I.1; attributed, to verify). Goethe reached the same shape independently, taking "Systole und Diastole" as the fundamental rhythm of living nature (morphological writings; attributed, to verify, held as company).
- The shape also follows from first principles: anything that only expanded would burst or dissipate, so the return-phase is what makes a rhythm rather than a runaway, and a rhythm is what can be sustained. The exhale earns its dignity on structural grounds before any tradition is invoked.
- Register: saṅkoca and vikāsa are held here as the two phases of the one pulse, kept unpaired with reflexive awareness (vimarśa), which stands on its own. The subject is kept on what the in-breath gives, so the note affirms the rhythm rather than defending contraction.
