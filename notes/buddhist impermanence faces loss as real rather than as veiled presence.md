# buddhist impermanence faces loss as real rather than as veiled presence

The recognition doctrine has no organ for genuine loss, reading what is gone as concealed rather than ended. Early Buddhist death-mindfulness (*maranasati*) and impermanence (*anicca*) face loss the other way: impermanence is a mark of all conditioned things, death is certain and its hour uncertain, and the practitioner recollects daily that they are subject to aging, illness, death, and separation from all that is dear. What ends, ends, and there is nothing abiding behind the change to recover. This is the independent second arrival the finite has lacked, and it pairs with Heidegger's being-toward-death as the finite's other pole, mirroring the aletheia and pratyabhijna convergence with Heidegger again one side of it. The reading is not done, so this is held privated.

## Substantiation

- The book's half is on record: change is un-forgetting, recovery of the already-present (atlas §I; [[reading is remembering]]).
- The Buddhist half is named, not shown: the Five Remembrances are in the Upajjhatthana Sutta (AN 5.57), the death-mindfulness discourses in the Maranassati suttas (AN 6.19–6.20), and *anicca* as a mark of existence in the Anattalakkhana Sutta (SN 22.59), with Dhammapada 277. To verify at the text; not quoted from memory. The vault seats Buddhist-adjacent figures (Loy, Kornfield, Ingram) but none for death or impermanence, so this is a clean gap.
- The refusal is kept, and it corrects two doctrines at once. The tradition's aim is not to recover a lost fullness but to loosen clinging by seeing there is no abiding self or object to hold — *anatta*, not-self — which cuts against "you already know everything *you are*; you've just forgotten," and against a relational field premised on a substantial witness. To admit it, the book must let genuine loss and impermanence stand un-recuperated beside recognition rather than collapse loss into concealment.
