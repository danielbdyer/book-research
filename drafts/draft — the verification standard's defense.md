This book asks you to check things in your body, and that is the most attackable sentence in it.

Everything else here can be argued about in the ordinary way. You can disagree with a distinction, or find that a figure does not fit your life, and the disagreement stays between us on the page. But the check is different, because the check is what decides whether any of the rest is doing anything. If the check does not work, the book is a collection of interesting sentences, and you would have no way of telling from the inside.

So it is worth saying plainly what the check is, what has been shown against it, and what is left.

## What you are actually being asked to notice

Start by narrowing it, because the usual version asks too much.

The idea of checking in the body has a strong form and a weak form. The strong form says your body can tell you whether something is true — that a settling in the chest is a kind of verdict. The weak form says only that your body can tell you whether something moved. It has stopped being what it was; the thing you could not say a minute ago is now sayable.

The man this check comes from held the weak form, and said so. Eugene Gendlin, working out the theory in 1964, wrote that when a set of words is correct, "'correctness' does not imply that a given set of symbols means what the felt referent alone means. Rather, 'correctness' refers to the experienced effect which certain symbols produce." What is being certified is an effect, not a match. And his negative version is even plainer: "From this lack of referent movement, one knows that nothing has really changed."

That is a smaller thing to ask of you, and it is the only thing this book asks. Not *is this true*. Only *did something move*.

## The reason to doubt it, stated at full strength

Here is what makes the check hard, and I would rather you had it from me.

Well-written prose produces the feeling of recognition all by itself. This is not a suspicion; it has been measured. A statement that rhymes is rated as more true than the same statement that does not. A claim printed beside a decorative photograph is believed more readily than the same claim alone. Fluency — the ease with which a sentence goes down — is itself a source of the bodily yes. Which means a book that is trying to give you a real recognition and a book that is merely well made will feel, at the moment of reading, very similar.

I have no way around this, and neither do you. If you have felt something land while reading these pages, that feeling is evidence of nothing on its own. Marketing copy is written by people who understand this better than I do.

There is a second and colder objection, and it is aimed at the smaller claim rather than the larger one. Two psychologists, Richard Nisbett and Timothy Wilson, spent the 1970s showing that people report on their own minds badly. In the paper that made the case, they carve out an exemption that seems to protect what I am asking of you: a person, they say, "knows the focus of his attention at any given point in time; he knows what his current sensations are," and these are "private facts that can be known with near certainty."

But the same paper has a section devoted to change. In one study, students argued about school busing for an hour and came out holding the opposite view. Asked afterwards what they had originally thought, they reported their new position as the one they had held all along. The researchers' summary of them: "No subject reported that the discussion had had any effect in changing or modifying his position." They had changed completely and felt no change at all.

That is a direct hit on the weak form. Noticing change was supposed to be the safe request.

## What survives, and how you would use it

Three things survive, and none of them is a feeling.

**The first is refusal.** A real felt sense can turn a word down. You offer it a description, and something in you says *not that* — not because you have an argument against the word, but because the word does not fit and the not-fitting is itself palpable. This is the part that fluency cannot counterfeit, and the reason is simple: fluency's whole business is producing agreement. It has no mechanism for costing you something. Gendlin's own sentence for it is odd and exact: "Not even the individual himself judges it but, we might say somewhat poetically, his direct referent does the judging."

So the first test is not *did that feel right*. It is *can this thing say no to me*. If nothing you have read in this book has been refused by your body — if it has all gone down smoothly — that is not a sign it is working. It is a sign the check has not started.

**The second is what you can say afterwards.** A real shift changes the sentences available to you. Something you could not have articulated on Tuesday is sayable on Thursday, and you notice it because you say it, out loud, to someone, and it surprises you. A fluent yes leaves your vocabulary exactly where it was. You agreed, warmly, and tomorrow you can produce the same sentences you could produce yesterday.

**The third is that this is old news to the people who do it seriously.** Gendlin, fourteen years before he wrote the popular book, listed four things that feel like inward attention and are not: raw emotion going round on itself, circling the circumstances, explaining yourself to yourself, and rearranging yourself on purpose. And Zen, which has no stake in any of this, instructs the sitter to dismiss compelling experiences outright — the tradition has a word for the vivid stuff that arrives during practice and its instruction is to let it go and keep sitting. The traditions that have run this check the longest are the least impressed by it.

## Where the two tests disagree, which I am not going to hide

The first test happens now. The second happens over days.

And there is a problem with the second one, which is that the same research that documents change-blindness also says distance in time makes self-report worse rather than better. Nisbett and Wilson put it first among the causes of error: "Perhaps chief among the circumstances that should decrease accuracy in self-report is a separation in time between the report and the actual occurrence of the process." The busing students failed at exactly the task of comparing now against remembered-then.

So checking in the moment leaves you exposed to the well-made sentence, and checking later leaves you exposed to rewriting your own past. I do not think that is fatal, and here is the way through it that I would ask you to try.

Do not ask, later, whether you felt different back then. That is the question that fails, because it needs the memory that does not survive. Ask instead what you can do now that you could not do before. Not a remembered feeling — an actual difference, visible from outside: a conversation you had that you would have avoided, a sentence you said out loud, a thing you stopped doing. Gendlin's own criterion was always about carrying forward rather than about recall, and this is what that means in practice.

## The part where you need somebody else

The two people who have argued hardest that you cannot read yourself accurately both explain your confidence the same way, and neither of them knew he was agreeing with the other.

Nisbett and Wilson gave it a heading — inadequate feedback — and an example: a man who believes stress keeps him awake will always find evidence that his life is stressful, and a good night's sleep in his worst week does not disturb the theory. Eric Schwitzgebel, writing thirty years later, put it more sharply: "Because no one ever scolds us for getting it wrong about our experience and we never see decisive evidence of error, we become cavalier. This lack of corrective feedback encourages a hypertrophy of confidence."

The example Schwitzgebel gives is his own marriage. His wife tells him he seems angry about being left the dishes. He looks honestly and finds no anger. He is wrong, and he knows he is usually wrong in this situation, and he says why: "My wife reads my face better than I introspect."

I want you to notice what that means for a book. The strongest case against reading yourself is not that the inside is unreadable. It is that nothing ever corrects you. And a book cannot correct you either. Which is why the check, as I am describing it, is not finished until it is said to somebody — and why the practices in this book keep asking you to speak, and to have someone there when you do.

## What I do not know

I do not know whether practice makes this reading more reliable. That is the obvious defence — that a person who has done this for years reads themselves better than a person who has not — and it is the one I would most like to be true. Schwitzgebel considered it and left it open: "Perhaps the right kind of learning, practice, or care could largely shield us from error — an interesting possibility that merits exploration! — but I see as yet no robust scientific support for such hopes." He wrote that in 2008. Some work has been done since; I have not established that it settles the question.

I do not know whether this works. I have not claimed it. What I have tried to do is describe a check accurately, tell you the strongest case against it that I could find, and give you two tests you can run yourself — one that asks whether anything in you can refuse a sentence, and one that asks what you can do this week that you could not do last week.

If neither of them fires, then nothing here has happened yet. That is a real possibility and I would rather you could see it than not.
