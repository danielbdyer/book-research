The concern is the one the author named as his main job. The first drafting-readiness dry run, run on this same concern, reached this verdict: the vault would have served "statement, origin, and limits well, practice partially, and the defense not at all." That was true until 2026-08-07, when four primary readings landed on exactly this argument. This brief is what changed.

## 1. What the standard is, and what makes defending it hard

The book admits nothing its reader's body has not countersigned. A primitive, a formula, or a practice earns its place by producing a felt shift in the person reading, which makes the framework falsifiable by the reader as she reads ([[nothing enters the lattice the body has not countersigned]]).

Two facts make this the hardest thing in the book to defend, and both should be stated early rather than discovered by a reader.

The standard belongs to one man. Every other claim in the book is supported by a convergence of independent arrivals, and this one is Gendlin's alone, so the book's usual form of support is unavailable exactly where the weight is heaviest ([[the verification standard rests on one name and cannot be defended by company]]).

And the author's own assessment is that it is underdefended, with everything else running downstream of it (`ops/methodology/the book's current shape.md`, tier one).

## 2. The narrowing, which is the move the whole defense turns on

Gendlin's 1964 paper does not claim what the book has been claiming on his behalf. He defines the correctness of a symbolization as its experienced effect on the process, and explicitly denies that correctness means the symbols match the felt referent: "'correctness' does not imply that a given set of symbols means what the felt referent alone means. Rather, 'correctness' refers to the experienced effect which certain symbols produce" (1964, fn. 9).

So the shift certifies that a process moved. It does not certify that a content is true ([[the felt shift certifies carrying forward rather than correspondence]]). His negative criterion is the same shape: "From this lack of referent movement, one knows that nothing has really changed."

This matters because it changes what the reader is being asked to do. Describing an inner state accurately is a large demand. Noticing change against no-change is a smaller one. The defense is winnable at the second and probably not at the first.

## 3. The two canonical attacks, and where each one lands

They do not overlap, and stating both is more honest than stating either.

**Nisbett and Wilson (1977) attack the report of a cause, and exempt the report of a sensation.** Their own words, in the section explaining why the illusion of introspective access persists: a person "knows the focus of his attention at any given point in time; he knows what his current sensations are," and these sit among "private facts that can be known with near certainty" (p. 255). Their target is stated as reports about "the effects of particular stimuli on higher order, inference-based responses" (p. 233). A report that something is present in the body now is on the safe side of their own line ([[the confabulation finding exempts current sensations and targets reports of causes]]).

**But they also attack change-detection directly, in a section the vault did not know existed.** Pages 235 to 237 report two studies in which people whose evaluations had moved sharply reported no experience of having moved. Goethals and Reckman turned pro-busing students against busing in one conversation, and "No subject reported that the discussion had had any effect in changing or modifying his position" (p. 237). Noticing change is precisely what the narrowing in section 2 reduces the standard to, so the narrowing relocates the objection rather than clearing it ([[a large change in evaluation can happen without being experienced as change]]).

**Schwitzgebel (2008) attacks the report of a present bodily state, which is where the book actually stands.** His first extended case is emotion, and the features he says elude us are the bodily ones: whether joy is "sometimes in the head, sometimes more visceral"; whether emotional consciousness "is simply the experience of one's bodily arousal"; whether it is "consistently located in space (for example, particular places in the interior of one's head and body)" (p. 249). He names the present-tense exemption and rejects it: "We may concede the past to the skeptic, but not the present" is a claim he calls "blatantly unrealistic" (pp. 250–251). The only reports he treats as secure are a vivid canonical pain and a bright foveal colour ([[the case against introspective reliability is strongest exactly where the book's standard stands]]).

**One thing about Schwitzgebel belongs in the book and is easy to miss.** He is not a general skeptic about inner life. He holds introspection to be "possible, important, necessary for a full life" and "highly untrustworthy" at once (p. 246). A book that says the same thing is standing beside him rather than against him, and the disagreement is narrower and more answerable than it looks.

## 4. The counterfeit problem, which is separate from both attacks

Fluent prose produces a bodily yes that responds to the writing rather than to the claim. This is measured rather than suspected: fluency, rhyme, decorative photographs, and induced insight each produce felt truth without truth ([[the felt sense of truth has a documented counterfeit catalogue]]). The corpus's own survey records the two limits — that fluent prose can manufacture the confirmation, and that the signal is faintest in the readers who most need it ([[felt shift versus felt confirmation]]).

The reader's own risk profile is measured too. Interoception research splits attention to the body into accuracy, sensibility, and awareness, and finds them dissociable, so constant attention paired with inaccurate reading is a real profile — and it describes the book's second reader type ([[attending to the body is not the same as reading it accurately]]).

## 5. The four defenses the vault holds, with their sources

**The refusal costs something.** A felt sense is precise enough to reject a wrong word, and a rejection is a cost that fluency never pays. Gendlin's own text: "He may find that his previous formulation which felt correct must be replaced by another which now feels more correct," and "Not even the individual himself judges it but… his direct referent does the judging" (1964, def. 8 and fn. 11). A trained yes is cheap; a trained capacity to reject the trainer's own sentence is a different fact ([[the felt sense rejects formulations and that friction is what separates it from fluency]]).

**A real shift changes what can be said next.** A step rearranges what is sayable, while fluent assent leaves it unchanged, which makes the counterfeit problem a checkable difference rather than a matter of taste ([[a genuine felt shift changes what can be said next]]).

**The standard's own author named the counterfeits fourteen years before *Focusing*.** Footnote 13 of the 1964 paper is a taxonomy of four kinds of inward attention that are not focusing: sheer emotion, the circumstantial orbit, the explanatory orbit, and self-engineering ([[gendlin names four kinds of inward attention that are not focusing]]). The Focusing protocol's own instructions carry the guards — quick answers dismissed, freshness required, the check repeated across days ([[gendlin's focusing protocol already guards against the counterfeit shift]]).

**The sharpest refusal of experience-as-verification comes from inside the book's own lineages.** Zen instructs the sitter to dismiss compelling experiences, and what it installs instead is relational verification in dokusan ([[zen's makyo instruction refuses the self-certifying experience]]).

## 6. The one collision among the defenses, which the draft must not paper over

Defense two says check across days. Nisbett and Wilson's account of when self-report degrades puts separation in time first among the causes — "Perhaps chief among the circumstances that should decrease accuracy in self-report is a separation in time between the report and the actual occurrence of the process" (p. 251) — and their two change-blindness studies are precisely people comparing a present state against a remembered earlier one and failing.

So checking immediately exposes the reader to the fluent counterfeit, and checking later exposes the reader to reconstructing the earlier state from the present one. The candidate resolution, derived and untested, splits what each check certifies: the immediate comparison certifies that something is moving now, and the later check asks what can be said or done now that could not be before, which is an outward difference rather than a remembered feeling ([[the counterfeit defense and the change-blindness finding ask for opposite timings]]).

## 7. The relational repair, argued by the attackers themselves

Both canonical attackers explain introspective overconfidence the same way and neither cites the other. Nisbett and Wilson give it a heading, "Inadequate Feedback," and the case of an insomniac who can always find evidence for his own theory (p. 256). Schwitzgebel: "Because no one ever scolds us for getting it wrong about our experience and we never see decisive evidence of error, we become cavalier. This lack of corrective feedback encourages a hypertrophy of confidence" (pp. 259–260). His own example of being corrected is another person: "My wife reads my face better than I introspect" (p. 252).

If the mechanism of the error is that nothing corrects the reading, the repair is something that can. Gendlin's founding paper already makes the listener part of the instrument rather than an audience for it ([[the second chair is the verification standard's own requirement]]), and the two attackers arrive at the same requirement from the other side ([[both attacks on the standard blame the absence of correction by another person]]).

## 8. What the book cannot claim, and should say plainly

The training defense is not evidenced, and the opponent said so rather than the book. Schwitzgebel: "Perhaps the right kind of learning, practice, or care could largely shield us from error — an interesting possibility that merits exploration! — but I see as yet no robust scientific support for such hopes" (p. 259). His verdict is dated 2008 ([[trained introspection is the repair the standard's sharpest opponent named and left open]]).

The reader who is fluent in the vocabulary is the one most at risk, and the objection has two independent bearers rather than one ([[the trained-vocabulary objection arrives independently from experimental social psychology]]).

The basic posture is describable clinically as depersonalization, and the contemplative adverse-effects research has never been put to it ([[the basic posture is describable as dissociation and the harm literature is unread]]).

And the book does not claim the standard works. Whether any of this works is not a claim the author is making (`ops/methodology/authorial stance.md`).

## 10. What is still missing, so the draft does not pretend otherwise

Three things would change this argument and none has been done. Petitmengin's 2013 elicitation-interview result is the strongest candidate for the evidence Schwitzgebel asked for, and it has not been read. The Experiencing Scale literature, which operationalised the construct in 1969 and produces a meta-analytic association with improvement, is held at citation depth only. And the contemplative adverse-effects research is unread, which leaves the safety paragraph unwritable.
