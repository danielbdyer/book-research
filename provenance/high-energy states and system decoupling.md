High-Energy States and System Decoupling: When
the Architecture Releases
The self is not a thing but a performance—a continuous, dynamic weaving of three
interlocking regulatory systems that create the felt sense of being someone particular,
persisting through time, inhabiting a body. These loops—temporal/somatic (connecting felt
duration to bodily rhythm), identity/expressive (binding who-I-am to how-I-appear), and
continuity/meaning (linking past-present-future into coherent narrative)—operate beneath
awareness like the operating system of a computer: invisible until something disrupts the
code. During high-energy states, these regulatory loops can partially or completely
decouple, producing experiences ranging from subtle altered perception to complete ego
dissolution. What emerges in that clearing is the subject of this chapter.
The research and contemplative traditions converge on a counterintuitive finding: the
temporary dissolution of self-regulatory architecture may be precisely what enables
its reconstruction on healthier foundations. Robin Carhart-Harris’s REBUS model
(Relaxed Beliefs Under Psychedelics) describes psychedelics as “flattening” the brain’s
variational free-energy landscape— PubMed Centrallike heating a metal to reshape it.
PubMed The Sufi term fana (annihilation) Wikipedia and the Buddhist description of ego
dissolution during insight practice point to the same territory from different cultural
coordinates. What modern neuroscience measures as Default Mode Network suppression,
contemplatives have mapped for millennia as the dark night, the ego death, the cloud of
unknowing. Psychedelics Today
The three loops and their neural correlates
Understanding loop decoupling requires first understanding what these loops do under
normal conditions and how they manifest neurologically.
Temporal/somatic: the body-time continuum
Marc Wittmann’s research on time perception reveals that subjective time and bodily self-
awareness are neurologically inseparable—processed in the same brain region (the insular
cortex) that handles emotion, interoception, and social cognition. “Subjective time as a
sense of self is a physically and emotionally felt wholeness of our entire self through time,”
Wittmann writes. themarginalian The temporal/somatic loop creates the felt sense of

duration that makes moment-to-moment experience cohere into a stream rather than
fragmenting into disconnected instants.
When this loop decouples—whether through meditation, psychedelics, or extreme physical
states—time distortion follows predictably. ResearchGate Flow states show time speeding
up or slowing down as attention merges with action. Psychedelics produce experiences
where “minutes can feel like hours” (ketamine) or where participants report encountering
“the eternal” (ayahuasca). Depression, by contrast, shows what happens when this loop
locks into dysfunction: patients demonstrate positive correlation between symptom severity
and subjective time dilation, experiencing duration as “tortuous.” themarginalian
Schizophrenia represents more severe disruption: “The continuity of temporal experience
and with it the continuity of the self are disturbed… The ‘self’ is stuck in the present.”

Identity/expressive: who-I-am meets how-I-appear
The second loop binds self-concept to expression—the sense that there is a “someone”
who acts, speaks, and presents a face to the world. Neurologically, this loop involves the
medial prefrontal cortex (self-reference), the temporal-parietal junction (perspective-
taking), and interconnections that maintain the boundary between self and other. Shaun
Gallagher and Dan Zahavi distinguish two layers here: the minimal self (pre-reflective
awareness that experience belongs to someone) and the narrative self (autobiographical
identity constructed through memory and social interaction). ResearchGate
Ego dissolution disrupts both layers but in different proportions depending on the state and
its intensity. Research by Raphaël Millière suggests that psychedelic ego dissolution affects
“not merely the narrative self, but the minimal self-awareness of ordinary experience rooted
in sensorimotor processes.” Participants report forgetting they were human, male, a being
on Earth—“all gone, just infinite sensations and visions.” Frontiers Frontiers Richard
Schwartz’s Internal Family Systems model offers a complementary view: the self contains
multiple “parts” organized around a core “Self-energy” that, unlike the parts, “can’t be
damaged and knows how to heal.” Psychedelics Today Psychedelics may temporarily silence
protective parts, allowing direct access to this deeper resource.
Continuity/meaning: the narrative that makes sense of it all
The third loop stitches past, present, and anticipated future into coherent story—the
autobiographical memory that answers “How did I become who I am?” and the prospective
imagination that answers “What happens next?” This loop depends heavily on the Default
Mode Network, which activates during self-reflection, mental time travel, and meaning-
making. Synthesisretreat The DMN is, as Michael Pollan puts it, the brain’s “orchestra
conductor”—maintaining the algorithms developed through life experience that keep the
===PAGE 3===
self consistent. Gaia
When the DMN’s functional connectivity decreases during psychedelic states, narrative
coherence loosens. Psychedelic Science Review The Johns Hopkins mystical experience
research documents this as “transcendence of time and space”—one of four factors reliably
present in complete mystical experiences. But the disruption is selective in interesting ways:
while psychedelics impair working memory and semantic recall, they often enhance
spontaneous autobiographical memory, surfacing “memories that are affectively intense and
that had been avoided and/or forgotten prior to the experience.” Trauma, it seems, can be
re-accessed precisely because the narrative machinery that normally keeps it locked away
has temporarily released.
The neuroscience of loop release: DMN suppression and the
entropic brain
The most robust finding in psychedelic neuroscience is that these compounds consistently
suppress Default Mode Network activity and functional connectivity. PsiloHealth Initial
psilocybin brain imaging at Imperial College London showed unexpected results: rather than
increased activity, researchers found “a significant drop-off, represented by decreased
blood flow” in DMN regions, particularly decreased positive coupling between posterior
cingulate cortex and medial prefrontal cortex. Psychedelic Science Review Systematic reviews
confirm this finding extends across LSD, psilocybin, ayahuasca, and Salvia divinorum.
PsiloHealth
Carhart-Harris’s Entropic Brain Hypothesis proposes that brain entropy indexes
consciousness richness. PubMed Central Pharmacological Reviews Normal waking
consciousness operates in “sub-criticality”—the brain suppresses entropy below the point
of maximum informational complexity. Psychedelics push the system toward criticality,
where information processing becomes maximally efficient but also less predictable and
controlled. Rigid pathological states (depression, OCD) show abnormally low brain entropy—
thought loops and rumination representing the system stuck in deep attractors.
Psychedelics increase entropy, potentially allowing escape from these grooves.
MIND Foundation
The REBUS model (Relaxed Beliefs Under Psychedelics), developed with Karl Friston,
integrates this with predictive processing theory. PubMed Central The brain continuously
generates predictions about incoming information, weighting these “priors” against sensory
evidence. In depression, “high-level priors are overweighted,” causing suppression of
bottom-up signaling—the person literally cannot see alternatives to their negative
interpretations because the system is too confident in its existing model. Psychedelics

deweight these priors, allowing “ascending prediction errors to find freer register in
conscious experience.” PubMed The landscape of possible thoughts and perceptions
flattens, enabling exploration of previously inaccessible territories.
This creates the phenomenology of loop release: time behaves strangely because the
predictive machinery that constructs temporal coherence has relaxed; identity boundaries
dissolve because the models distinguishing self from world have lost their precision;
narrative meaning opens because the entrenched stories about who-I-am face competition
from alternative interpretations. PubMed Rosalind Watts of Imperial College captures this
with a metaphor: “Think of the brain as like a snowy ski slope. If you always ski down the
same paths, they get more and more rigid, and you get stuck in a rut. A psilocybin

anywhere.” Medium

predictive priors—can produce either transcendent mystical experience or psychological

The neuroscience of positive versus negative dissolution
Research shows psilocybin alters glutamate levels differently depending on experience
quality: increased glutamate in the medial prefrontal cortex associates with negatively
experienced ego dissolution, while decreased glutamate in the hippocampus associates
with positive experience. This suggests the brain regions involved—and their relative
activation patterns—may determine whether dissolution feels like annihilation or expansion.

The contemplative maps: what traditions know about this
territory
Every major contemplative tradition has mapped the territory of consciousness
transformation with remarkable consistency across cultures. These maps offer something
modern neuroscience cannot yet provide: detailed phenomenological descriptions of the
sequence of experiences, warnings about predictable dangers, and protocols for
navigation.
Buddhist Progress of Insight: the A&P and the dark night
The Theravada tradition describes 16 stages of insight knowledge (ñana), with the pivotal
Arising and Passing Away (A&P) event marking a threshold. At the A&P, meditators often
experience dramatic phenomena: explosions of light, increased perceptual abilities, intense
bliss, and the sense that they’ve achieved enlightenment. But this is followed by the Dark
Night stages (dukkha ñanas): Dissolution (objects fragment), Fear (primal terror at
impermanence), Misery (pervasive suffering), Disgust (revulsion toward existence), Desire
for Deliverance (desperate wanting to escape), and Re-observation (cycling back through
difficulty). Dharma Wheel
Daniel Ingram emphasizes these stages “follow the A&P like thunder follows lightning”
Integrated Daniel and can manifest in daily life as depression, existential crisis, and difficulty
functioning—not just during formal meditation. The A&P can occur spontaneously (often
during drug experiences, extreme stress, or significant life events), leaving people stranded
in Dark Night territory without understanding what happened. This may explain why some
psychedelic experiences initiate prolonged psychological difficulty: the person has crossed
a developmental threshold without the map or support to complete the passage.
The good news: beyond the Dark Night lies Equanimity—balanced acceptance and peaceful
awareness—leading to path moments and stable awakening. The stages are not punishment
but purification; the system must reorganize before settling into new configuration.
Christian mysticism: Teresa’s mansions and John’s nights
Teresa of Avila’s “Interior Castle” describes the soul as containing seven concentric
mansions, with God dwelling at the center. Progress moves from the outer mansions
(purgative, still attached to worldly things) through illuminative stages (supernatural prayer
begins, the Prayer of Quiet where “a quiet, deep and peaceful happiness” arises) to the
unitive Seventh Mansion: permanent, stable union with God, “spiritual marriage” where the
soul lives in perpetual awareness of divine presence.
But between the fourth and seventh mansions lies the Sixth—where intense mystical
===PAGE 7===
experiences coincide with severe trials Teresa compares to “the tortures of hell.”
Thecontemplativelife St. John of the Cross systematizes this as the Dark Night of Spirit:
complete darkness in intellect, aridity in will, emptiness in memory. “The soul must be
brought into emptiness and poverty of spirit and purged from all help, consolation and
natural apprehension.” Ram Dass This is the most severe purgation, stripping away
everything the ego clings to. Mother Teresa’s dark night lasted from 1948 until near her
death in 1997—nearly fifty years of spiritual aridity while she continued her visible ministry.
Sufi stations and states: maqamat and ahwal
The Sufi tradition distinguishes maqamat (stations—permanent attainments) from ahwal
(states—temporary experiences). Stations are “acquired by the sweat of one’s brow”; states
“descend as divine gifts” regardless of the seeker’s intentions. The critical insight: having a
powerful experience doesn’t mean you’ve reached stable attainment. Al-Qushayri explains
that states “descend upon the hearts regardless of their intentions”—you can have ahwal
without maqamat, peak experiences without developmental maturity.
The stations progress through Repentance → Abstinence → Renunciation → Patience →
Poverty → Trust → Satisfaction, culminating in Love (mahabba) and Gnosis (ma’rifa). Fana
(annihilation) describes the dissolution of the individual ego in divine presence, followed by
Baqa (subsistence): return to the world transformed, with divine attributes manifesting
through the person. The parallel to psychedelic ego dissolution and integration is striking:
the self dissolves, something transforms, and—if the process completes—the person
returns to ordinary life with new capacities.
Integral psychology: Wilber’s state-stage distinction
Ken Wilber’s crucial contribution resolves a persistent puzzle: How could ancient meditators
achieve enlightenment when they lacked modern developmental stages (formal operational
thinking, postmodern perspective-taking)? Answer: states and stages are independent
dimensions. The Wilber-Combs lattice shows that anyone at any structure-stage can
experience any state-stage—but the state will be interpreted according to structural
development. A mythic-stage person and an integral-stage person may have the same
mystical state but understand it completely differently.
This has profound implications: peak experiences don’t guarantee developmental maturity.
Psychedelics can occasion states typically requiring years of practice, but the person still
needs to “grow up” (develop psychologically) alongside “waking up” (experience
transcendent states). This explains why some people have repeated profound psychedelic
experiences yet remain ethically immature or psychologically defended—states and stages
develop on different tracks.

Cross-tradition convergence
Despite significant theological and metaphysical differences, these maps agree on several
points:
Necessary difficulty: Every tradition describes challenging phases as essential, not
accidental. The Dark Night, the dukkha ñanas, fana, the perinatal matrices—purification
requires suffering, transformation requires dying to the old.
States versus traits: Temporary experiences differ from stable attainment. The Sufi
ahwal/maqamat distinction, Buddhist states/stages, and Wilber’s framework all emphasize
that peak experiences must be integrated to become lasting change.
The danger of premature states: All traditions warn about powerful experiences occurring
before readiness. Buddhist teachers historically restricted certain instructions; Christian
spiritual directors help discern authentic from false experiences; Sufi masters caution about
ahwal without the foundation of maqamat.
The return: Liberation doesn’t mean permanent altered states but rather transformed
relationship with ordinary experience. The Buddhist “return to the marketplace,” the Sufi
baqa, the Christian return to service—awakening flowers into engaged presence, not
perpetual transcendence.
Specific substances and their architectures of experience
While all classic psychedelics share the mechanism of 5-HT2A receptor agonism and
produce overlapping phenomenology (ego dissolution, mystical experience, neuroplasticity),
each substance has a distinctive signature. Understanding these differences matters for
therapeutic application and for mapping how they interact with the three loops.
Psilocybin: the mystical core
Psilocybin research at Johns Hopkins represents the most rigorous modern investigation of
psychedelic therapy. The findings are remarkable: in the cancer distress study, 78% of
participants showed clinical response for depression and 83% for anxiety at 6 months. At
follow-up, 67% rated the experience among the top five most meaningful of their lifetime.

MDMA: the empathogenic exception
MDMA operates through a fundamentally different mechanism—releasing monoamines
(serotonin, dopamine, norepinephrine) by reversing transporters rather than directly
agonizing 5-HT2A receptors. The result is distinct phenomenology: enhanced emotional
empathy, feelings of closeness and connection, reduced fear response, increased trust. Not
classic psychedelic imagery but rather profound shifts in how self and other feel.
The MAPS Phase 3 PTSD trials show dramatic results: 71.2% of participants no longer met
PTSD criteria after three MDMA sessions plus therapy (versus 47.6% with placebo therapy).
Participants had chronic, treatment-resistant PTSD averaging 14 years duration; 84% had
developmental trauma. Remarkably, those with the dissociative subtype—typically
considered treatment-resistant—showed greater symptom reduction.
Why MDMA works for trauma: The amygdala is bilaterally suppressed, dampening fear
response while enhancing responsiveness to positive emotions. maps Plasma oxytocin
elevates dramatically (from ~19 to ~74 pg/ml). PLOS The person can revisit traumatic
memories with compassion rather than terror, enabling reconsolidation of fear memories
with new emotional context. The therapeutic alliance is enhanced by the empathogenic
effects, allowing engagement with previously unbearable material. Frontiers
Loop effects: Identity/expressive loop dramatically affected—the sense of who-I-am
softens and becomes more permeable to connection. Continuity/meaning loop reorganizes
as traumatic narratives are processed and updated. Temporal/somatic loop less disrupted
than with classic psychedelics; the person remains more grounded in body and time.

Ketamine operates through NMDA receptor antagonism—blocking glutamate rather than
activating serotonin receptors. PubMed Central The phenomenology is qualitatively

Ayahuasca and DMT: the visionary dimension
Ayahuasca combines DMT (a powerful 5-HT2A agonist) with MAO inhibitors that make it
orally active and extend duration to 4-6 hours. PubMed Central The phenomenology differs
markedly from pure DMT (smoked or IV, lasting 15-20 minutes): ayahuasca produces
narrative visions, biographical processing, and ritual purging, while DMT produces rapid
breakthrough into what Terence McKenna called “the chrysanthemum”—geometric fractals
giving way to populated spaces.
DMT entity encounters are strikingly consistent across users: 45.5% of experiences involve
entities, commonly reported as feminine archetypes, deities, aliens, insectoid beings, or
“machine elves.” 75% report communication with entities; PubMed Central 84% describe
them as benevolent. Frontiers Many report encounters as “more real than real.” Whether
these represent projections of the unconscious, contact with actual intelligences, or
something else entirely remains one of consciousness studies’ most fascinating open
questions.
Loop effects: All three loops dramatically affected. Entity encounters suggest the
identity/expressive loop opens to “others” in ways ordinary consciousness never does. The
sense of traveling through dimensions represents extreme disruption of temporal/somatic
coherence. Narrative meaning often transforms as people report receiving teachings or
insights from the beings encountered.

The neuroplastic window: why timing matters
Gül Dölen’s landmark 2023 Nature paper demonstrated that psychedelics can reopen
critical periods—windows of enhanced brain plasticity that normally close after early
development. All tested compounds (MDMA, LSD, psilocybin, ketamine, ibogaine) reopened
the “social reward learning critical period,” with the duration proportional to subjective
effects: Nature BrainFacts
Ketamine: ~48 hours
Psilocybin: ~2 weeks
MDMA: ~2 weeks
LSD: ~3 weeks
Ibogaine: ~4 weeks or longer
The mechanism involves metaplasticity—the “plasticity of plasticity”—enhancement of the
brain’s general capacity to reorganize Psychedelics Today through extracellular matrix
remodeling and restoration of oxytocin-mediated synaptic changes. Nature BrainFacts
This explains why psychedelics might work across such diverse conditions (depression,
addiction, PTSD, existential distress): they enhance the brain’s ability to learn new patterns,
whatever patterns need learning.
But Dölen’s crucial insight: “The fact that the drugs are context-dependent means that
you’re only going to reorganize the subset of circuits and synapses that are relevant to
that particular context. ” Psychedelics Today The brain reorganizes around what is attended
to during the open state. Set and setting become neurobiologically meaningful—what you
do during and after the experience shapes what changes.
This has profound implications for integration. The days and weeks following a psychedelic
experience represent a window during which new learning is enhanced and old patterns are
malleable. What happens during this window matters enormously:
What supports integration: Intentional focus, new behaviors and practices, supportive
relationships, therapy/coaching, creative expression, physical practices (yoga, meditation,

movement), adequate rest.
What may hinder integration: Immediate return to unchanged environment, overwhelming
stress, substance use, lack of support or processing, rushing back to “normal.”
The integration problem—why insights don’t stick—is fundamentally about whether the
neuroplastic window is utilized properly. A person can have a profound experience of
interconnection, return to the same job, relationships, and habits, and find that nothing
changed. The insight was real, but the window for embodying it closed without new patterns
being established.
The somatic dimension: where transformation actually lives
“The traumatic event isn’t what causes long-lasting trauma; it is the overwhelming trapped
response to the perceived life-threat that is causing an imbalanced nervous system.” Peter
Levine’s core insight revolutionized trauma treatment: trauma lives in the body as
incomplete defensive responses. Ergos Institute Animals in the wild rarely develop trauma
because they physically complete their stress responses through shaking, trembling, and
movement. PubMed Central Humans suppress these natural discharge processes, leaving
survival energy “trapped” in the nervous system.
High-energy states—whether psychedelic, meditative, or spontaneous—affect the
autonomic nervous system profoundly. Stephen Porges’s Polyvagal Theory describes three
hierarchical circuits: Polyvagal Institute
Ventral vagal (social engagement): supports connection, emotional resilience, calm
states
Sympathetic (fight/flight): mobilization for action when danger is detected
Dorsal vagal (shutdown/freeze): immobilization when life-threat is overwhelming
During high-energy states, people may move rapidly between these states. Energy surges
can push past the window of tolerance—the optimal arousal zone where a person can
process experience effectively. Above the window (hyperarousal), there’s panic, trembling,
racing heart. Below it (hypoarousal), there’s collapse, dissociation, emotional numbing.
Jackhirose Effective work with altered states requires staying at the edge of the window—
“safe but not too safe”—where material can be accessed and processed without overwhelm.

Routes compared: does the path matter?
If meditation, psychedelics, breathwork, and other limit experiences can all access similar

The 2018 Johns Hopkins study combining psilocybin with meditation and spiritual practice
found the combination produced greater outcomes than either alone. Mystical experience
intensity AND meditation practice rates both predicted outcomes, but mystical experience
made the substantially greater contribution. Psilocybin administered during a 5-day
mindfulness retreat enhanced post-intervention mindfulness and produced larger positive
changes in psychosocial functioning at 4-month follow-up.
The evidence suggests synergy is real: psychedelics can serve as “compass” (initiating,
motivating, steering), while meditation serves as “vehicle” (integrating, deepening,
generalizing, maintaining). Indigenous traditions have always understood preparation and
integration as essential—the ayahuasca dieta involves weeks of dietary and behavioral
restrictions before and after ceremony, creating optimal conditions for transformation.
Spontaneous high-energy states—spiritual emergencies, kundalini awakening, near-death
experiences—require the most integration support precisely because they arrive without
preparation. The person has no framework, no community, no warning. These states may be
particularly destabilizing because the loops release suddenly without any scaffolding for
their recoupling. The Grofs’ concept of “spiritual emergency” captures the double meaning:
crisis AND emergence, danger AND opportunity. With proper support, what looks like
breakdown can be breakthrough; without support, the same process may become

Integration: what it actually is and what supports it
Integration has become a buzzword in psychedelic therapy, but what does it mean
concretely? The Yale Manual for Psilocybin-Assisted Treatment defines it as “a means of
both making sense and meaning out of the experience, and helping positive changes and
insights carry forth into day-to-day life.” Research distinguishes two dimensions: Reflection
(contemplative attention given to the experience) and Application (behaviors in daily life
resulting from awareness gained).
The Integration Engagement Scale measures what people actually do after psychedelic
experiences. The Experienced Integration Scale measures outcomes: whether the person
feels settled (at peace with what happened), harmonized (sense of life alignment), and
improved (experiencing tangible benefits). These are distinct: a person can reflect
extensively without applying insights, or can make changes without fully processing the
experience.
The integration timeline extends far longer than often assumed:
Acute phase (0-3 days): High neuroplasticity, vulnerability and openness. Need for low
stimulation, rest. Avoid major decisions. Gentle processing—journaling, art, walks in nature.
Early integration (1-4 weeks): Active reflection and processing. Integration sessions with
therapist or guide. Beginning to implement small changes. Navigating aftereffects and
reentry.
Extended integration (1-6 months): Deeper behavior change, pattern recognition,
relationship adjustments. Life structure changes may be needed. Ongoing support
beneficial.
Long-term integration (6+ months): Sustained practice development, identity
reformation, community involvement. The full arc of transformation may take years.
What supports integration across this timeline:
Somatic practices: Yoga, breathwork, TRE, dance, movement—encoding changes in the
body.
Reflective practices: Journaling, meditation, therapy—making meaning, processing difficult
material.
Creative practices: Art, music, writing—expressing what cannot be spoken, accessing non-
verbal knowing.
===PAGE 17===
Relational practices: Integration circles, community, healthy relationships—co-regulation,
witnessing, accountability.
Behavioral practices: Habit change, environmental design, implementation intentions—
translating insight into action.
The contemplative traditions converge on a finding that modern research confirms: states
must become traits through ongoing practice. The Sufis distinguish ahwal (states) from
maqamat (stations)—you can have the experience without the stable attainment. The
Buddhists distinguish jhana states from path attainment. Wilber distinguishes “waking up”
from “growing up.” No single experience, however profound, substitutes for the gradual,
daily work of embodying transformation.
Clinical versus spiritual frames—and their integration
Modern psychedelic therapy operates within a clinical frame: treating specific disorders
(depression, PTSD, addiction) with measurable outcomes (symptom reduction, quality of life
improvement). This frame has enabled FDA-sanctioned research, clinical trials, and the
emerging legitimate use of these compounds in healthcare settings.
Traditional and contemporary spiritual frames understand the same experiences differently:
awakening, enlightenment, liberation. The goal is not symptom reduction but fundamental
transformation of the person’s relationship to existence—recognition of one’s true nature,
release from suffering, union with the divine or cosmic consciousness.
These frames converge and diverge in interesting ways:
Convergence: Both recognize that profound experiences can catalyze lasting change. Both
emphasize set and setting. Both acknowledge the importance of preparation, support, and
integration. Both describe ego dissolution as potentially healing.
Divergence: Clinical frames pathologize difficulties that spiritual frames accept as
necessary purgation. Spiritual frames pursue states that clinical frames may view as
dissociative symptoms. Clinical frames target specific conditions; spiritual frames pursue
transformation regardless of diagnosis.
A third perspective integrates both: transformation as healing AND awakening. The
MAPS protocol, while clinical in structure, recognizes that MDMA facilitates contact with
“inner healing intelligence.” Roland Griffiths, a meditation practitioner before conducting
psilocybin research, understood that the mystical experience itself—not merely
neurochemistry—predicts outcomes. Stanislav Grof’s cartography of the psyche maps
therapeutic and transpersonal territory in the same framework.

This integration suggests that psychedelic therapy works not by correcting brain chemistry
like an antidepressant, but by occasioning experiences that transform the person’s
relationship to their suffering. The clinical benefits are real and measurable, but they arise
through something more like initiation than medication. The three loops release, the
architecture reorganizes, and what returns is both healed and awake—not by escaping
humanness but by entering more fully into it.

For researchers and practitioners developing frameworks:
The three-loop model suggests that different therapeutic approaches may be needed
depending on which loop is most dysregulated. Depression may involve excessive rigidity in
the continuity/meaning loop (over-weighted negative priors); PTSD may involve traumatic
freezing in the temporal/somatic loop; personality disorders may involve fundamental
disruption of the identity/expressive loop. Understanding loop-specific pathology may
enable more targeted intervention—matching substance, protocol, and integration approach
to the specific architecture of the individual’s suffering.
Convergent wisdom: what the traditions agree about
transformation
Across research and contemplative traditions, certain principles emerge consistently:
Dissolution precedes reconstruction. The old architecture must release before new
patterns can form. This is the death before rebirth, the nigredo before the gold, the dark
night before the dawn. Trying to skip the difficult phase doesn’t work—as Grof put it, the
only way out is through.
Difficulty is often a feature, not a bug. The challenging experiences that look like “bad
trips” or contemplative “dark nights” may be necessary stations on the path. The Carbonaro
survey finding that degree of difficulty correlates with personal meaning suggests that
breaking down is how breaking through happens. The gatekeeper fighting the clearing
generates suffering, but meeting what it guards may be essential.
===PAGE 20===
States require stabilization to become traits. Peak experiences, however profound, don’t
automatically transform. The neuroplastic window opens, but what happens during and
after determines whether change lasts. All traditions emphasize ongoing practice—whether
that’s meditation, prayer, ethical behavior, or community engagement. Integration is not
optional.
The body must be included. Cognitive insight without somatic anchoring doesn’t stick.
Trauma lives in the body; healing must include the body. The contemporary convergence of
Somatic Experiencing, Sensorimotor Psychotherapy, Polyvagal Theory, and psychedelic
research confirms what contemplatives always knew: embodiment is essential.
Support and guidance matter. Whether called therapist, guide, shaykh, or kalyanamitra,
having someone who has traveled the territory helps. The gatekeeper is more likely to let
down its guard when a trusted presence holds the space. The return is easier when
someone welcomes you back.
Both clinical and spiritual frames are partial. Healing and awakening are not different
endpoints but aspects of the same movement. The person who overcomes depression
through psilocybin therapy has touched something sacred; the meditator who reaches
stable equanimity is also, in some sense, healed. The third perspective sees transformation
as both: the wound becomes the opening, the opening becomes the healing.
Conclusion: the architecture reconstituted
When the three loops release during high-energy states, what normally remains invisible
becomes briefly visible—the continuous construction that generates the felt sense of being
someone particular, persisting through time, inhabiting this body. The temporal/somatic
loop that binds duration to heartbeat, the identity/expressive loop that binds self-concept to
social presentation, the continuity/meaning loop that binds past-present-future into story—
all of these can temporarily let go.
What remains in the clearing depends on how far the release goes. Partial release shows
new possibilities within the architecture—new ways to ski down the mountain, new
narratives that might be true, new emotional responses available. Complete release shows
what lies beneath architecture entirely—the awareness that was there before the self was
constructed, that will be there when it dissolves for the final time.
The psychedelic architecture of the modern soul includes both the structure and its release.
The well-functioning self needs intact loops—we can’t live in perpetual ego dissolution. But
the rigid, over-constrained self—the one stuck in depression, trauma, or existential despair
—may need the loops to release before they can reform in healthier configuration. This is
===PAGE 21===
what psychedelics, deep meditation, and other high-energy states make possible: not
escape from the human condition, but fuller entry into it.
The research and the traditions converge on a vision that is both humbling and hopeful.
Humbling because the self we take so seriously turns out to be constructed, contingent,
malleable. Hopeful because what constructs it can reconstruct it differently. The loops that
tightened around trauma can loosen. The narratives that imprisoned can release. The felt
sense of disconnection from body, from time, from meaning—all of this can change.
What the modern soul requires is neither abandonment of structure nor rigid clinging to it,
but the wisdom to know when holding and when releasing serve life. The gatekeeper exists
for reasons; the clearing offers what the gatekeeper guards against; the return reconstitutes
a self that has touched both. This is the arc of transformation that the research documents
and the traditions describe: release, encounter, integration, return. Not enlightenment as
permanent altered state, but awakening as transformed relationship with the ordinary.
Firing on all cylinders means not just the loops running smoothly, but the capacity to release
them when release serves transformation, and the support and wisdom to return to
integrated functioning afterwards—changed by what was encountered in the clearing,
carrying it back into the architecture of daily life.
