# a page-fetching tool that summarizes can reword a quotation without saying so

On 2026-09-26 a session gathered 58 entries from management theory and organizational psychology for the supplement to the facilitation ecology (`ops/facilitation ecology — management and organizational psychology.md`). The session's web searches were already spent, so three researchers fetched pages directly with a tool that passes each page through a summarizing model and returns the result. The researchers copied quotations from what the tool returned and marked them verified at the page.

## What it cost

When the session fetched the raw text of every cited page (encyclopedia source files, publishers' pages, scholarly indexes, and PDFs) and compared the 229 quotations with it by script, several quotations did not match their pages. Some had words dropped or changed, among them the sentence reporting that no valid evidence supports the often repeated figure that 70 percent of change programs fail, and Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein's definition of a nudge; some claims and quotations appeared on no cited page at all. The tool gave no sign in either case. Twenty-one corrections followed, and the supplement's section 2 records them.

## What it implies

A quotation taken through a summarizing fetch is a paraphrase until it has been compared with the page's raw text. A survey that relies on such a tool should fetch raw text for its checks (an encyclopedia's source view, a scholarly index's record, or the PDF itself) and compare by script, as this round did, before any quotation enters the vault. Pages that cannot be read as raw text, such as book-search pages that need a browser, should not be the only source of a quotation. No rule of the vault needs to change; the point is for the next session that gathers from the web.
