# The facilitation ecology — the field around the book, sorted across scales

## 1. What was asked, and the answer in one paragraph

On 2026-09-26 the author asked: "Do you think we could explore the ecology of facilitative techniques as a whole throughout spectrums? From a single person's self reflection to parts to various dimensionalities of dyad/triad and group work and social mores and so on and so forth? And then also maybe on another dimension the specific subject of engagement? And another the spectrum or mental model?" He gave the purpose: "discovering where our current research maps in against all the other moves we haven't yet discovered, just to contextualize it for now." And he said why the outward view matters to him for its own sake: "the experience of the other perspective and me facilitating myself into expanding my own vocabulary for the book's contextualization and reification is a part of the core work too."

The survey gathered 299 facilitative techniques from outside the vault, across twelve scales from one person alone to a society's customs and the living world. It also gathered 61 single moves that recur across scales, 37 named stances a facilitator can hold, 24 mental models, and 12 places where several traditions name something similar in different words. It then placed 264 things the vault already holds on the same coordinates. Three findings stand out.
- **The vault's research sits at one end of the field.** Of its 264 holdings, 100 work at the scale of one person alone. From three people outward it holds almost nothing: 2 holdings at the scale of three people, both from the author's own practice; 3 each for organizations, communities, and the living world; and 4 for a society's customs. The outside techniques spread across all twelve scales, with the most at two people where one helps (47), small groups (37), and organizations (32).
- **The author's own facilitation sits where the field is densest.** In 15 of the 25 practices in his record, he shapes the process and the people supply the content. That is the position of 114 of the 299 outside techniques, more than any other. Most of the vault's holdings (138 of 264) sit instead where no live facilitator is present, and all twelve of the book's own forms of practice sit there too.
- **Three subjects the field works on heavily are nearly absent from the vault**: conflict (55 outside techniques, 2 vault holdings), the future (26 and none), and decisions (65 and 17).

Two quieter findings follow. Four mental models have no holding in the vault: Gestalt therapy, which the vault mentions only as a name in other people's lineages, and the solution-focused, process-oriented, and civic and deliberative models, which it does not mention at all. And the arts are thin: voice and music appear in 21 outside techniques and in 1 vault holding, although the author is a poet.

**The survey comes as two files and a page.** This file walks the field and states what the sorting shows. The companion file, `ops/facilitation ecology — the entries.md`, holds every technique with its originator, date, what happens in it, and the words its practitioners use, each word with a plain meaning; those words are the vocabulary the author asked for. The same entries can be browsed and filtered on a web page, at https://claude.ai/artifact/DuVyj9ecaSZuBzXXgBEXHG.

## 2. The coordinates

The survey places every technique by seven coordinates. The author named the first three, and the survey adds four, each because the vault already uses it or because the book's own question needs it. A coordinate is a way of finding a technique. It is not a claim about how facilitation is built, and section 12 says why that difference matters.

**Scale: who is present.** This is the author's first spectrum, "from a single person's self reflection to parts to various dimensionalities of dyad/triad and group work and social mores." The survey uses twelve bands:
- **one person alone**, reflecting, praying, writing, walking, or meditating;
- **one person and their parts**, working with voices or sub-selves within themselves;
- **a text or tool with no one present**, such as a self-help book, a letter, a guided recording, or a workbook, which is where Book II's reader stands;
- **two people, one helping**, such as a therapist, coach, spiritual director, or teacher with one person;
- **two people as equals**, usually taking turns;
- **three people**, often with a witness or observer, or two people with a facilitator, as in couples work and mediation;
- **a small group** of about four to fifteen people, including families;
- **a large group** of about fifteen to several hundred people in one event;
- **an organization**, a team or institution over time, including how it decides;
- **a community or public**, such as a neighborhood, a movement, or a nation, in civic, restorative, and popular-education practice;
- **a society's customs**, the rituals, norms, and institutions a culture uses to hold its members, such as rites of passage, mourning customs, festivals, and rules for who speaks when;
- **the more-than-human**, practices that include animals, land, or the living world as participants.

**Subject: what the technique engages.** This is the author's second spectrum, "the specific subject of engagement." The survey uses twenty-two subjects: the body, emotion, thought and belief, memory and the past, meaning and purpose, identity and self, the relationship between the people present, a relationship with someone absent or dead, conflict, a decision, a task or work, learning a skill, creativity and play, the spiritual or ultimate, death and finitude, justice and power, nature, the future, the group's own process, trauma and healing, grief, and attention itself.

**Mental model: what the technique takes a person to be, and where it locates change.** This is the author's third spectrum, "the spectrum or mental model." The survey uses twenty-four models, from the psychodynamic and the person-centered, through the somatic, systemic, narrative, dialogic, contemplative, developmental, critical, and participatory, to the ritual, the Indigenous relational, the civic, and the organizational. Section 5 states each in plain words.

**Structure of the model.** The vault's survey of practice models (`ops/practice-model ecology.md`) sorted about seventy models already in the notes into eleven structural families: tending conditions, reading a difficulty by what it lacks, holding two poles, moving through stages, repeating a cycle, turning awareness back on itself, treating the self as many, combining elements, nesting levels, a word said aloud that acts, and letting something run to completion, with shifting a state directly and telling the real thing from its counterfeit recorded beside them. The survey tags every outside technique with the same families, so the outside field and the vault's own material can be compared on one scale.

**Who decides the content.** This coordinate is the book's own question asked of each technique: how much of what happens is decided by the person, and how much by the facilitator. It has five positions:
- **A** means that the person decides all the content, and the facilitator adds none;
- **B** means that the facilitator shapes the process, and the people supply the content;
- **C** means that the facilitator offers content or interpretation as an option the person may take or leave;
- **D** means that the facilitator directs, teaches, or interprets with authority;
- **E** means that no live facilitator is present, and a structure, a text, or a custom does the work.

**Medium.** The survey uses thirteen media: talk, silence, breath and body, movement and dance, touch, writing, art and image, voice and music, enactment and role, ritual and object, nature and place, reading, and technology.

**Time.** The survey uses six spans: a single moment, a session, a series of sessions, a residential stay, an ongoing community, and a whole life.

Running across all seven are the spectrums the author asked about in the plural. Each is a line with two ends, and a technique sits somewhere on it: directive to non-directive; content to process; the individual to the collective; words to the body; insight to action; the problem to what already works; the past to the future; structure to emergence; the expert to the peer; the secular to the sacred; and the human to the more-than-human. The entries do not score each technique on these lines. They supply what a reader needs to place a technique on any of them.

## 3. The field walked by scale

Each part below says what facilitation is at that scale, names techniques from the outside field with some of their own words, says what the vault holds there, and names what the vault has not met. The counts are outside techniques whose main scale this is, and vault holdings whose first scale this is. The entries file lists every technique at each scale.

### One person alone (27 outside techniques; 100 vault holdings)

At this scale the facilitator is a practice the person runs on themselves: a question asked each evening, a word repeated, a page written without stopping, or a walk taken alone. The outside techniques fall into four kinds. Some review the day or the life: the Ignatian examen, the Stoic evening review, the Islamic practice of *muḥāsaba* (self-accounting), Naikan, the life review, and the fourth step of Alcoholics Anonymous. Some rest attention on one thing: centering prayer with its "sacred word," mental noting, the Jesus Prayer, and the self-enquiry of Ramana Maharshi, "Who am I?" Some write: expressive writing (Pennebaker, 1986), morning pages, and Ira Progoff's Intensive Journal. And some go somewhere alone: the *poustinia* (a room or hut for solitary prayer), the Shikoku pilgrimage, and the dream incubation of the ancient Greek healing sanctuaries.

The vault holds this scale more than any other, mostly as contemplative doctrine read at its source texts: 29 of its 100 holdings here take the nondual contemplative model. What it has not met is the family of daily self-review, which several traditions reached with different questions. Sextius, as Seneca reports him, asked each night what fault he had cured that day, what vice he had resisted, and in what respect he was better (Seneca, *De Ira* 3.36). The Ignatian examen reviews how God was present in the day and how one responded. Naikan, founded by Yoshimoto Ishin in the 1940s, asks of each relationship what one received, what one returned, and what trouble one caused. *Muḥāsaba* weighs one's deeds before, in the saying attributed to Umar, they are weighed for one. The differences matter as much as the shared form: one review looks for faults, one for a presence, and one for what was received.

One solitary practice speaks directly to the vault's open disagreement over whether this work can be done alone (`notes/the solitary form stands against seven witnesses who hold a second person constitutive.md`). The pilgrim on the Shikoku circuit walks alone and is held by the tradition to be accompanied by Kōbō Daishi; the words *dōgyō ninin*, "two travelling together," are written on the pilgrim's hat. Naikan offers a second answer to the same question: the person reflects alone for about a week, and a guide visits briefly to hear what was recalled.

### One person and their parts (17 outside techniques; 3 vault holdings)

These techniques treat one person as many. The vault holds Internal Family Systems and the book's own practice of bowing to the gatekeeper, the corpus's figure for a protective part. The outside field holds fifteen more that the vault has not met: Voice Dialogue (Hal and Sidra Stone), Gestalt empty-chair and two-chair work, the two-chair tasks of emotion-focused therapy, psychosynthesis subpersonalities (Roberto Assagioli), ego state therapy, schema therapy's modes, Jung's active imagination, the multiple selves exercise of compassion-focused therapy, Virginia Satir's Parts Party, Hubert Hermans's dialogical self methods, Core Transformation, inner child work, the 3-2-1 shadow process, Tsultrim Allione's Feeding Your Demons, and Inner Relationship Focusing. The vault names a sixteenth, the ego states of transactional analysis, only in an unexamined question about the lineage of the Landmark Forum's work (`archive/reading — the routing surfaces recounted (speaking being, moreno, the scharmer count).md`).

Each tradition names differently the one who meets the parts, and the differences bear on the book's doctrine. Internal Family Systems calls it the Self and holds it already present in everyone. Voice Dialogue calls it the Aware Ego and holds that it is developed through the work. Schema therapy speaks of a healthy adult mode that the therapy strengthens. Inner Relationship Focusing speaks of Self-in-Presence, a friendly and curious listening to each part, and psychosynthesis of the "I" that can disidentify from any subpersonality. So the parts traditions divide on a fork the vault's list of what the book's center cannot see already records: "Deep change: recovery of the already-present, or genuine novelty?" (`ops/clearing.md`). The book's recognition doctrine sides with the Self that is already there; Voice Dialogue and schema therapy are company on the other side.

### A text or tool with no one present (13 outside techniques; 22 vault holdings)

This is the scale at which Book II's reader stands, and the vault holds it fairly well, mostly as thinkers on how a text works on a reader (Iser, Kierkegaard, Wittgenstein, Rilke's letters) and as the book's own devices. The outside field adds forms in which a text answers one person's request. In the Sayings of the Desert Fathers, a disciple asks an elder, "Give me a word, abba," and receives a short saying to live by; the collected exchanges were read by later monks as guidance. In spiritual direction by letter, a director answers a directee's letters, and the collected letters are later read by others, as happened with the letters attributed to Jean-Pierre de Caussade, published in 1861 as *Abandonment to Divine Providence*. The field also adds bibliotherapy, self-help books built on cognitive therapy, guided recordings, Julia Cameron's *The Artist's Way* as a course in a book, programmed instruction, internet-delivered cognitive therapy, consulting the *I Ching*, and the moral inventory of Alcoholics Anonymous. One finding from this band bears on Book II's form: internet-delivered therapy distinguishes "guided" programs, with brief contact from a professional, from "unguided" ones used alone, and the vault already records that the working alliance keeps its size when the exchange is written and delayed (`notes/the alliance holds its size when the exchange is mediated and written.md`).

### Two people, one helping (47 outside techniques; 40 vault holdings)

This is the largest scale in the field and the one the vault has met best (19 of the 47). The vault holds the older names well, most of them at their source texts: Rogers, Freud, Jung, Gendlin, Ignatius, the Zen private interview, the Sufi companionship of master and student, Socrates, and confession. It has not met most of the twentieth-century practice outside those lineages: motivational interviewing, solution-focused brief therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy, dialectical behavior therapy, EMDR, AEDP, Hakomi, emotion-focused therapy, mentalization-based treatment, Compassionate Inquiry, the Bonny Method of guided imagery and music, Morita therapy (1919), the GROW model of coaching, co-active and ontological coaching, Nancy Kline's Thinking Session, John Heron's six categories of intervention, Gerard Egan's skilled helper, and befriending as the Samaritans practise it.

Several of these name what a helper does to the other person's authorship, in words the vault could use directly:
- **Motivational interviewing** names the helper's urge to correct the person or supply the solution the "righting reflex," renamed the "fixing reflex" in its fourth edition, and trains the helper to hold it back while drawing out the person's own reasons for change.
- **Nancy Kline's Thinking Session** makes uninterrupted attention the helper's main act, and when the thinker reaches a limiting assumption, the partner helps the thinker form an "Incisive Question" from the thinker's own words.
- **John Heron's six categories** divide a helper's interventions into three that lead (prescriptive, informative, confronting) and three that draw on the person's own capacities (cathartic, catalytic, supportive), as a way to notice what one is doing from moment to moment. This entry rests on general knowledge and is marked unverified.
- **Ontological coaching**, founded on the work of Fernando Flores and developed by Rafael Echeverría and Julio Olalla, works with language, moods, and the body, and treats speech acts as its material, which places it beside the vault's family of practices in which a word said aloud acts.

### Two people as equals (17 outside techniques; 22 vault holdings)

Here the second person is a peer, and the forms usually take turns, so each person is helper and helped in turn: co-counselling, Focusing partnerships, the empathy exchange of Nonviolent Communication, one-on-one Circling, the dyad of the Enlightenment Intensive, *chavruta* (paired study in Jewish learning), listening partnerships, Insight Dialogue, Intentional Peer Support, the Buddhist *kalyāṇa-mittatā* (admirable friendship), the Irish *anam cara* (soul friend), Kline's Thinking Pair, the appreciative interview, and the thirty-six questions of Arthur Aron's closeness study. The vault holds this scale mostly as philosophy of the encounter (Buber, Bakhtin, Levinas, De Jaegher and Di Paolo) and through four practices from the author's own record, among them his question "what remains here?"

The outside field adds forms in which the second person's only job is to be present without steering. In Holotropic Breathwork's pairs, the sitter stays present and available without interfering or guiding. In Authentic Movement, a witness watches the mover without judging or interpreting, and the practice aims to develop in the mover an "inner witness," a capacity to observe one's own experience. That is a route from being witnessed to witnessing oneself, which is Book II's concern, and it sits beside the vault's reading of Winnicott (`notes/the deepest contact with your own inner life is first found alone in the presence of a non-impinging other.md`).

### Three people (18 outside techniques; 2 vault holdings, both from the author's own record)

Three people is the scale at which the author designs. The provenance record describes his design of 12 March 2026 as groups of three with sentence stems and a witness role that "speaks to the field, not just the content" (`provenance/provenance of the corpus round two.md`, lines 118–121), and his event of May 2026 ran on trio rotations with a witness role. No thinker, reading, practice note, or book form in the vault works at this scale.

The outside field has three kinds of triad. The first is the training triad: the practice triad of counselor and coach training with a client and an observer; Allen Ivey's microcounseling; Norman Kagan's Interpersonal Process Recall, in which an "inquirer" sits with a person watching a recording of their own conversation, and the person stops the tape whenever a feeling or thought comes back, while the inquirer asks open questions and offers no judgment or advice; and Paul Pedersen's Triad Training Model, in which a third role player, the "anticounselor," says aloud the negative things a client from another culture is thinking but not saying. The second is couples work: Imago Dialogue, with its mirroring; the Gottman method; emotionally focused couple therapy; integrative behavioral couple therapy; relationship enhancement; and discernment counseling for couples unsure whether to stay together. The third is mediation, in six models, among them transformative mediation (Robert Bush and Joseph Folger, 1994), whose two aims are "empowerment," a party becoming able to decide for themselves, and "recognition," a party becoming able to hear the other.

A session's reading, at the level "proposed": transformative mediation's two aims correspond, independently and at the scale of a conflict, to the two halves of the book's master question, empowerment to a person remaining the author of what they decide, and recognition to that person becoming able to be changed by hearing another.

### A small group (37 outside techniques; 33 vault holdings)

The vault holds this scale fairly well, 9 of its 33 holdings from the author's own record, but mostly through thinkers read for their ideas (Lewin, Moreno, Minuchin, Bohm, Spolin, Johnstone, Gurdjieff) rather than through the forms of group practice. The outside field's forms include T-groups, encounter groups, Tavistock study groups, group analysis, Irvin Yalom's interpersonal group therapy, psychodrama, Playback Theatre, Balint groups for doctors, the Quaker clearness committee, Parker Palmer's Circles of Trust, council, The Circle Way, Circling and the Authentic Relating games, the reflecting team, Open Dialogue's network meetings, structural and Milan family therapy, Satir's family sculpting, action learning sets, the dream appreciation group, dharma sharing in Plum Village, twelve-step meetings, Hearing Voices groups, the definitional ceremony, the Lewis Method of Deep Democracy, and Dynamic Facilitation.

Five unmet forms keep the person as the author of their own meaning in a group, each by a different rule:
- In **the clearness committee**, the members help a person find the answer within by listening and asking questions, and they deliberately give no advice, because the answer is sought in the person.
- In **the dream appreciation group** (Montague Ullman), members speak of the dreamer's dream as if it were their own, so every interpretation is offered as the speaker's own projection, and the dreamer decides at each stage whether to go on. The entry's name for this way of speaking rests on general knowledge and is marked unverified.
- In **the definitional ceremony** (Michael White, drawing on Barbara Myerhoff), outsider witnesses retell what struck them in a person's story, what images it evoked, why it resonated with their own lives, and how they were moved, and they are asked not to applaud, judge, give opinions, or hold up their own lives as moral examples.
- In **the reflecting team** (Tom Andersen), the professionals who have been observing talk tentatively among themselves while the family listens, and the family then comments on what the team said.
- In **Open Dialogue** (Jaakko Seikkula and colleagues), after a crisis, the team meets the person with their family and friends, responds to what each person says, and delays conclusions while a shared understanding forms, which its practitioners call tolerating uncertainty.

The field also holds documented harms at this scale. The Synanon Game, a harsh group confrontation, grew into a form its founder acknowledged as brainwashing, and Family Constellations is widely described as pseudoscientific, with its effects attributed to suggestion. The vault already records harms of this kind for the earlier syntheses it holds as case histories (`notes/the nearest prior attempts are case histories rather than interlocutors.md`) and for the est training, whose published safety record its note on Werner Erhard gives (`notes/werner erhard.md`); these two extend that record from people and trainings to group methods.

### A large group (21 outside techniques; 12 vault holdings)

The vault holds Werner Erhard's est and the Landmark Forum, Tony Robbins's events, and Thomas Hübl's collective trauma groups, and it mentions the Art of Hosting, Bohm dialogue, and sociodrama. The field adds Open Space Technology, World Café, Future Search, the Appreciative Inquiry summit, Liberating Structures, the Search Conference, Real Time Strategic Change, Worldwork, the Samoan circle, Pro Action Café, the unconference, graphic facilitation, the group relations conference, and Robert's Rules of Order.

At this scale the question of who decides becomes a matter of the room's design. Open Space Technology (Harrison Owen, 1985) has one law, the "Law of Two Feet": a person who is neither learning nor contributing goes somewhere else, so each person is responsible for their own participation, and the facilitator explains the principles and then stays out of the content. Liberating Structures publishes dozens of short meeting formats, each fixing the invitation, the arrangement of space, how participation is spread, how groups form, and the steps and timing, and a facilitator combines them as needed. Worldwork (Arnold Mindell) invites people to speak from the roles in a conflict and to switch roles, and it attends to rank and to the "ghost role," a voice that is felt but that no one present speaks for.

### An organization (32 outside techniques; 3 vault holdings)

This is the scale the author works at (he located the need for the books in his work as a software engineering manager, transcript line 862), and the vault holds only 3 things at this scale against 32 outside techniques. The field's forms include sociocracy's consent round, Holacracy, the advice process, consensus with blocks and stand-asides, the Quaker business method, Ignatian communal discernment, process consultation and humble inquiry (Edgar Schein), action research, double-loop learning and the ladder of inference, the learning organization's disciplines, Immunity to Change and the deliberately developmental organization, the after-action review, agile retrospectives, kaizen, Gestalt organization development, organizational constellations, the Cynefin framework, design thinking, LEGO Serious Play, the Delphi method, polarity management, scenario planning, the Japanese practice of *nemawashi* (quiet consultation before a decision), Systemic Modelling, which carries Clean Language into teams, and *pavāraṇā*.

Two of the oldest organizational forms in the field are religious, and both concern the books' question at the scale of an institution. The Rule of Benedict (c. 530) requires the abbot, before an important decision, to call the whole community to counsel and to hear everyone, the youngest included, because the Lord often reveals what is better to the younger (ch. 3). And at *pavāraṇā*, which closes the rains retreat, each monk invites the assembled community to point out any fault of his that they have seen, heard, or suspected. The Quaker business method is a third and younger religious form: the clerk listens for "the sense of the meeting" and drafts a minute that the meeting unites on or edits, and the meeting never votes.

### A community or public (29 outside techniques; 3 vault holdings)

The field's forms here are civic (citizens' assemblies, deliberative polling, National Issues Forums, study circles, the Wisdom Council, participatory budgeting), restorative (family group conferencing from Māori practice, victim-offender mediation, peacemaking circles, South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission, Rwanda's *gacaca* courts, the Pashtun *jirga*, and *sulha* in Arab communities), educational and theatrical (Freire's culture circles, the Highlander Folk School, participatory action research, photovoice, Boal's Forum Theatre), and organizing (the relational one-to-one meeting of the Industrial Areas Foundation, Marshall Ganz's public narrative, asset-based community development), together with dialogue across division (Sustained Dialogue, Reflective Structured Dialogue), the Pacific *talanoa* and Aboriginal Australian yarning, consciousness-raising, death cafés, and design charrettes.

The vault holds three entries at this scale and none of them is a community practice read at its source: its own account of standpoints held side by side, the Hasidic community as a companion in the survey of the second person, and one entry that is not facilitative. Of the 29 outside techniques it has met one, Freire's culture circle, and only as one root of the facilitated room (`notes/the facilitated room has several independent roots and improv's distinction is theorizing play as craft.md`), although a reply in the reframe conversation named Freire for the question of "who gets to name reality" (line 1795). The Industrial Areas Foundation holds a rule, the "iron rule," that one should never do for others what they can do for themselves. It sits beside the book's rule that nothing transforms from the audience as company with a difference kept: the iron rule is an organizer's rule for building people's power to act, and the book's rule is a claim about where change happens.

### A society's customs (29 outside techniques; 4 vault holdings)

Customs are facilitation with no facilitator, or with one the custom appoints. The field's customs include sitting shiva, the Irish wake and keening, the Māori *tangihanga* and *pōwhiri*, the Samoan *ifoga* (a ceremonial apology in which the family of the one who did harm sits covered in fine mats before the house of the wronged family until the mats are lifted), the family practice of *hoʻoponopono* as Mary Kawena Pukui described it, the sabbath, the Days of Awe, the Passover seder, bar and bat mitzvah, the quinceañera, the West African outdooring (naming ceremony), Día de los Muertos, the Qingming festival, the tea ceremony, the Hajj, the Camino de Santiago, carnival's role reversal, the potlatch, the Georgian feast led by its toastmaster, the salon, the talking stick, the Quaker meeting for worship, the sacrament of penance, the Lakota *inípi*, the Sámi *joik*, and *dadirri*, the deep listening of the Ngan'gikurunggurr and Ngen'giwumirri peoples as Miriam-Rose Ungunmerr-Baumann presents it. The vault holds four entries at this scale (van Gennep's rites of passage, Turner's liminality, Erikson's life cycle, and ubuntu), and it mentions the sabbath, the Quaker meeting, and the Dagara grief ritual.

For the books, the mourning customs bear most directly on a doctrine the vault already holds. The vault's note on grief records the book's doctrine that grief requires a witness and a ceremony, and it credits the communal ritual to Sobonfu Somé (`notes/grief cannot complete alone.md`). The field adds three more communal forms of that requirement, each with its own rule for the witnesses. At shiva, the Talmud rules that visitors may not offer words of consolation until the mourner speaks first (Moed Katan 28b). At a *tangihanga*, the body is never left alone, and visitors speak directly to the dead person, naming faults as well as virtues. At an Irish wake in its older form, a keening woman led a sung, partly improvised lament naming the dead person's lineage and virtues and the survivors' grief. The shiva rule is company for the clean question and the clearness committee, since all three hold back the helper's own words: the clean question uses only the person's words, the clearness committee only asks, and the shiva visitor waits for the mourner to speak first, as a law of mourning.

Other customs carry the book's concerns in their structure. The Passover seder builds in four kinds of child, the wise, the wicked, the simple, and the one who does not know how to ask, each answered differently, so the telling is fitted to each listener. And the Sámi verb for joiking takes its object directly: one joiks a friend and not about a friend, a grammar of address that bears on the book's concern with the call and the answering (`notes/the call and the answering.md`).

### The more-than-human (12 outside techniques; 3 vault holdings)

The vault holds Kimmerer and Abram, read together, Buber's tree met as a Thou, and the companions in the author's poems and reflection, the cats and the sky among them. The field adds the Work That Reconnects and the Council of All Beings, the wilderness vision fast, *shinrin-yoku* (forest bathing), the sit spot and Coyote Mentoring (Jon Young), the Haudenosaunee Thanksgiving Address, ecotherapy, animal-assisted and equine-assisted therapy, wilderness therapy (whose documented harms the entry records), and Welcome to Country. Two of these turn what the poems record, the company of animals and sky, into a practice. At a sit spot, a person returns again and again to one chosen place and gets to know it in all weathers and seasons. And the Thanksgiving Address greets, in order, the people, the earth, the waters, and on through the stars to the Creator before a gathering begins, closing each greeting with the refrain "Now our minds are one" and leaving each listener to give thanks for anything left out.

**The vault is richer than the field on three subjects.** Attention itself appears in 65 vault holdings against 48 outside techniques, the body in 43 against 27, and the spiritual or ultimate in 69 against 65. These are the subjects of the contemplative traditions, which supply 66 of the vault's 264 holdings, and of the somatic traditions and Gendlin's Focusing.

**Three subjects the field works on heavily are nearly absent from the vault.**
- **Conflict** appears in 55 outside techniques and in 2 vault holdings, Jessica Benjamin's account of mutual recognition and Salvador Minuchin's structural family therapy, both held as thinkers rather than as practices. The field's conflict techniques include six models of mediation, six forms of couples work, restorative circles, Worldwork and the Lewis Method of Deep Democracy, and the procedures a meeting uses to disagree, from consensus with blocks to Robert's Rules of Order. Conflict tests the book's master question where it is hardest to meet, since each party must remain the author of what they become while being changed by the other.
- **The future** appears in 26 outside techniques and in no vault holding. The field's future techniques ask a person or a group to picture a preferred future in detail: the miracle question of solution-focused therapy, the Dream phase of Appreciative Inquiry, the desired future that a Future Search describes as if it had already happened, and scenario planning. The Stoic premeditation of future evils also looks ahead, but it imagines the worst in advance rather than a preferred future. Section 13 sets these techniques beside the book's doctrine that "Reading is remembering" (`abridged` p. 1).
- **Decisions** appear in 65 outside techniques and in 17 vault holdings. The vault holds decision mostly through Ignatius's discernment of spirits and through the author's own practice of offering a question so that another person reaches their own conclusion. The field adds the clearness committee for one person's decision and, for groups, the consent round of sociocracy, the advice process, the Quaker business method, and consensus with blocks and stand-asides.

**Two subjects close to the book's doctrine are thin in the vault.**
- **Memory and the past** appear in 39 outside techniques and in 6 vault holdings, three of which are the book's own forms (Wiping the Mirror, Cutting the String, and the Backward Glance) and one the author's daily practice of a morning scan and an evening synthesis. The field's memory techniques recover a person's own history: the life review, Naikan, the examen and the Stoic evening review, expressive writing, the fourth-step inventory, psychodrama, and the definitional ceremony. The book's remembering is a different thing. Its front matter says "You already know everything in this book. You have just forgotten. Reading is remembering." (`abridged` p. 1), and the vault reads that remembering as the recognition of what never left rather than the recovery of a personal past (`notes/aletheia and pratyabhijna converge on truth as un-forgetting.md`). A memory technique borrowed from the field brings the recovery of a personal past with it, so the difference needs stating wherever the book borrows one.
- **A relationship with someone absent or dead** appears in 28 outside techniques and in 7 vault holdings. The vault's holdings include the executed draft's invitation "Think of someone who truly sees you" (`abridged` p. 7) and the prompt the author swapped into his Seattle event, "Think of someone who loves you. Describe how they see you." (`provenance/provenance of the corpus round two.md`, line 100). The field adds the empty chair of Gestalt therapy, the mourning customs of section 3, and the unsent letter, written to someone dead, absent, or unreachable with no intention of sending it. A session's reading, at the level "proposed": the unsent letter matters to the book's form, because the vault holds that the book's native genre is the letter, "the one genre whose every sentence expects an answer" (`notes/the book's native genre is the letter from a friend read alone.md`), and the unsent letter is the one use of that genre in which no answer is expected.

**Emotion is the largest gap by count.** It appears in 87 outside techniques and in 34 vault holdings. The outside techniques that engage emotion are spread across the small group, the helping dyad, one person alone, and parts work, so the gap is not confined to one scale.

**Grief, death, and nature are thin in both.** Grief appears in 17 outside techniques and 8 vault holdings, death and finitude in 14 and 4, and nature in 13 and 3. The mourning customs of section 3 are the field's densest material on grief, and half of the vault's eight grief holdings rest on Karla McLaren's work.

## 5. The mental models

The third coordinate asks what a technique takes a person to be and where it locates change. The survey used twenty-four models. Another 48 outside techniques and 46 vault holdings fit none of them well and were marked "other". Outside the vault these include Stoic philosophy practised as a way of life, twelve-step spirituality, restorative and transitional justice, deep ecology, and complexity science; in the vault they are mostly philosophers read for their ideas (Aristotle, Wittgenstein, Polanyi), empirical readings, and the corpus's own system. Each model below is stated as its own practitioners state it, with its count in the field and in the vault, some of its people, and some of its own words with plain meanings. Phrases in quotation marks are the tradition's own, recorded with their sources in the entries file.

Four findings stand out in the counts.
- The vault leans heavily toward one model. The contemplative nondual model accounts for 46 vault holdings against 15 outside techniques, and the existential and phenomenological model for 15 against 5.
- The field leans toward models the vault barely holds: the ritual and anthropological model (16 outside techniques, 3 vault holdings), the Indigenous and relational (17 and 2), learning theory and reflective practice (15 and 5), and organizational learning (11 and 2).
- Four models have no holding in the vault. Gestalt therapy appears in the vault only as a name in other people's lineages, among them the training Tony Robbins reports taking just after his twenty-first birthday (`notes/tony robbins.md`), and the solution-focused model, the process-oriented model of Arnold Mindell, and the civic and deliberative model are not mentioned in the vault at all.
- The two contemplative models are weighted in opposite directions. Outside the vault, the theistic contemplative traditions (26 techniques) outnumber the nondual (15); inside it, the nondual (46 holdings) outnumbers the theistic (20).

The **psychodynamic and psychoanalytic** model (13 outside techniques; 8 vault holdings) takes a person to be shaped by unconscious wishes, fears, and conflicts formed in early relationships, which show up as defenses and as feelings transferred onto present relationships. It locates change in bringing that material into awareness, largely through the relationship with the analyst and through interpretation. Its people include Sigmund Freud, Carl Jung, Melanie Klein, Donald Winnicott, and Wilfred Bion. Its own words include *transference* (the patient's projection onto the analyst of feelings about figures from early life); *countertransference* (the analyst's own reactions to the patient, which can be a source of information when recognized); *free association* (saying whatever comes to mind without censoring it); *resistance* (the ways a person avoids painful material during treatment); *holding environment* (Winnicott's term for reliable care that keeps impingements to a minimum).

The **humanistic and person-centered** model (17 outside techniques; 19 vault holdings) takes a person to have an inborn tendency toward growth and fulfillment that develops when conditions allow it. It locates change in a relationship in which the person feels accepted, understood, and met by a genuine other, so the helper supplies conditions rather than direction. Its people include Carl Rogers, Abraham Maslow, and William R. Miller and Stephen Rollnick (motivational interviewing, described as a person-centered approach). Its own words include *unconditional positive regard* (accepting the person without conditions on what they must feel or do); *congruence* (the helper's genuineness, with what is expressed matching what is felt); *empathic understanding* (sensing the person's inner frame of reference and communicating that understanding); *actualizing tendency* (an inbuilt tendency toward growth and fulfillment); *client-centered* (the view that the answers lie within the client rather than the therapist).

The **existential and phenomenological** model (5 outside techniques; 15 vault holdings) takes a person to be a free and responsible being who must face given conditions of existence, such as death, freedom, isolation, and the absence of ready-made meaning. It locates change in facing those conditions honestly in an authentic relationship and choosing how to live with them, and it treats distress about them as human rather than as illness. Its people include Ludwig Binswanger, Medard Boss, Viktor Frankl, Rollo May, and Irvin Yalom. Its own words include *ultimate concerns* (Yalom's four givens of existence: death, freedom and responsibility, isolation, and meaninglessness); *epoché or bracketing* (setting aside assumptions in order to describe experience as it appears); *paradoxical intention* (Frankl's technique of deliberately rehearsing the feared pattern); *here-and-now* (working with what is happening between the people present, now).

The **Gestalt therapy** model (4 outside techniques; 0 vault holdings) takes a person to be an organism in constant contact with an environment, whose difficulties arise where contact is interrupted or where past experience remains unfinished. It locates change in full awareness of what one is doing now, and by Arnold Beisser's paradoxical theory of change a person changes by becoming more fully what they already are rather than by trying to be different. Its people include Fritz Perls, Laura Perls, Paul Goodman, Arnold Beisser, and Gary Yontef. Its own words include *awareness* (being in touch with one's own existence and with what is); *contact boundary* (the point at which a person meets and is affected by others and the environment); *unfinished business* (unexpressed feelings from past relationships that keep pressing on the present); *experiment* (a task the therapist proposes to expand direct experience); *paradoxical theory of change* (change happens when a person becomes what they are, not when they force themselves to be otherwise).

The **cognitive and behavioral** model (10 outside techniques; 5 vault holdings) takes a person's feelings and actions to depend heavily on how they interpret events and on learned patterns of thought and behavior. It locates change in identifying those thoughts and patterns, testing them against evidence, and practicing new ones in a structured collaboration. Its people include Aaron Beck, Judith Beck, Marsha Linehan, and Steven C. Hayes. Its own words include *automatic thoughts* (quick, habitual thoughts that often carry negative views of the self, the world, or the future); *collaborative empiricism* (therapist and patient investigating the evidence for a belief together); *guided discovery* (the therapist designing questions and experiences through which the patient learns); *cognitive defusion* (in Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, reducing the tendency to treat thoughts as literal facts); *validation* (in dialectical behavior therapy, communicating that a response makes sense).

The **somatic and body-centered** model (4 outside techniques; 10 vault holdings) takes a person's history, including trauma and defense, to be present in the body as posture, muscular tension, breath, and patterns of nervous-system arousal. It locates change in working directly with bodily sensation and movement, in small and tolerable steps, so that defensive responses that were cut short can complete and the nervous system can settle. Its people include Wilhelm Reich, Alexander Lowen, Peter Levine, and Ron Kurtz. Its own words include *character armor* (Reich's term for protection organized into posture and breathing that outlasts the threat it answered); *grounding* (Lowen's term for feeling one's contact with the ground through the feet and legs); *titration* (approaching difficult material in very small amounts); *pendulation* (moving attention back and forth between activation and settling); *discharge* (the release of survival energy, for example through shaking and trembling).

The **experiential and Focusing-oriented** model (7 outside techniques; 13 vault holdings) takes a person to have an implicit bodily sense of their situation that is richer than what they can yet say. It locates change in attending to that felt sense until a fitting word or image comes, which brings a felt shift, a bodily release that Gendlin describes as the situation "carrying forward". Its people include Eugene Gendlin, Ann Weiser Cornell, Barbara McGavin, and Leslie Greenberg (emotion-focused therapy). Its own words include *felt sense* (a vague, still unformed bodily sense of a situation or issue); *handle* (a word, phrase, or image that fits the quality of the felt sense); *resonating* (checking the handle against the felt sense to see whether it fits); *felt shift* (an opening or release in the body, perhaps with a sigh, when something fits); *Focusing partnership* (two people who take turns focusing and listening).

The **systemic and family** model (9 outside techniques; 6 vault holdings) takes a person's difficulty to be part of a pattern of relationships in a family or other system, a pattern that keeps itself going through feedback. It locates change in altering the pattern, through questions that reveal it, new boundaries, or interventions that interrupt it, rather than in changing one individual alone. Its people include Gregory Bateson, Paul Watzlawick, John Weakland, Richard Fisch, and Salvador Minuchin. Its own words include *circular questioning* (asking family members about relationships and differences among the others); *neutrality* (the Milan team's guideline that the therapist not side with any member or explanation); *joining* (structural family therapy's term for the therapist's entry into the family's way of relating); *multidirected partiality* (siding with each family member in turn to evoke responsible mutual position-taking); *prescribing the symptom* (asking the family or person to continue the problem behavior on purpose).

The **narrative and social constructionist** model (7 outside techniques; 3 vault holdings) takes a person to live by the stories told about them and by them, and some of those stories become problem-saturated and limit what the person notices about their own life. It locates change in separating the person from the problem and co-authoring a richer account that includes the moments the problem story left out. Its people include Michael White, David Epston, Harlene Anderson, Harry Goolishian, and David Cooperrider (Appreciative Inquiry, which takes a social constructionist approach). Its own words include *externalizing conversations* (talking about the problem as separate from the person); *problem-saturated story* (an account of a life dominated by the problem); *unique outcomes* (moments that contradict the problem story); *re-authoring* (building a new account of oneself around one's values); *outsider witnesses* (listeners who respond to a person's preferred story with what resonated for them).

The **solution-focused** model (1 outside technique; 0 vault holdings) takes a person to already have resources and moments when the problem is absent or smaller, and it treats a detailed picture of the preferred future as more useful than an analysis of the problem's causes. It locates change in finding and building on what already works, in small steps the person names. Its people include Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg. Its own words include *miracle question* (imagining the problem solved overnight and describing what one would notice); *scaling question* (placing the situation on a scale from 0 to 10 and asking what one step higher would be); *exceptions* (times when the problem is less severe or absent); *coping questions* (questions about how the person has managed to carry on); *compliments* (direct and indirect acknowledgment of what the person is doing well).

The **dialogic** model (19 outside techniques; 22 vault holdings) takes a person to come into being in relation and in speech with others, so meaning is made between people rather than inside one of them. It locates change in conversation in which different voices stay distinct without merging and assumptions are noticed, so that something new can emerge between the participants. Its people include Martin Buber, Mikhail Bakhtin, David Bohm, Paulo Freire, and Harlene Anderson. Its own words include *I–Thou* (Buber's term for meeting another as a whole person rather than as an object); *polyphony* (Bakhtin's term for "a plurality of independent and unmerged voices and consciousnesses"); *unfinalizability* (Bakhtin's view that a person can never be fully and finally defined from outside); *suspension* (Bohm's practice of holding assumptions in view without acting on or suppressing them); *problem-posing education* (Freire's alternative to "banking" education, in which learners create knowledge through dialogue).

The **contemplative, nondual** model (15 outside techniques; 46 vault holdings) takes a person's deepest nature to be an awareness that is already present and not separate from what it knows, though it is habitually overlooked. It locates change in recognizing what was always the case, reached through practices that let a person notice, and stop relying on, the habit of treating thoughts and a separate self as final, rather than in adding something new. Its people include Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta (Kashmir Śaiva Pratyabhijñā), Śaṅkara (Advaita Vedānta), Longchenpa (Dzogchen), Dōgen, and Shunryu Suzuki. Its own words include *pratyabhijñā* (recognition, in the Kashmir Śaiva school, of what never left); *neti neti* ("not this, not this", the Upaniṣadic and Advaita method of negation); *shushō-ittō* (Dōgen's teaching that practice and realization are one); *shoshin* (beginner's mind, keeping a beginner's openness in long practice).

The **contemplative, theistic** model (26 outside techniques; 20 vault holdings) takes a person to be made for relationship with God, whose presence and guidance can be discerned in prayer, in community, and in the inner movements of feeling that the tradition attends to. It locates change in consenting to and discerning that presence, often with a companion or a community, rather than in the person's own effort alone. Its people include George Fox (the Quakers), Ignatius of Loyola, John Cassian, Thomas Keating, and Basil Pennington. Its own words include *clearness committee* (a Quaker group that helps a person reach clarity by questioning and reflection rather than advice); *Inner Light* (the Quaker conviction that the light of God is present in all people); *sacred word* (in centering prayer, a word used as a symbol of consent to God's presence and action); *spiritual direction* (accompanying a person's relationship with God without replacing their own choices); *discernment of spirits* (Ignatius's rules for telling apart inner movements of consolation and desolation).

The **developmental** model (4 outside techniques; 2 vault holdings) takes a person's way of making meaning to grow through a sequence of stages or orders, each able to handle more complexity than the one before. It locates change in the move from being embedded in a way of seeing to being able to look at it, a move that needs both challenge and support from the surrounding environment. Its people include Erik Erikson, James Marcia, Robert Kegan, Ken Wilber, and Mary Belenky and colleagues. Its own words include *meaning-making* (Kegan's term for the activity of understanding experience, which he treats as the activity of being a person); *self-authoring mind* (an order of consciousness that is internally directed rather than defined by others' expectations); *culture of embeddedness* (Kegan's term for the environment that holds, challenges, and releases a person at each stage); *immunity to change* (the title of Kegan's 2009 approach to what keeps a person from making changes they want); *identity crisis* (Erikson's term for the turning point in which a person works out who they are).

The **critical and liberatory** model (7 outside techniques; 3 vault holdings) takes a person's suffering and silence to be shaped by social structures of power and oppression, and it holds that education or therapy that ignores this can reproduce it. It locates change in critical consciousness, in which people name their situation together through dialogue and act to change it. Its people include Paulo Freire, Augusto Boal, and bell hooks. Its own words include *conscientização* (critical consciousness, recognizing and challenging systems of domination); *banking model* (Freire's name for education that deposits knowledge in passive learners); *problem-posing education* (education in which learners create knowledge with the teacher through dialogue); *spect-actor* (Boal's term for an audience member who steps in to act); *Forum Theatre* (a performance the audience stops so that audience members can replace the protagonist and try other actions).

The **participatory and emergent** model (17 outside techniques; 19 vault holdings) takes a group to hold more knowledge and more capacity to organize itself than any single leader, and what it needs emerges when the conditions for conversation are right. It locates change in inviting participants to set the agenda and supply the content themselves, while hosts look after the process and the harvest rather than the answers. Its people include Harrison Owen, Juanita Brown, David Isaacs, Henri Lipmanowicz, and Keith McCandless. Its own words include *Open Space* (an event format in which participants create the agenda and schedule on arrival); *fully present and invisible* (Owen's description of the ideal Open Space facilitator); *harvest* (the planned gathering of what a group has discovered); *co-operative inquiry* (research carried out "with" rather than "on" people, as co-researchers); *1-2-4-All* (a structure of thinking alone, then in pairs, then in fours, then all together).

The **ritual and anthropological** model (16 outside techniques; 3 vault holdings) takes a society to support its members through customs and rites that mark transitions in status and give each change a shared form. It locates change in a structured sequence: separation from the old status, a liminal period between, and incorporation into a new status that the community recognizes. Its people include Arnold van Gennep and Victor Turner. Its own words include *rites of separation* (acts that mark leaving a former status behind); *liminality* (the threshold period in which a person is between statuses); *incorporation* (the rejoining of society in a new, recognized status); *communitas* (Turner's term for the bond among equals in the liminal phase).

The **transpersonal** model (4 outside techniques; 8 vault holdings) takes a person's identity to extend beyond the individual ego and treats spiritual, mystical, and self-transcending experiences as part of human development. It locates change partly in such experiences and in integrating them into ordinary life, using practices drawn from Eastern and Western traditions. Its people include Abraham Maslow, Stanislav Grof, Anthony Sutich, Roberto Assagioli, and Ken Wilber. Its own words include *transpersonal* (beyond the personal; experiences in which identity extends past the individual self); *disidentification* (Assagioli's exercise of recognizing "I have a body, but I am not my body"); *subpersonalities* (Assagioli's term for the different roles and selves a person acts from in different settings); *self-transcendence* (going beyond the concerns of the individual self).

The **learning theory and reflective practice** model (15 outside techniques; 5 vault holdings) takes a person to learn from experience only when the experience is reflected on and the reflection is tested in further action. It locates change in a cycle of experience, reflection, conceptualizing, and experimenting, often in groups or organizations that study their own practice. Its people include John Dewey, David Kolb, Kurt Lewin, Chris Argyris, and Donald Schön. Its own words include *experiential learning cycle* (the sequence by which experience becomes learning through reflection and a further act); *double-loop learning* (Argyris's term for learning that questions the governing assumptions behind an action); *espoused theory and theory-in-use* (the difference between what people say guides them and what actually guides their actions); *reflection-in-action* (Schön's term for thinking about practice while doing it); *wait time* (Rowe's term for the pause between a teacher's question and the student's answer).

The **improvisation, play, and the arts** model (9 outside techniques; 7 vault holdings) takes a person's spontaneity and imagination to be resources that approval, fear, and habit tend to block. It locates change in play, enactment, and art-making, where people take on roles and respond in the moment, so that new responses are rehearsed in action rather than only discussed. Its people include J. L. Moreno, Viola Spolin, Keith Johnstone, Augusto Boal, and Mary Starks Whitehouse. Its own words include *spontaneity training and warm-up* (Moreno's named methods for training a person toward spontaneous action); *offer and block* (Johnstone's terms for proposing something in a scene and for preventing it from developing); *point of concentration* (Spolin's shared focal task that keeps attention off the self); *spect-actor* (Boal's term for an audience member who steps in to act); *mover and witness* (the two roles of Authentic Movement).

The **Indigenous and relational** model (17 outside techniques; 2 vault holdings) gathers many Indigenous traditions, and the southern African ethic of ubuntu, which take a person to be constituted through relationships with kin, community, ancestors, and the living world rather than to be a self-contained individual. They locate healing and change within those relationships, through shared forms such as circles, ceremony, and reciprocity, with knowledge kept and passed on by elders and communities. Its people include Albert D. Marshall and Murdena Marshall (Mi'kmaq Elders), Robin Wall Kimmerer, and Kay Pranis (a non-Indigenous practitioner who credits Native American circle traditions). Its own words include *Two-Eyed Seeing (Etuaptmumk)* (seeing with the strengths of Indigenous ways of knowing through one eye and of Western ways through the other, using both together); *talking piece* (an object passed around a circle that gives its holder the turn to speak); *keeper* (the circle keeper, who maintains the shared space without directing the group); *ubuntu* (the view that a person becomes a person through relation with others); *reciprocity* (a relationship of mutual exchange between people and a living world whose beings are addressed as subjects rather than objects).

The **process-oriented** model, also called process work (3 outside techniques; 0 vault holdings), takes a person or group to have experiences it identifies with and experiences it finds hard to identify with, and it treats disturbances as signals of what is trying to emerge. It locates change in following those signals through channels such as body feeling, movement, relationship, and world events, and in giving every viewpoint in a group a voice. Its people include Arnold Mindell. Its own words include *primary and secondary process* (the experiences a person identifies with, and those they find hard to identify with); *channels* (the modes in which experience appears: visual, auditory, movement, body feeling, relationship, and world); *deep democracy* (the stance that all viewpoints, including emotional and irrational ones, matter to a group's decisions); *rank* (social, psychological, and spiritual power differences among people); *worldwork* (process work applied to conflict, leadership, and social issues in groups).

The **organizational learning and management** model (11 outside techniques; 2 vault holdings) takes an organization to be a system whose people, structures, and shared habits of thinking determine what it can do. It locates change in learning at the level of the whole: examining shared assumptions, redistributing decisions, and improving how teams think and talk together. Its people include Kurt Lewin, Edgar Schein, Chris Argyris, Peter Senge, and Robert Greenleaf. Its own words include *double-loop learning* (learning that questions the assumptions behind a group's actions); *learning organization* (Senge's term for an organization built on systems thinking, personal mastery, mental models, shared vision, and team learning); *process consultation* (Schein's model of consulting as a helping relationship); *servant leadership* (Greenleaf's model in which the leader's first aim is to serve); *advice process* (a rule that anyone may decide after seeking advice from those affected and those with expertise).

The **civic and deliberative** model (11 outside techniques; 0 vault holdings) takes citizens to be capable of reaching better and more legitimate public decisions when they deliberate together with good information, balanced views, and equal consideration of arguments, rather than only voting or deferring to experts. It locates change in structured public conversations and representative citizen bodies whose conclusions inform decisions. Its people include Jürgen Habermas, Joshua Cohen, James Fishkin, and Joseph M. Bessette. Its own words include *deliberative democracy* (democracy in which deliberation is central to decision-making); *deliberative polling* (Fishkin's method of bringing a representative sample of citizens together for informed discussion); *citizens' assembly* (a body of ordinary citizens convened to deliberate on a public issue); *mini-public* (a small, representative group of citizens who deliberate on behalf of the wider public); *public sphere* (Habermas's term for the space in which citizens discuss matters of common concern).

## 6. Who decides the content

This coordinate is the book's own question asked of every technique. The table counts the outside techniques and the vault's holdings at each position, and it breaks out two parts of the vault's holdings: the 25 practices in the author's own record and the 13 practice forms in the executed draft (the twelve forms and the second chapter's invitation). Both are included in the vault's 264.

| Position | Outside techniques | Vault holdings | The author's practices | The book's forms |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A, the person decides all the content | 22 | 33 | 5 | 0 |
| B, the facilitator shapes the process | 114 | 51 | 15 | 0 |
| C, the facilitator offers content as an option | 62 | 13 | 0 | 0 |
| D, the facilitator directs or interprets with authority | 30 | 29 | 0 | 0 |
| E, no live facilitator | 71 | 138 | 5 | 13 |

**Most of the field sits at B.** In 114 of the 299 outside techniques the facilitator designs the process, keeps the time, and holds the agreements, and the people present supply everything that is said. B is the most common position at every scale from three people to a community: 8 of the 18 techniques for three people, 22 of 37 for a small group, 16 of 21 for a large group, 19 of 32 for an organization, and 20 of 29 for a community.

**The author works at B, and the book's forms work at E.** Fifteen of the author's 25 practices sit at B, among them the event script he brought to Seattle, "five agreements, trio rotations with a witness role, closing circle" (`provenance/provenance of the corpus round two.md`, line 97). All 13 of the book's forms sit at E, because the reader carries them out with no facilitator present. The corpus records one such conversion in its own words. The treatment says that "'Calling the Room' is the facilitator's move — the field made explicit — turned into a solo form" (`arc-gloss` p. 15), and the executed draft gives the form as "Name what is alive in the field. I notice we've gotten tense." (`abridged` p. 42). A reader who uses that form speaks aloud to the people they are with, so the book at E hands its reader a move that sits at B once the reader uses it. That bears on the author's idea, recorded in the reframe conversation, of a first draft "whose readership was exclusively facilitators themselves," which would make the book "just a facilitation of facilitation" (line 1358).

**Few outside techniques sit at A, and the vault holds that position relatively well.** In 22 outside techniques the helper adds no content at all, and the helper's only acts are attention and questions built from the person's own words. They cluster in the dyads: person-centred therapy, Clean Language, Nancy Kline's Thinking Session and Thinking Pair, befriending as the Samaritans practise it, co-counselling, Focusing partnerships, listening partnerships, the sitter in Holotropic Breathwork, and the witness in Authentic Movement. Interpersonal Process Recall and transformative mediation hold the same position with three people, and four community practices and customs hold it with more: sitting shiva, where visitors wait for the mourner to speak first; the Quaker meeting for worship; consciousness-raising; and the death café. The vault holds 33 items at A, 15 of them in the helping dyad, largely through its readings of Rogers, Gendlin, and Winnicott, and 5 of them are the author's own, among them his question "what remains here?"

**E has two kinds, and the difference bears on Book II.** Of the 71 outside techniques at E, 26 are practices of one person alone, 12 are texts or tools with no one present, and 16 are a society's customs. A text at E gives its rules to one reader. A custom at E carries its rules in a practice that the people present keep for one another: whoever holds the talking stick speaks while the others listen; at an *ifoga* the family of the one who did harm sits covered in fine mats until the wronged family lifts them; on the sabbath the day's rules hold for everyone in the household. A custom does a facilitator's work without a facilitator because everyone present knows the rule. A session's reading, at the level "proposed": a book that gave its readers a rule to keep together, rather than a practice each performs alone, would sit at E in the way a custom does. The executed draft comes nearest to this when it tells a reader in crisis to use Calling the Room "with the people who can be called (do not isolate)" (`abridged` p. 45).

## 7. The single moves that cross every scale

A move is smaller than a technique: one act that a facilitator, a helper, or a person alone makes, such as asking an open question, saying back what was heard, or pausing. The survey gathered 61 moves that recur across scales and traditions and grouped them by what the move does. Each entry gives the move's position on who decides, what happens in it, the other names traditions give it, and whether the vault holds it. Phrases in quotation marks are the practitioners' own, recorded with their sources in the entries file; 41 of the 61 moves are verified at those sources and 20 in part, and the entries mark which.

Moves matter to the book because a move is the unit a book can most easily hand to a reader, in the way the corpus turned the facilitator's move into Calling the Room (section 6). The vault holds 21 of the 61. Among those it has not met, four speak directly to the question of who decides:
- asking permission before offering, by which a helper leaves the choice of hearing advice with the person;
- the contact statement, which names only what the person is experiencing now;
- the consent round, which gives every member of a group a way to stop a decision without a vote;
- the pause in The Circle Way, which any member may call.

### Questions
- In **the open question** (who decides: B), the facilitator asks a question that cannot be answered with yes, no, or a single fact, such as "What is that like for you?" It is also known as open questions, the O in OARS (motivational interviewing); open-ended questions (counselling skills training).
- In **the clean question** (who decides: A), the facilitator asks a short question of fixed form that uses only the person's own words, such as "And what kind of X is that X?" or "And is there anything else about X?", and adds no ideas, metaphors, or assumptions of their own. It is also known as clean questions (Clean Language, David Grove); the questions of Symbolic Modelling (James Lawley and Penny Tompkins, who built on Grove's work). The vault holds it.
- In **the circular question** (who decides: B), the therapist asks one family member about the relationship or the differences between other members who are present, for example who is most upset when a certain thing happens, and uses each answer to shape the next question. It is also known as circular questioning (Milan systemic family therapy).
- In **the scaling question** (who decides: B), the facilitator asks the person to place their situation on a scale, usually from 0 to 10, then asks what puts it at that number rather than lower and what one step higher would look like. It is also known as scaling questions (solution-focused brief therapy).
- In **the miracle question** (who decides: B), the facilitator asks the person to imagine that a miracle happens overnight while they sleep and the problem is solved, then asks them to describe in detail what they would notice first the next morning. It is also known as the miracle question (solution-focused brief therapy, Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg); questions of the Dream phase, envisioning "what might be" (Appreciative Inquiry).
- In **the exception question** (who decides: B), the facilitator asks about times when the problem was absent, weaker, or handled well, and then asks what was different at those times. It is also known as exception questions, such as "Are there times when this has been less of a problem?" (solution-focused brief therapy); coping questions, such as "How have you managed to carry on?" (solution-focused brief therapy); unique outcomes, moments that contradict the problem story (narrative therapy).
- In **asking "what else?"** (who decides: A), after the person answers, the listener asks whether there is anything else or anything more, and keeps asking until the person says there is nothing more. It is also known as "And is there anything else about...?" (Clean Language); "Is there more about that?" (Imago Dialogue); "And what else?", the AWE question (Michael Bungay Stanier, The Coaching Habit, 2016).
- In **the Socratic question** (who decides: C), the helper asks a sequence of questions that lead the person to examine the evidence, assumptions, and consequences of a belief, so that the person reaches a conclusion by their own reasoning. It is also known as Socratic questioning and guided discovery (cognitive therapy, Aaron Beck and Judith Beck); the midwife's questioning, maieutics (Socrates in Plato's Theaetetus); Socratic questioning in critical-thinking education (Richard Paul and Linda Elder). The vault holds it.
- In **asking what is alive** (who decides: A), a person asks another, or themselves, what is alive in them right now, meaning which feelings are present and which needs those feelings point to. It is also known as "What's alive in you?" (Nonviolent Communication, Marshall Rosenberg); Calling the Room, which names what is alive between the people in it (a practice form in the executed draft, `abridged` p. 42). The vault holds it.
- In **asking permission before offering** (who decides: C), before giving advice, information, or an observation, the helper asks whether the person wants to hear it, and accepts a no. It is also known as asking permission to give advice (motivational interviewing); getting permission from protective parts (Internal Family Systems).

### Reflecting and receiving
- In **reflecting back** (who decides: A), the listener says back, in their own words or the speaker's, what they understood the speaker to mean or feel, and leaves room for the speaker to correct it. It is also known as reflection of feeling (client-centered therapy, Carl Rogers); testing understandings, or checking perceptions (Rogers's own proposed renaming, 1986); reflective listening (motivational interviewing). The vault holds it.
- In **summarizing** (who decides: B), the listener gathers several things the speaker has said into one short account, offers it back, and asks whether anything is missing. It is also known as summaries, the S in OARS (motivational interviewing); summary mirror (Imago Dialogue).
- In **mirroring** (who decides: B), the receiver in a structured dialogue between partners repeats the sender's message word for word or in close paraphrase and asks "Did I get that?" and "Is there more about that?" before responding at all; then the two switch roles. It is also known as mirroring, the first step of the Imago Dialogue ("Let me see if I've got you. You said..."); summary mirror (Imago Dialogue).
- In **the contact statement** (who decides: A), the therapist makes a very short statement that names the person's present experience rather than the content of their story, such as "Sad, huh?" It is also known as contact statements (Hakomi method, Ron Kurtz); brief reflection of feeling (person-centered practice).
- In **tracking** (who decides: A), the facilitator continuously watches the person's present experience as it shows in posture, breath, voice, gesture, and small changes of expression, without commenting on all of it. It is also known as tracking (Hakomi method).
- In **validation** (who decides: C), the listener communicates that the other person's feeling or view makes sense given their situation or history, without having to agree with it. It is also known as validation, described in six levels from being present to radical genuineness (dialectical behavior therapy, Marsha Linehan, 1997); validation, the second step of the Imago Dialogue ("You make sense to me, and what makes sense is...").
- In **normalizing** (who decides: C), the helper tells the person that their reaction is one that many people have in the same circumstances. It is also known as normalizing, recognizing reactions that anyone would have (the fifth level of validation in Marsha Linehan's scheme, dialectical behavior therapy).
- In **labelling a feeling** (who decides: A), the person, or a helper with them, puts a present feeling into a word, such as "angry" or "afraid". It is also known as affect labeling (Matthew Lieberman and colleagues, 2007); "name it to tame it" (Daniel Siegel and Tina Payne Bryson); naming feelings and the needs behind them (Nonviolent Communication). The vault holds it.
- In **the witness role** (who decides: C), one person is given the role of watching another person's process without taking part, and afterward speaks about what they saw and what it stirred in them, without judging or interpreting the other. It is also known as the witness, who watches the mover "without judgement, projection or interpretation" (Authentic Movement, Mary Starks Whitehouse and Janet Adler); outsider witnesses in a definitional ceremony (narrative therapy); the audience, who witness an enactment and later share (psychodrama). The vault holds it.

### Slowing, pausing, and staying
- In **silence and waiting** (who decides: A), the facilitator or the group stays silent after a question or a statement for longer than ordinary conversation allows and does not fill the gap. In unprogrammed Quaker worship a whole meeting sits in silence, a person speaks only when moved to, and several minutes often pass between speakers. It is also known as wait time (Mary Budd Rowe, science education, coined 1972); unprogrammed meeting for worship, sitting in silence until someone is moved to speak (Quakers); the silent form of circle (The Circle Way). The vault holds it.
- In **the pause** (who decides: B), someone stops the conversation or activity for a short, agreed time, such as three slow breaths, and everyone breathes and waits before going on. In The Circle Way a volunteer guardian rings a bell to call the pause, rings it again to end it, and anyone may ask for one. It is also known as practice the pause, called by the circle guardian's bell (The Circle Way).
- In **titration** (who decides: B), the practitioner approaches difficult material in very small amounts, one small piece at a time, and lets the body settle before going further. It is also known as titration, approaching trauma "drop by drop" (Somatic Experiencing, Peter Levine). The vault holds it.
- In **pendulation** (who decides: B), the practitioner guides the person's attention back and forth between activation or distress in the body and a place of settling or ease, in small cycles. It is also known as pendulation (Somatic Experiencing, Peter Levine).
- In **grounding** (who decides: B), the person brings attention to physical contact with the ground and to the immediate surroundings, for example by feeling the feet on the floor or naming things they can see and hear right now. It is also known as grounding (bioenergetic analysis, Alexander Lowen); 5-4-3-2-1 sensory grounding, naming things one sees, hears, feels, smells and tastes (trauma therapy).
- In **resourcing** (who decides: B), the practitioner helps the person find, and feel in the body, something that gives safety, strength, or comfort, such as a supportive person, a place, or a sensation of steadiness, before or during difficult work. It is also known as resourcing (Somatic Experiencing); coping questions, such as "How have you managed to prevent things from becoming worse?" (solution-focused brief therapy).
- In **noting** (who decides: E), the meditator silently names each experience as it arises, such as "rising, falling" for the breath or "hearing" for a sound, without further thinking about it. It is also known as noting, or mental noting, such as "rising, falling" (Mahasi Sayadaw's vipassana method); noting (Shinzen Young's teaching, which adapts Mahasi's technique).
- In **suspending assumptions** (who decides: A), a person notices an assumption or reaction arising and keeps it in view without acting on it and without suppressing it; in Bohm's words, "You don't believe them, nor do you disbelieve them". It is also known as suspension (David Bohm's dialogue); epoché or bracketing (Edmund Husserl's phenomenology). The vault holds it.
- In **staying with what arises** (who decides: A), the person keeps attention on a feeling or sensation that is present, especially an unpleasant one, and lets it be there without trying to change, fix, or get away from it. It is also known as "stay with it" (Gestalt therapy); keeping it company (Inner Relationship Focusing, Ann Weiser Cornell and Barbara McGavin); Allow, the second step of RAIN (Tara Brach's teaching). The vault holds it.
- In **checking a word against the felt sense** (who decides: A), the person goes back and forth between a word or image and the bodily felt sense it is meant to describe and checks whether the word fits; when it does not fit, they wait for the felt sense to give a better word. It is also known as resonating (Focusing, Eugene Gendlin); the felt sense's refusal of a word that does not fit, which the vault holds is what separates the felt sense from fluency (`notes/the felt sense rejects formulations and that friction is what separates it from fluency.md`). The vault holds it.

### Structures for a group
- In **the check-in** (who decides: B), at the start of a meeting each person in turn says briefly how they are arriving and what brings them, usually without discussion or response from others. It is also known as check-in, the first round of sharing (The Circle Way); the opening round (group facilitation).
- In **the check-out or closing circle** (who decides: B), at the end of a gathering each person in turn says briefly what they are taking with them, what they learned, or what they appreciated, and the facilitator then marks the close, for example with a short silence. It is also known as check-out (The Circle Way); ceremonies that mark the closing of a circle (peacemaking circles, Kay Pranis); the closing circle (the author's own event script, May 2026, `provenance/provenance of the corpus round two.md`, line 97). The vault holds it.
- In **the talking piece** (who decides: B), an object is passed around the circle, and only the person holding it speaks while the others listen. It is also known as talking piece (peacemaking circles, Kay Pranis, drawing on Native American traditions); talking piece circle (The Circle Way).
- In **the round or go-around** (who decides: B), the facilitator asks every person in the circle to speak in turn on the same question, one after another, with no one skipped and no cross-talk. In the Gestalt game called making the rounds, a single person instead says something to each member in turn. It is also known as rounds, in which each person speaks in turn during decisions (sociocracy); making the rounds, in which one person addresses each group member in turn (Gestalt group therapy, Levitsky and Perls); talking piece circle (The Circle Way).
- In **the fishbowl** (who decides: B), a few people, traditionally five, talk in an inner circle while everyone else sits around them and listens. In an open fishbowl one inner chair stays empty, anyone from the outer circle may take it, and a current member then leaves. It is also known as open fishbowl and closed fishbowl (group facilitation and classroom teaching).
- In **the pair-share** (who decides: B), people first think alone for a minute, then talk in pairs, and then share with a larger group; 1-2-4-All adds a round in fours before the whole group. It is also known as think-pair-share (Frank Lyman, University of Maryland, 1981); 1-2-4-All (Liberating Structures, Henri Lipmanowicz and Keith McCandless).
- In **harvesting** (who decides: B), the hosts decide in advance how what a group discovers will be gathered and made visible, such as notes, drawings, a shared report, or an agreement to act, and they collect it during and after the conversations. It is also known as the harvest (Art of Hosting); harvesting and sharing collective discoveries, the seventh design principle of the World Café (Juanita Brown and David Isaacs); debriefing documents (Open Space Technology).
- In **the consent round** (who decides: B), the facilitator asks each person in turn whether they have a reasoned, paramount objection to a proposal, meaning a reason it would harm the group's ability to work toward its aim; the proposal passes when no such objection remains. It is also known as consent round, asking for any reasoned and paramount objection (sociocracy, Gerard Endenburg).
- In **the advice process** (who decides: E), any person may make any decision after seeking advice from everyone who will be meaningfully affected and from people with expertise; the advice must be considered, but the decision-maker does not have to follow it. It is also known as the advice process (Dennis Bakke at AES Corporation); the advice process in self-managing organizations (Frederic Laloux, Reinventing Organizations).
- In **group agreements** (who decides: B), at the start, the group states or builds a short list of commitments about how members will treat one another, such as keeping confidentiality and listening without judgment, and returns to them when needed. It is also known as agreements, such as "Listen with curiosity and compassion, withholding judgment" (The Circle Way); guidelines agreed by consensus (peacemaking circles, Kay Pranis); community agreements (education and anti-oppression facilitation). The vault holds it.
- In **step up, step back** (who decides: B), each participant watches their own share of the talking: people who usually stay quiet are invited to speak more, and people who usually speak a lot are invited to hold back so others can speak. It is also known as step up, step back (community agreements in education); move up, move up (a variant in anti-oppression facilitation).
- In **the appreciation round or blessing** (who decides: B), near the end of a gathering, people take turns speaking an appreciation, a thanks, or a good wish to the group or to particular people. It is also known as a closing round in which people say what they learned, heard, or appreciated (The Circle Way); leaving "with the blessings of the group" (The Circle Way).

### Enactment and experiment
- In **doubling** (who decides: C), a group member stands beside or behind the protagonist and speaks aloud the thoughts and feelings the protagonist has not yet expressed. It is also known as doubling, played by an auxiliary ego (psychodrama, J. L. Moreno). The vault holds it.
- In **role reversal** (who decides: B), the protagonist steps into the role of another person in their life, such as a parent, and speaks and acts as that person, while someone else plays the protagonist. It is also known as role reversal (psychodrama, J. L. Moreno).
- In **sculpting** (who decides: B), one person arranges other people, or objects, in space and in postures to show how they experience the relationships in a family or group, without using words; then the group looks at the arrangement and speaks from it. It is also known as family sculpture (David Kantor with Fred and Bunny Duhl, Boston Family Institute, from 1969); family sculpting and family reconstruction (Virginia Satir); choreography (Peggy Papp).
- In **the empty chair** (who decides: B), the person speaks to an imagined person placed in an empty chair, often someone absent or dead with whom they have unresolved feelings, and says what was never said. It is also known as the empty chair (Gestalt therapy, Fritz Perls); the auxiliary chair (psychodrama, which Perls adapted); empty-chair work for unfinished business (emotion-focused therapy, Leslie Greenberg).
- In **the two-chair dialogue** (who decides: B), the person gives two conflicting parts of themselves a chair each, such as an inner critic and the criticized self, and moves between the chairs so that each part speaks directly to the other. It is also known as the two-chair technique to resolve splits (Gestalt therapy); games of dialogue between "top dog" and "under dog" (Gestalt therapy, Levitsky and Perls, 1970); two-chair work for self-critical splits (emotion-focused therapy, Leslie Greenberg).
- In **trying on a sentence** (who decides: C), the facilitator offers a short sentence and asks the person to say it aloud, or to hear it said, and to notice what happens inside them in response, such as whether it feels true, false, or stirs something. It is also known as the probe, "What happens when I say..." (Hakomi method); feeding a sentence (Gestalt therapy; the phrase "May I feed you a sentence?" is attributed to Levitsky and Perls but was not checked at their text).
- In **the little experiment in mindfulness** (who decides: C), while the person is in a quiet, mindful state, the therapist offers a small deliberate input, such as a sentence ("Your life belongs to you"), a touch, or a gesture, and both notice together what happens inside the person in response. It is also known as the probe, "What happens when I say..." (Hakomi method, Ron Kurtz); the experiment (Gestalt therapy).
- In **the sentence stem** (who decides: B), the facilitator supplies the opening words of a sentence, and the participant completes it in their own words, often several times in a row. It is also known as lead sentences of the Imago Dialogue, such as "You make sense to me, and what makes sense is..."; sentence stems (Authentic Relating, in the author's own practice record, `provenance/provenance of the corpus round two.md`, lines 118–121). The vault holds it.
- In **accepting an offer ("yes, and")** (who decides: B), a participant accepts what another has just proposed as given and then adds something that builds on it, instead of rejecting or correcting it. It is also known as "Yes, and" (improvisational theatre, later used in brainstorming and business); accepting offers rather than blocking them (Keith Johnstone, Impro, 1979). The vault holds it.

### Parts and protection
- In **honoring a protective part** (who decides: B), the facilitator, or the person alone, addresses the part of the self that guards against pain with respect, asks about its concerns, and asks its permission before going toward what it protects, instead of trying to push past it. It is also known as getting permission from the parts that protect, before approaching what they protect (Internal Family Systems, Richard Schwartz); taking over, supporting a defense instead of fighting it (Hakomi method); meeting the gatekeeper with a bow, never a fight (the book's own corpus, `walked` p. 12). The vault holds it.
- In **stepping back from a part or a thought** (who decides: B), the person notices a feeling, thought, or inner voice and relates to it as something they have, at a small distance, rather than as what they are, for example by saying "a part of me is afraid" or by imagining setting a concern down beside them. It is also known as unblending, asking a part to step back so the Self can be present (Internal Family Systems); cognitive defusion (Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, Steven C. Hayes); disidentification, "I have a body, but I am not my body" (psychosynthesis, Roberto Assagioli). The vault holds it.

### Offering meaning
- In **reframing** (who decides: C), the helper offers a different frame in which the same facts make a different sense, for example describing a behavior as protection rather than as stubbornness. It is also known as reframing and relabeling (Mental Research Institute brief therapy; the term was set out by Paul Watzlawick, John Weakland and Richard Fisch in Change, 1974); reframing (structural family therapy).
- In **externalizing the problem** (who decides: B), the therapist talks about the problem as a separate thing with its own name and asks how it influences the person's life and how the person influences it. It is also known as externalising conversations (narrative therapy, Michael White and David Epston). The vault has not read it: its reading queue holds a question for White and Epston's chapter on externalizing (`ops/reading queue.md`, entry 28).
- In **interpretation** (who decides: D), the analyst tells the patient a proposed meaning of what the patient has said, dreamed, or done, often linking it to an unconscious conflict, a defense, or feelings the patient has toward the analyst. It is also known as interpretation (psychoanalysis and psychodynamic therapy). The vault holds it.
- In **amplification** (who decides: C), the analyst or the dreamer first gathers the dreamer's own associations to a dream image, then sets the image beside parallel images from myth, religion, history, and culture, and then returns to the dreamer's own life. It is also known as amplification (Jungian analytical psychology).
- In **paradoxical intention or prescribing the symptom** (who decides: D), the therapist asks the person to do deliberately, or even to increase, the very thing they fear or are trying to stop, for example to try to produce the feared symptom on purpose. It is also known as paradoxical intention (Viktor Frankl, logotherapy, term coined 1939); prescribing the symptom, and restraining or going slow (Mental Research Institute brief therapy); counterparadox (Milan systemic therapy).
- In **challenge or confrontation** (who decides: D), the helper points out directly a discrepancy, a defense, or an avoided fact, for example the gap between what the person says and what they do. It is also known as confronting the patient's ego with its defenses (psychoanalysis); the Game, a harsh group confrontation (Synanon, 1958 to 1991).

### Speaking from oneself
- In **self-disclosure by the facilitator** (who decides: C), the facilitator shares something of their own feeling, reaction, or experience with the person, briefly and for the person's benefit. It is also known as therapist self-disclosure (psychotherapy); the therapist's self-expression of observations, preferences, feelings and experience (Gestalt therapy, as Gary Yontef describes presence).
- In **the I-statement** (who decides: B), a person speaks about their own feeling or reaction in the first person and connects it to a specific event, for example "When you said that, I felt hurt", instead of describing the other person's character. It is also known as I-message (Thomas Gordon, Parent Effectiveness Training, 1970); observations, feelings, needs and requests (Nonviolent Communication, Marshall Rosenberg); "I" language in the Imago Dialogue. The vault holds it.
- In **the here-and-now statement** (who decides: B), a person says what they are experiencing, or what is happening between the people present, at this moment, instead of reporting events from elsewhere. In Yalom's account the group then examines what the moment showed. It is also known as working in the here-and-now, followed by illumination of what happened (group psychotherapy, Irvin Yalom); the principle of the now (Gestalt therapy, Levitsky and Perls, 1970); Reveal Your Experience, first-person, present-tense and bodily (Authentic Relating, in the author's own practice record, `provenance/provenance of the corpus round two.md`, lines 118–121). The vault holds it.

## 8. Where traditions name something similar in different words

The survey's researchers gathered twelve places where several traditions name a similar thing, and for each tradition they recorded the difference that keeps it apart from the others. The similarity is company and not proof. The researchers chose the clusters because they bore on the book's concerns, so part of the resemblance among the members of a cluster was produced by the choosing. The differences are the useful part: each one is a choice the book makes in one direction or the other whether or not it names the choice. Each table gives the tradition, the name it uses, and how its version differs. Where the vault or the author's record is one of the members, the row names the file.

### The facilitator's restraint from supplying content

| Tradition | Its name | How its version differs |
|---|---|---|
| Clean Language (David Grove) | *clean questions* | The restraint is built into the grammar of the questions, which may use only the person's own words inside a fixed set of forms. |
| Collaborative therapy (Harlene Anderson and Harry Goolishian) | *not-knowing* | The restraint is a stance toward knowledge: the therapist treats the client as the expert on the client's life and works without diagnostic labels. |
| Psychoanalysis (Wilfred Bion) | *without memory, desire or understanding* | The restraint is an inner discipline of the analyst's own mind, and the analyst still offers interpretations. |
| Socratic philosophy (Plato's Theaetetus) | *the barren midwife* | The helper claims no wisdom of their own yet actively judges whether what the other brings forth is sound or false. |
| The Quaker clearness committee | *questioning without advice* | The restraint is a rule for a whole group and rests on the religious conviction that the inner light is present in every person. |
| Gestalt therapy (Fritz Perls) | *"I avoid any interpretation"* | Perls refused to interpret the meaning of what the client said while still directing the process with instructions and experiments. |
| Motivational interviewing | *evocation* | The helper draws out the person's own reasons, but gives particular attention to the language of change, so the restraint serves a direction. |
| Open Space Technology (Harrison Owen) | *fully present and invisible* | The restraint applies to a whole event: the facilitator sets up the space and the participants create the agenda. |
| The vault (`notes/the helper's restraint is the active ingredient rather than the absence of one and it is trained.md`) | *the helper's restraint as a trained discipline* | The vault treats restraint as an active, trained ingredient that keeps the change the participant's own, not as passivity. |

### The witness who receives without judging

| Tradition | Its name | How its version differs |
|---|---|---|
| Authentic Movement (Mary Starks Whitehouse, Janet Adler) | *the witness* | The witness watches the mover's movement "without judgement, projection or interpretation" while also attending to their own sensations, and speaks only afterward. |
| Narrative therapy (Michael White) | *outsider witnesses* | The witnesses respond to a person's preferred story with the phrases and images that resonated for them, so the witnessing is used to acknowledge a new account of a life. |
| Psychodrama (J. L. Moreno) | *the audience who later share* | The group witnesses an enactment as its audience and shares with the protagonist only after the action is over. |
| The author's triad design of 12 March 2026 (`provenance/provenance of the corpus round two.md`, lines 118–121) | *the triad witness who "speaks to the field, not just the content"* | The witness reports on what happened between the two others, not only on what each said. |
| Person-centered therapy (Carl Rogers) | *unconditional positive regard* | The acceptance belongs to the helper inside an active helping relationship, not to a separate observer. |
| The Circle Way (Christina Baldwin and Ann Linnea) | *listen with curiosity and compassion, withholding judgment* | Every member of the circle takes this stance toward every speaker, so no single person holds the witness role. |
| Inner Relationship Focusing (Ann Weiser Cornell and Barbara McGavin) | *Self-in-Presence* | The witness is inward: the person's own self listens with equanimity to their own felt experience. |

### The container or holding environment

| Tradition | Its name | How its version differs |
|---|---|---|
| Psychoanalysis (Donald Winnicott) | *holding environment* | It is named by what it must not do: reliable provision that keeps impingements to a minimum and is scarcely noticed. |
| Psychoanalysis (Wilfred Bion) | *container and contained* | The container actively receives and processes another person's unbearable feelings, rather than only providing a reliable setting. |
| Popular helping language (Heather Plett's 2015 definition) | *holding space* | It is a non-clinical version: walking alongside a person without judging, fixing, or trying to affect the outcome. |
| The Circle Way | *the center that "holds neutral space"* | The container is shared by the group, whose members rely on the center while leadership rotates among them. |
| Social justice education (Brian Arao and Kristi Clemens) | *brave space* | The container is defined by agreements that expect controversy and discomfort rather than a promise of safety. |
| Constructive-developmental psychology (Robert Kegan) | *culture of embeddedness* | The holding environment must both support and challenge, so that a person can eventually grow beyond it. |

### The pause before responding

| Tradition | Its name | How its version differs |
|---|---|---|
| Science education (Mary Budd Rowe) | *wait time* | The pause is measured in seconds after a teacher's question, and Rowe reported better student responses when teachers waited three seconds or more. |
| The Quakers | *unprogrammed worship* | The whole meeting waits in silence for inward guidance, and several minutes often pass between speakers. |
| The Circle Way | *practice the pause* | A guardian's bell stops all action for about three breaths, and any member may request it. |
| Bohm dialogue (David Bohm) | *suspension* | The pause happens inside thinking: a reaction is kept in view and neither acted on nor suppressed, while the conversation may continue. |
| Psychoanalysis (Sigmund Freud) | *evenly suspended attention* | The analyst suspends selection and judgment during listening rather than pausing before speaking. |
| Somatic Experiencing (Peter Levine) | *titration* | The pause is in the body: the work stops after a small amount so the nervous system can settle before going on. |

### Mirroring and reflecting

| Tradition | Its name | How its version differs |
|---|---|---|
| Person-centered therapy (Carl Rogers, 1986) | *testing understandings, or checking perceptions* | Each response is meant as a check on the listener's understanding and carries an implied question, "Is this accurate for you?" |
| Motivational interviewing | *reflective listening* | Reflections are part of a method that pays particular attention to the language of change, so they are used with a direction. |
| Imago Dialogue (Harville Hendrix and Helen LaKelly Hunt) | *mirroring* | The receiver repeats the sender near word for word inside a fixed turn-taking structure between partners, before any reply. |
| Psychodrama (J. L. Moreno) | *the mirror technique* | It is not verbal repetition: another person enacts the protagonist so that the protagonist can watch themselves from outside. |

## 10. What the vault has not met, by scale

The field holds 238 techniques the vault has not met. The researchers each named the ones a drafting session would most likely reach for, and those are gathered here, a handful at each scale, with the reason each bears on the book. Several are described more fully in section 3. The entries file holds the rest.

**One person, parts, and texts.**
- **Inner Relationship Focusing** (Ann Weiser Cornell and Barbara McGavin, 1990s) develops Gendlin's Focusing, which the vault has read at its source, by treating each inner experience as a part and meeting it from "Self-in-Presence," a calm, friendly, and curious listening. It bears on the vault's material on the felt shift and on the fork in section 3, since it holds that the listening self is already present.
- **Voice Dialogue** (Hal and Sidra Stone, early 1970s) speaks directly with one of a person's selves and then helps the person notice the difference between being identified with that self and being aware of it, a capacity it calls the "Aware Ego." It is company on the other side of the same fork, because the Aware Ego is developed through the work rather than found.
- **Naikan** (Yoshimoto Ishin, 1940s) asks three questions of each relationship, what one received, what one returned, and what trouble one caused, over about a week of reflection alone, interrupted by brief visits from a guide who hears what was recalled. It is a solitary form whose second person is reduced to a listener who visits, which bears on the solitary-form tension in section 13.
- **Expressive writing** (James Pennebaker, 1986) asks a person to write for fifteen to twenty minutes a day, on three or four days, about their deepest thoughts and feelings concerning a stressful or traumatic experience. It is a writing technique with a research program behind it, and the correspondence the author chose asks its reader to write an answer between letters (`ops/decisions.md`, 2026-08-08).
- **The Shikoku pilgrimage**, walked alone, carries the words *dōgyō ninin*, "two travelling together," on the pilgrim's hat, because the pilgrim is held to be accompanied by Kōbō Daishi. It is the field's plainest case of solitude held as company.
- **The unsent letter**, a common technique in grief counseling, is written to someone dead, absent, or unreachable with no intention of sending it (section 4).

**Two people.**
- **Nancy Kline's Thinking Session** (1990s) makes uninterrupted attention the listener's whole contribution, and when the thinker reaches a limiting assumption, the listener helps the thinker build an "Incisive Question" from the thinker's own words. It is the field's clearest case of a helper at A.
- **Motivational interviewing** (William Miller and Stephen Rollnick, 1983–1991) calls the helper's urge to correct the person or supply the solution the "righting reflex" and trains the helper to hold it back. It names, from the helper's side, the restraint the vault holds must be trained (`notes/the helper's restraint is the active ingredient rather than the absence of one and it is trained.md`).
- **John Heron's six categories of intervention** (1970s) divide a helper's interventions into three that lead and three that draw on the person's own capacities. If the entry holds, it is a published precedent for this survey's coordinate of who decides; it rests on general knowledge and is unverified.
- **The sitter in Holotropic Breathwork** (Stanislav and Christina Grof) stays present and available while the breather works, without interfering, interrupting, or trying to guide, and trained facilitators step in only when needed.
- **Authentic Movement** (Mary Starks Whitehouse, developed by Janet Adler and Joan Chodorow) pairs a mover with a witness who watches without judging or interpreting, and it aims to develop in the mover an "inner witness." That route, from being witnessed to witnessing oneself, is Book II's concern.

**Three people and small groups.**
- **The definitional ceremony** (Michael White, drawing on Barbara Myerhoff) has outsider witnesses retell what caught their attention in a person's story, the images it evoked, why it resonated with their own lives, and how they were moved, and it asks them not to applaud, judge, give opinions, or hold up their own lives as moral examples.
- **Transformative mediation** (Robert Bush and Joseph Folger, 1994) leaves decisions about both process and outcome to the parties and watches for the moments when a party becomes clearer and more able to decide, which it calls "empowerment," or more able to hear the other, which it calls "recognition."
- **The clearness committee** (the Quakers, described for wider use by Parker Palmer) helps a person find the answer within by listening and asking questions, and its members give no advice.
- **Interpersonal Process Recall** (Norman Kagan, 1960s) has a person stop the replay of their own conversation whenever a feeling or thought comes back, while an inquirer asks open questions and offers no judgment, so the person decides what mattered.
- **The dream appreciation group** (Montague Ullman) lets the dreamer decide at each stage whether to go on, while the members offer meanings only as their own projections.

**Large groups and organizations.**
- **Open Space Technology** (Harrison Owen, 1985) has one law, the "Law of Two Feet," under which each person stays in a session or leaves it on their own judgment; section 13 sets it beside the field's documented harms.
- **Process consultation and humble inquiry** (Edgar Schein, 1969 and 2013) leave ownership of the problem and its solution with the client and ask questions to which the consultant does not already know the answer. They work at the organizational scale, where the author works as a manager.
- **Worldwork** (Arnold Mindell) invites people to speak from the roles in a conflict and to switch roles, and it attends to rank and to the "ghost role," a voice that is felt but that no one present speaks for.
- **The group relations conference** (the Tavistock Institute, from 1957) makes a group's own behavior around authority and leadership the subject of study, with staff consultants who take no leadership role in the usual sense, so the facilitator's authority is itself the material.
- **Double-loop learning** (Chris Argyris and Donald Schön, 1974) asks, when an action fails, not only what to do differently but which goals and assumptions governed the choice.

**Communities, customs, and the living world.**
- **Sitting shiva** holds visitors to silence until the mourner speaks first (Moed Katan 28b).
- **The Industrial Areas Foundation's iron rule**, that one should never do for others what they can do for themselves, governs a practice of one-to-one meetings that builds people's power to act together.
- **The peacemaking circle** (Kay Pranis, Barry Stuart, and Mark Wedge, drawing on First Nations practice in the Yukon) passes a talking piece so that only its holder speaks, and its keeper offers guiding questions without directing the group.
- **The sit spot** (Jon Young) brings a person back to one chosen place in every weather and season, and its mentor asks questions that begin with what the learner can answer and move toward the edge of what they know, instead of giving answers.
- **Dadirri**, as Miriam-Rose Ungunmerr-Baumann presents the practice of the Ngan'gikurunggurr and Ngen'giwumirri peoples, is silent and unhurried listening, and in community it means waiting for the right time, the right people, and proper preparation.

## 11. What the map offers the two forms the author is weighing

The author has not decided whether the book is "narrative/persuasive based or sort of 'take what works for you'." The field holds both kinds of work, and this section says what each form would take from the map. It is a session's reading at the level "proposed," and it does not choose between them.

**The field holds both forms, and so does the corpus.** Some facilitative work leads a person along one path in a set order:
- a rite of passage separates a person from their former status, holds them for a time between statuses, and incorporates them into a new one (Arnold van Gennep, 1909, with Victor Turner's account of the middle phase);
- a Future Search runs five half-day sessions over three days, from time lines of the past through present trends and a desired future to the common ground everyone shares and a plan of action;
- Appreciative Inquiry moves an organization through four phases, from interviews about the organization at its best to imagining, designing, and acting on a future;
- the Twelve Steps take a member through a fixed sequence with a sponsor who has walked it.

Other work hands a person or a facilitator a repertoire and leaves the choosing to them:
- Liberating Structures publishes thirty-three short meeting formats (forty-three in the 2026 fieldbook), each fixing the invitation, the arrangement of space, how participation is spread, how groups form, and the steps and timing, and a facilitator combines them as a meeting needs;
- Viola Spolin's theater games give a leader a set of games, each with a single point of concentration;
- Nancy Kline's Thinking Environment names ten components, among them attention, ease, and incisive questions, which any meeting can adopt.

The corpus already holds both. Its arc is a path in a set order, and its treatment's note on the chapter of the twelve forms says "The reader owns a repertoire," with the build note that "the book keeps the vocabulary, the companion keeps the drills" (`arc-gloss` p. 15).

**The choice between the two forms is itself a position on who decides.** A narrative book decides the order in which the reader meets things, and a repertoire hands the order to the reader. Either form can still leave the content to the reader, since a path can ask only questions at each step and a repertoire can dictate at every step. So the two forms differ in who decides the sequence, and they need not differ in who decides the meaning. The book's rule that "nothing transforms from the audience" (`atlas` §IX.3, recorded at `notes/nothing transforms from the audience.md`) constrains the content in both forms and leaves the sequence open.

**A narrative book would take a route through the scales from the map.** Three routes have real instances in the field:
- inward to outward, from one person alone to a pair and then a group, as in the structure 1-2-4-All, which moves from a minute alone to pairs, to fours, and to the whole group;
- outward to inward, from being witnessed to witnessing oneself, as in Authentic Movement's aim of an inner witness;
- out and back, from the community into a time apart and back into the community in a new standing, as in a rite of passage or a pilgrimage.

The book's own arc ends in descent and integration, so it has the out-and-back shape at the scale of one reader. The arc does not settle which scales the reader passes through on the way, and the executed draft already takes the reader outward at one point, when it tells a reader in crisis to use Calling the Room "with the people who can be called" (`abridged` p. 45).

**A repertoire would sort its practices by the coordinates a reader chooses by.** Most readers would choose by scale, asking whether they are alone, with one other person, or with a group, and by subject, asking what the practice is about. The coordinate of who decides would tell each reader, before they begin, how much a practice will decide for them.

**A third form sits between the two, and the letter already carries it.** The Circle Way is one instance: a small fixed core, made of a centre, a check-in, stated agreements, a guardian who can call a pause, and a check-out, inside which a group supplies all of its own content, so that a path and a repertoire are combined in one handbook any group can host. The letter is another instance. Seneca's moral letters were written to one friend for many readers, and the early letters end with a maxim, often from Epicurus, for the reader to meditate on, so each carries an argument and a practice. Rilke, asked to judge a young poet's work, declined, told him that nobody could advise or help him, and counselled him to go into himself and to live his questions (the vault holds Rilke at `notes/rainer maria rilke.md`), so a letter can also leave all of the content to its reader. The correspondence the author chose on 2026-08-08 can therefore carry either form, since one letter can tell a story and the next can offer a practice to try.

Three questions follow from the map for the author, and none of them needs an answer now:
- At which scale does the book address its reader: alone, with one friend, or with a group?
- Does the book hand its reader moves to use with other people, as Calling the Room does, or only forms to use alone?
- Is the order of the book the book's decision or the reader's?

The vault records the same risk in Wilber's case history: an unfalsifiability charge, and a stage scheme that became a status ladder among his readers (`notes/the nearest prior attempts are case histories rather than interlocutors.md`).

Wilber's first book is the nearest precedent for this survey. *The Spectrum of Consciousness*, completed in 1973 and published by Quest Books in 1977, "sought to integrate knowledge from disparate fields" (Wikipedia, "Ken Wilber"), and the vault lists it among his works without a reading (`notes/ken wilber.md`). Whether it sorted the therapies by level, as this survey sorts techniques by scale, and whether it ranked them, is a reading question in section 14. Later the same evening, a reply in the conversation gave Wilber's later quadrants a bounded role: they become "a **coordinate system**, rather than an ontology," answering "*Where are we looking?*" rather than "*What is happening?*" (the reframe conversation, lines 17834–17847; recorded at `notes/wilber's four quadrants are a coordinate system, not an architecture.md`).

The same holds here. The seven coordinates are ways to find a technique and to see what the vault has not met. They do not claim that facilitation is built from these parts, they do not rank one technique above another, and they do not arrange the models as levels. The survey also runs under the four safeguards of the vault's recipe for surveys (`ops/ecologies/ecologies.md`):
- **Gathering is not agreement.** The researchers gathered techniques for being facilitative, so the facilitative features they share were produced by the gathering and are not a finding. Where section 8 shows traditions naming a similar thing, the similarity is company and not proof, and the differences are kept.
- **Each technique is asked what kind of structure it is:** a single move, a method, a sequence of phases, or a model of what a person is made of (`ops/methodology/the four kinds of structure.md`). The entries record the answer: 246 of the 299 are methods, 28 are sequences of phases, 20 are single moves, and 5 are models of the person.
- **The writing test governs what enters the notes.** Nothing here is a note. A technique earns a note only when a drafting session would reach for it, and it enters through the pipeline.
- **The stance holds.** The survey allocates reading and never ranks evidence, and where a technique refuses something, the refusal is kept.

## 13. Tensions the map brings into view

The survey resolves none of these. Each is a disagreement the sorting brings into view, stated as a session's reading at the level "proposed." None has been entered as a note; any the author takes up would enter the graph through the pipeline, and a doctrinal one would be listed at `notes/tensions.md`.

1. **The book's forms sit at E, and the author's craft sits at B.** What a facilitator at B does that a text cannot is to see the person now and adjust: tracking, the contact statement, titration, and asking permission before offering all depend on it. The vault's own finding points the same way, since every guard a solitary reader can run declines to evaluate and every discrimination routes to another person (`notes/a solitary reader can run every guard that declines to evaluate and none that discriminates.md`). The field shows two ways a structure carries a facilitator's work without one: a custom whose rule everyone present keeps, and a move handed to the reader to use with others, which the corpus already made of Calling the Room (section 6).

2. **The book remembers, and much of the field imagines forward.** The field holds 26 techniques that work on the future and the vault holds none. Two questions from the field stand between the two directions: the exception question asks when the problem was absent or smaller, and the Discovery phase of Appreciative Inquiry asks about times when a person or an organization was at its best. Both recover a capacity from the person's own past in order to build a future. They are company for the book's "You already know everything in this book" (`abridged` p. 1), with a difference kept: they recover what a person has done, and the book's remembering recovers what never left.

3. **Conflict is nearly absent from the vault, and the book's master question is hardest to meet there.** In a conflict, each party must remain the author of what they become while being changed by the other. Transformative mediation names two aims at that scale, a party becoming able to decide for themselves and a party becoming able to hear the other (section 3), and section 14 asks whether its second aim is the book's recognition or only shares the word.

4. **The field offers a third position on the solitary form.** The live tension at `notes/the solitary form stands against seven witnesses who hold a second person constitutive.md` sets seven traditions that say the work cannot be done alone with a text against the book's letter read alone, and the handling the note proposes, a candidate drawn from the author's reflection that awaits his decision, holds that "recognition is already a with, so solitude was never alone." The field holds forms of accompanied solitude: the Shikoku pilgrim walking alone with *dōgyō ninin* on the hat, the Naikan guide who visits briefly to hear what was recalled, and the sit spot, where the companion is a place. These are company for the proposed handling, with a difference kept: in each of them a tradition names the companion or supplies a person who visits, and the proposed handling names no companion other than recognition itself.

5. **Each of the four documented harms in the entries takes the content or the exit out of the person's hands.** Synanon's Game grew into a 72-hour version that its founder acknowledged as brainwashing, and it was used to pressure the members of a residential community. The large-group awareness trainings led audiences of hundreds through consecutive long days of confrontation toward a shift the trainer designed, with reports of psychiatric casualties. Wilderness therapy has involved the involuntary transport of young people into remote programs. And in Family Constellations the facilitator decides which arrangement of the family counts as resolved. Open Space Technology's Law of Two Feet is the explicit opposite, since it leaves the exit with each person. The book's master question names the condition these cases violate, "the conditions under which a person remains the author of what they become while being genuinely changed" (`notes/the master question is the conditions under which a person remains the author of what they become while being genuinely changed.md`), and the vault already holds one of the four, since its note on Werner Erhard records the est training's published safety record (`notes/werner erhard.md`).

## 15. Limits, and where to browse the entries

**The verification is uneven.** Of the 299 techniques, 233 are verified at their sources, 62 in part, and 4 not at all: Heron's six categories, one-on-one Circling, the dyad of the Enlightenment Intensive, and the wilderness vision fast of the School of Lost Borders. Of the 61 moves, 41 are verified and 20 in part; of the 37 stances, 18 are verified, 16 in part, and 3 not at all (the window of tolerance, use of self, and Heron's modes of facilitation); of the 24 models, 22 are verified and 2 in part; and of the 12 crosswalk clusters, 10 are verified and 2 in part. Where this file uses an unverified entry, it says so.

**The sources are mostly secondary.** The researchers worked from web sources: practitioners' own sites, institutes, and encyclopedias, with a few primary texts such as the Talmud at Sefaria and the *Spiritual Exercises* at the Christian Classics Ethereal Library. Of the 299 techniques, 186 cite an encyclopedia article among their sources and 110 cite nothing else. The web searches the researchers shared ran out near the end of the gathering, so the last checks relied on pages already fetched.

**The coordinates are judgments.** Each technique's scale, subjects, model, structure, and position on who decides were assigned by the one researcher who gathered it, and no second researcher checked them. The vault's holdings were placed by a single researcher in the same way.

The vault's concentration at one person alone was not produced this way, because one researcher placed all of the vault's holdings.
