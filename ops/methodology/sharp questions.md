# sharp questions

This is a standing directive, issued 2026-08-06 under the author's instruction to take the acuity of the recent work and "endow the rest of the base with" it, moving unilaterally. The acuity has a definable shape, and this file defines it so that every future queue entry, reading, and evaluation can be held to it rather than to the taste of whichever session wrote it.

The specimen that defines the standard is the Gendlin round of 2026-08-05. The question went in as "is introspection reliable," which no reading can settle, and came out as "does an attack on introspective *description* reach a standard that only asks a person to *notice change against no-change*" — which one paper can now answer, in either direction, with consequences stated in advance for each.

## The five properties

A question is sharp when it has all five. Each is stated with a real instance from the record, so the property is checkable against something rather than felt.

1. **It can fail.** The reading can return an answer that ends the question rather than coloring it. The instance: Nisbett and Wilson's own carve-out for present-state reports may put the narrowed verification standard outside their paper's range entirely — in which case the vault learns the standard survives its most famous objection, which is worth exactly as much as learning that it does not.

2. **Its stake is named.** The entry says what in the vault changes on each answer, before the reading starts. The instance: whether Minuchin frames enmeshment as an absent boundary or a present pattern decides whether he joins the privation convergence or complicates it — one sentence, two named outcomes, both prepared for.

3. **Its address is page-level.** A reading with a book-level address gets deferred forever; a reading with a page-level address gets done. The instances: Maslow's plateau correction is in the preface added to the 1970 edition; Aristotle's privation is *Physics* I.7–9; Rogers's conditions are a six-page 1957 paper that states them as a numbered list.

4. **It states what it inherits.** Findings upstream change questions downstream, and an entry that does not carry its inheritance sends the reader in with last month's question. The instance is the specimen above: every reading queued against the verification standard now inherits the narrowed certificate from the Gendlin reading, and the entries say so.

5. **Its current evidence is graded.** The entry says whether what the vault currently holds came from a primary text, a secondary account, or a collaborator's characterization — because those are different situations and a reader should know which one the reading is testing. The instance: every chair written from the mesh grid says in its own body that the grid's characterization stands in for a reading not yet done, and the grid itself is cosigned at 65–75%.

## The shelf rule

Not every entry must be sharp, because the vault deliberately keeps a long field of candidates — "the list is deliberately long — its sixty-odd entries are the field, not the task" (`ops/queue/candidates.md`). The rule is labeling, not pruning: **an entry that is a name plus a topic gloss is a shelf entry, and a shelf entry is not work.** Work is what has been sharpened into the five properties. The two kinds are never mixed in one list without the difference being visible, and the sharpened form of the vault's reading backlog lives at `ops/reading queue.md`.

## What this directive does not license

Sharpening a question is not answering it, and the stake-naming in property 2 is not a prediction. A sharp question prepared for two outcomes is still open; the reading decides. And the directive does not apply backward as a demand that every old entry be rewritten at once — the queue's shelf tier stays a shelf until an entry is promoted to work, and the promotion is when the sharpening happens.
